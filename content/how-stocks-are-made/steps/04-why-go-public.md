### What happens

At some point the company may want a stock exchange listing: to **raise more money**, to let **early investors sell**, to pay for acquisitions with its own shares, and for **visibility**. The price is high: fees, ongoing **disclosure**, daily scrutiny of the share price, and new shareholders who vote.

The classic route is an **initial public offering (IPO)**. Other routes include a **direct listing** (existing shares are admitted to trading), a **SPAC** merger, a **reverse merger**, a **spin-off** from a listed parent, and the **privatisation** of a state-owned company.

### Who is involved

The board and owners decide; investment banks, lawyers and the stock exchange advise on the route and the market segment.

### Example with numbers

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company valued at **€400 million** wants **€100 million** of new money. Selling new shares worth €100m in an IPO would give the new shareholders 100 ÷ (400 + 100) = **20%** of the company, the same logic as a private round, only now the buyers are the public.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> The Vienna Stock Exchange has different segments: the **prime market** and **standard market** for shares admitted to the Official Market, and the **direct market plus** and **direct market** on the Vienna MTF, aimed at smaller and younger companies.

> [!NOTE] In the United States
> The SEC warns that in a SPAC, investors rely heavily on the sponsors, whose financial interests can differ from those of ordinary shareholders. See the SPAC case study below.
