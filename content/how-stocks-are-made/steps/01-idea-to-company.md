### What happens

Someone has a business idea. As a **sole trader**, they can start at once, but they are personally liable for every debt and there are no shares to sell. To bring in co-owners, they found a **corporation**: a separate legal person that owns its assets, signs contracts and is liable for its own debts. Its owners pay in **share capital** and receive ownership in return.

### Who is involved

The founders, a notary or lawyer who drafts the founding documents, the bank that holds the paid-in capital, and the commercial register.

### Example with numbers

Two founders want to start an AG in Austria. The law requires at least **€70,000** of share capital. They pay in **€100,000** together, so the company has more than the minimum from day one.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> A **GmbH** needs at least **€10,000** of share capital, an **AG** at least **€70,000**. Both come into existence when they are entered in the **Firmenbuch** (commercial register). Only an AG can list its shares on the Vienna Stock Exchange, which is why turning into an AG is one of the first steps before an IPO.

> [!NOTE] In the United States
> The lighter form is usually the **LLC**; companies that list on an exchange are corporations ("Inc."). The principle is the same: only a company whose capital is divided into shares can sell shares to the public.
