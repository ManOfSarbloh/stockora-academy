### What happens

The company divides its share capital into **shares** and hands them to the founders in proportion to what they paid in. The founding documents may allow more shares to be created later (**authorised shares**) than have been handed out so far (**issued shares**). The founders usually sign a **shareholders' agreement** with rules on selling shares, leaving the company and big decisions.

From now on, the company keeps a **cap table**: a list of everyone who holds its shares and related securities, and how many.

### Who is involved

The founders, the company's management, and lawyers who draft the shareholders' agreement.

### Example with numbers

> [!EXAMPLE]
> Anna and Ben found an AG with **1,000,000 shares**. Anna paid in more and receives **600,000** shares (60%); Ben receives **400,000** (40%). The number of shares is a choice; what matters is each owner's fraction.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> An Austrian AG can have **par value shares** (at least €1 each) or **no-par shares**, but not both. AGs that are not listed must issue **registered shares** and keep a share register.

> [!NOTE] In the United States
> The SEC's small-business glossary describes the cap table as a record of who owns a company's shares and related securities, and **authorised shares** as the maximum number a company may issue under its founding documents.
