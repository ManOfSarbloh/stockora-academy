### What happens

The founders' own money rarely lasts. The company raises money in **rounds**: friends and family, **angel investors**, a **seed round**, then **Series A, B, C**, usually led by **venture capital funds**. In each round, new investors buy newly issued shares at a price based on an agreed company value. Investors in private rounds often get **preferred shares** with extra rights, such as a **liquidation preference**. Employees may receive **stock options** that **vest** over time.

### Who is involved

Founders, angel investors, venture capital funds, sometimes crowdfunding platforms, and lawyers on both sides.

### Example with numbers

> [!EXAMPLE]
> An investor pays **€1 million** at a **pre-money valuation of €4 million**.
>
> - Post-money valuation: €4m + €1m = **€5 million**
> - Investor's share: €1m ÷ €5m = **20%**
> - Price per share: €4m ÷ 1,000,000 shares = **€4**, so the investor receives **250,000** new shares
>
> Anna now owns 600,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = **48%**. Her percentage fell, but her stake is still worth 48% × €5m = €2.4m, the same as before.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> Crowdfunding platforms in the EU can be authorised under a common EU regulation; in Austria the FMA supervises the platforms, but not the companies that receive the money. The FMA warns that such investments are often unsecured.

> [!WARNING]
> Early-stage investments carry a high risk of total loss: young companies can fail completely, and their shares usually cannot be sold easily.
