Imagine a bakery that is worth a lot of money. The owners want to open ten more shops, but they do not have enough cash. One solution: split the ownership of the bakery into many equal pieces and sell some of those pieces to other people. Each piece is a **share**. Whoever buys one becomes a part-owner of the bakery, a **shareholder**.

That is the whole idea behind a stock: **a stock is a share of ownership in a company.**

## Owning a slice of a company

When a company issues shares, its ownership is divided into equal units. If you own some of those units, you own the same fraction of the company.

![Pie chart of a company with 1,000 shares: a founder owns 400 (40%), a pension fund 300 (30%), many small investors 275 (27.5%) and you 25 (2.5%)](./ownership-pie.svg)

A company usually has many owners at the same time. Each owns exactly the fraction that their shares make up of all shares.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company is divided into **1,000 shares**. You buy **25** of them.
>
> Your ownership: 25 ÷ 1,000 = 0.025 = **2.5%** of the company.
>
> If each share costs €40, your 25 shares are worth 25 × €40 = **€1,000**, and the whole company is valued at 1,000 × €40 = **€40,000**. That total value is called the market capitalisation.

Real companies usually have millions or even billions of shares, so a normal investor owns a tiny fraction. The maths is exactly the same.

## Why companies sell shares

Companies sell shares to raise money without borrowing it. They might use the money to grow, build new facilities, develop new products, enter new markets or pay off debt. In return, the new shareholders get a piece of the company's future, good or bad. Unlike a loan, the company does not have to pay this money back.

## What you get as a shareholder

Owning shares can give you several things:

- **A share in the company's success.** If the company does well and more people want to own it, the share price can rise. Selling for more than you paid is a capital gain.
- **Dividends.** Some companies pay part of their profit to shareholders as a dividend. This is never guaranteed: the company decides whether to pay, and how much.
- **A vote.** Ordinary (common) shareholders can usually vote at the company's general meeting, for example on electing board members. One share normally means one vote, so a small holding means a small voice.
- **Information.** Listed companies must publish regular reports, so owners can see how the business is doing.

## What you do not get

Owning a share does **not** mean you can walk into the company and take a chair home. The company owns its buildings, machines and cash; you own a piece of the company. You also do not get any promise of a return: stock prices go down as well as up.

If a company goes bankrupt and its assets are sold, ordinary shareholders are **last in line**. Lenders and bondholders are paid first, then holders of preferred shares, and ordinary shareholders only get whatever is left, which may be nothing.

> [!WARNING]
> You can lose all the money you put into a stock. That is why spreading money across many companies (diversification) matters, a topic covered later in the learning path.

## Stock, share, equity, security: same thing?

You will see several words used for nearly the same idea:

- **Share** usually means one unit of ownership: "I own 25 shares."
- **Stock** is often used more generally: "I invest in stocks", "I bought some stock in a car maker". In everyday English, stock and share are mostly interchangeable.
- **Equity** (or "equities") is the more formal word for ownership in companies. Investment professionals say "equities" where others say "stocks".
- **Security** is the umbrella term for tradable investments such as stocks and bonds. Every stock is a security, but not every security is a stock.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> In German, a share is an **Aktie** and a shareholder an **Aktionär** or **Aktionärin**. In Austria, a company whose share capital (Grundkapital) is divided into shares is an **Aktiengesellschaft (AG)**. Its shareholders together form the general meeting (Hauptversammlung), and they are not personally liable for the company's debts. Their risk is limited to the money they put in.

> [!MISTAKE] "A €5 share is cheaper than a €500 share"
> The price of one share tells you almost nothing on its own. A company with 1 million shares at €500 has the same total value as a company with 100 million shares at €5: €500 million. To compare companies, look at the total value (market capitalisation) and what the business earns, not the price of a single share.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A stock (share) is a small unit of ownership in a company.
> - Your share of the company = your shares ÷ all shares.
> - Shareholders can profit from rising prices and dividends, and usually get a vote, but nothing is guaranteed.
> - If a company fails, ordinary shareholders are paid last and can lose everything they invested.
> - The price of one share alone does not tell you whether a company is "cheap" or "expensive".
