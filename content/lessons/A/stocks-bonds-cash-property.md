Most savings and investments fall into a few big groups, called **asset classes**. Each behaves differently, and most people own a mix of them, often without thinking about it. Here are the four you meet most often.

## The four at a glance

| | What you own | Income | Typical ups and downs | How easy to sell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stocks** | Part of a company | Dividends (not guaranteed) | Large | Usually easy (listed shares) |
| **Bonds** | A loan to a company or government | Interest | Smaller than stocks, usually | Usually easy, price can vary |
| **Cash** | Money in an account or very short-term deposits | Interest (often low) | Very small | Very easy |
| **Property** | A building or land | Rent | Prices change more slowly, but can fall | Slow and expensive to sell |

## Stocks

Ownership in companies. Over long periods, stocks have historically offered the highest returns of the three main financial asset classes (stocks, bonds, cash), with the biggest swings. You can lose a large part, or in a single company even all, of what you invest.

## Bonds

Loans to governments or companies that pay interest and repay the amount at the end. Generally less volatile than stocks with more modest returns. Risk depends heavily on the borrower: a stable government is a very different borrower from a struggling company.

## Cash

Savings accounts, deposits and similar. Cash is the safest of the three main asset classes in the sense that its value hardly ever falls, and many bank deposits are protected up to a limit by deposit guarantee schemes (covered in Category F). Its main enemy is **inflation**: if prices rise faster than your interest, your money buys less every year.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You keep **€10,000** in a savings account paying **1%** interest. Over the year, prices in the shops rise by **3%**.
>
> After one year you have €10,100. But the things that cost €10,000 a year ago now cost €10,300.
>
> In real terms (what your money can buy), you are about **2% poorer**, even though your account balance went up.

## Property

Owning a flat, a house or land. Property can provide rent and may grow in value, but it is very different from the other three:

- It usually needs a **large amount of money** (often borrowed through a mortgage).
- It is **slow and costly to buy and sell**: agents, notaries, taxes and fees.
- One property is a **concentrated** bet on one location and one building.

Investors who want property exposure without buying a building can use listed real estate companies, REITs or property funds (Categories C and H).

> [!MISTAKE] "Cash is risk-free"
> Cash protects you from price swings, not from inflation. Over long periods, money that earns less than inflation slowly loses purchasing power. Cash is the right place for an emergency fund and money you need soon, but holding everything in cash for decades has a cost too.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Stocks: ownership, highest historical returns and biggest swings.
> - Bonds: lending, usually steadier, with risk depending on the borrower.
> - Cash: safest against price swings, but exposed to inflation.
> - Property: real assets with rent, but large, slow and concentrated.
> - Most people hold a mix; the right mix depends on goals and time horizon (Category M).
