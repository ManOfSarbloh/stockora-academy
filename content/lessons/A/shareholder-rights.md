When you own shares, you are a part-owner of the company. That comes with rights, but they work differently from owning, say, a bicycle. You cannot use the company's things or tell its staff what to do. Instead, your rights work through a few clear channels.

## 1. The right to vote

Ordinary (common) shareholders can vote at the company's **general meeting**, usually held once a year. What exactly shareholders decide depends on the country's company law, but typical items include electing members of the board, choosing the auditor and approving big changes such as mergers. In Austria, for example, the ordinary general meeting also decides how the company's distributable profit is used, which includes how much dividend is paid (§ 104 Aktiengesetz).

Votes are usually counted **per share**, not per person. If you own 100 of a company's 10 million shares, you have 100 votes out of 10 million. Many shareholders vote in advance or authorise someone else to vote for them (proxy voting) instead of attending in person.

Some types of shares have **no voting rights**. Preferred shares, for example, usually do not vote, but get their dividends first. You will meet these in Category C (types of stocks).

> [!AUSTRIA]
> In an Austrian AG, the shareholders together form the **Hauptversammlung** (general meeting). An ordinary general meeting must take place within the first eight months of each financial year. At the meeting, shareholders have a legal right to ask for information on company matters that they need to judge an item on the agenda (§ 118 Aktiengesetz). The company may refuse only in limited cases, for example if the answer could cause it significant harm.

## 2. A share in the profits (dividends)

If a company decides to pay a **dividend**, every shareholder of that share class receives the same amount per share. Owning more shares means receiving more.

Note the word *if*: a dividend is never guaranteed. Many companies, especially young and fast-growing ones, pay no dividend at all and reinvest their profits instead.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company declares a dividend of **€1.50 per share**. You own **200 shares**.
>
> You receive 200 × €1.50 = **€300**, before taxes. (How dividends are taxed is covered in Category Q.)

## 3. The right to information

Listed companies must keep their owners and the public informed. In the EU, companies whose shares trade on a regulated market have to publish, among other things:

- an **annual financial report** (audited financial statements and a management report),
- a **half-yearly financial report**, and
- important price-relevant news **without delay** (so-called ad-hoc announcements), so that all investors learn about it at the same time.

These reports are public, so you can read them before you ever buy a share. Category I explains how.

## 4. The right to what is left (residual claim)

Shareholders own whatever remains after everyone else has been paid. In good times, that "remainder" (all future profits) is what makes shares valuable. In bad times, it is why shareholders are last in line: if a company goes bankrupt, lenders and preferred shareholders are paid first, and ordinary shareholders get whatever is left, which may be nothing.

## 5. Limited liability

There is also a protection: shareholders of a company such as an AG are **not personally liable** for the company's debts. If the company fails, creditors cannot come after your savings or your home. The most you can lose is the money you paid for your shares.

> [!MISTAKE] "As a shareholder I can tell the company what to do"
> A small shareholder has a small vote. Decisions are made by majority at the general meeting and, day to day, by the company's management and board. Your real power is to vote, to ask questions, and to sell your shares if you disagree with how the company is run.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Ordinary shares usually come with a vote at the general meeting, counted per share.
> - Dividends are paid equally per share, but only if the company decides to pay them.
> - Listed companies must publish regular reports and important news promptly.
> - Shareholders get what is left after everyone else, so they are last in a bankruptcy.
> - Limited liability means you cannot lose more than you invested.
