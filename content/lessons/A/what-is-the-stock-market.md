News reports say things like "the stock market fell today", as if it were one place. It is not. **"The stock market" is a collective name** for all the places and systems where shares are bought and sold, and for the people and firms that take part.

## Stock exchanges

The best-known part is the **stock exchange**: an organised, regulated marketplace where shares of listed companies are traded under fixed rules. Examples are the Wiener Börse (Vienna Stock Exchange), the Frankfurt exchange, the London Stock Exchange, or the New York Stock Exchange. There are many dozens of exchanges around the world (see World Markets).

Today, almost all trading is **electronic**. The famous trading floors with people shouting are mostly history; orders are matched by computers. What makes an exchange an exchange is not the building but the **rules**: who may trade, how orders are matched, how prices are published, and which companies may be listed.

## Other trading venues

Shares do not only trade on exchanges. In the European Union, the MiFID II rules define three kinds of **trading venue**:

- **Regulated markets:** the classic stock exchanges.
- **Multilateral trading facilities (MTFs):** systems, often run by investment firms or by exchanges themselves, that also bring many buyers and sellers together under fixed rules.
- **Organised trading facilities (OTFs):** a category for bonds, derivatives and similar instruments, not for shares.

In addition, some trades happen **"over the counter" (OTC)**: directly between two parties, for example between a bank and a client, instead of on a venue. In the US, "OTC securities" also refers to shares that are not listed on a national exchange; information about such companies can be much harder to find.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You live in Vienna and buy shares of an Austrian company through an online broker. Depending on your broker and your order, the trade might be executed on the Wiener Börse, on an exchange in another country where the share is also listed, on an MTF, or directly with a trading firm.
>
> In each case, you end up owning the same share. The venue affects things like the price you get, the fees and the trading hours.

## "The market" as a number

When people say "the market went up 1%", they usually mean a **stock index**: a number that tracks the prices of a selected group of shares, such as Austria's ATX or the US S&P 500. An index is a summary, not a market itself. Category H explains how indexes are built.

> [!MISTAKE] "If the market is up today, my shares are up too"
> An index is an average. On a day when "the market" rises, many individual shares fall. Your own shares can do very differently from the index.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - "The stock market" is a network, not one place.
> - Stock exchanges are regulated marketplaces with fixed rules; trading is almost entirely electronic.
> - In the EU, shares can also trade on MTFs, and some trades happen over the counter.
> - "The market" in the news usually means an index, which is an average of many shares.
