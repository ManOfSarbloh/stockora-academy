A share price is not decided by the company, the stock exchange or the government. It is simply the price at which a buyer and a seller **last agreed** to trade. The Vienna Stock Exchange describes it this way: prices are not fixed by anyone, they are the result of supply and demand at the moment the price is formed, and they reflect the individual opinions of thousands of market participants.

## A price is an agreement

Imagine a few people who want to trade shares of the same company:

| Sellers want at least | Buyers will pay at most |
|---|---|
| €42 | €40 |
| €41 | €39 |
| €41 | €38 |

Nobody trades yet: the cheapest seller wants €41, the most generous buyer offers €40. Now a new buyer arrives who wants the shares quickly and accepts €41. A trade happens at **€41**, and that becomes the new share price shown on screen.

The exchange's job is to collect these orders and match them fairly. (How exactly this works, with order books and auctions, is covered in Category D.)

## What moves the price

A price moves when the balance between buyers and sellers changes. That happens when people change their minds about what the company will be worth in the future. Typical reasons:

- **Company news:** better or worse profits than expected, a new product, a lost customer, a change of management.
- **Expectations about the future:** investors pay for what they think a company will earn in the coming years, not for what it earned last year.
- **The economy and interest rates:** when interest rates rise, safer investments such as bonds and savings pay more, so shares have to compete harder for investors' money (Category L).
- **Mood and emotion:** fear and excitement can push prices further than the facts justify (Category O).

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company's shares trade at **€50**. It then announces that its profit was much higher than analysts expected.
>
> More investors now want to own it, and fewer owners want to sell. Buyers have to offer more to find sellers: €51, €52, €54... until enough sellers are willing again. The price might settle at **€55**, a 10% rise, within minutes.
>
> Nothing about the company's factories changed in those minutes. Only expectations did.

## Price is not the same as value

The price tells you what the last buyer paid. It does not tell you what the company is "really" worth: that is a matter of opinion, and investors disagree all the time. Their disagreement is exactly why trades happen: the seller thinks €41 is a good price to sell, the buyer thinks it is a good price to buy.

> [!MISTAKE] "The price fell, so the company must be in trouble"
> Not necessarily. Prices can fall because the whole market falls, because interest rates rise, or because a big investor needed cash and sold. And a price can rise even when a company is struggling, if the news was simply less bad than expected. Always look at what actually happened.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A share price is the price at which a buyer and seller last agreed to trade.
> - Prices move when the balance of buyers and sellers changes.
> - Expectations about the future matter more than past results.
> - Interest rates, the economy and emotions affect prices too.
> - Price and value are not the same: the price is a fact, the value is an opinion.
