**Return** is what an investment earns you. **Risk** is the chance that things turn out differently from what you hoped, including the chance that you lose money. In investing, these two are tied together.

## Measuring return

Your return comes from two things:

- **Price change:** the difference between what you paid and what the investment is worth now (or what you sold it for).
- **Income:** dividends from shares, interest from bonds or savings.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You buy a share for **€80**. One year later it is worth **€86**, and during the year you received a **€2** dividend.
>
> Total gain: (€86 − €80) + €2 = **€8**
>
> Return: €8 ÷ €80 = 0.10 = **10%** for the year (before fees and taxes).

## What "risk" means

In everyday language, risk means danger. In investing it mainly means **uncertainty**: how much the result can swing, in both directions. An investment whose value jumps up and down a lot is called **volatile**.

The basic rule: **investments that offer higher possible returns come with higher risk.** Nobody would accept the ups and downs of shares if they could get the same expected return from a savings account with no swings.

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) summarises the history of the three main asset categories like this: stocks have historically had the greatest risk and the highest returns; bonds have generally been less volatile with more modest returns; cash is the safest but has offered the lowest returns. It also notes that large-company stocks as a group have lost money on average in about one out of every three years, sometimes dramatically.

> [!WARNING]
> Past returns do not guarantee future returns. "Historically" means "in the past, in the markets that were measured": it is not a promise.

## Two investments, same average

Here are two made-up investments over four years. Both average +5% per year:

| Year | Investment A | Investment B |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | +5% | +25% |
| 2 | +5% | −15% |
| 3 | +5% | +20% |
| 4 | +5% | −10% |

Investment B is far more volatile. If you had needed your money at the end of year 2 or year 4, you could have been unlucky. And because losses hurt more than equal gains help (a −15% followed by +15% does not get you back to the start), the *actual* growth of B over the four years is lower than A's, despite the same average.

## Time changes the picture

Risk feels very different depending on **when you need the money**:

- Money you need **soon** (for rent, a holiday next year, an emergency) should not be in volatile investments: a bad year could hit exactly when you need it.
- Money you will not need for **many years** can ride out bad years, which is why long-term investors can accept more volatility.

> [!MISTAKE] "Higher risk means higher return"
> Higher risk means a higher *possible* return, together with a higher possible *loss*. Taking more risk does not guarantee anything. Some risks, such as putting all your money into one company, increase the danger without increasing the expected return at all. That is what diversification (Category M) is about.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Return = price change + income (dividends, interest).
> - Risk in investing means uncertainty: how much results can swing, up and down.
> - Higher possible returns come with higher risk; there is no free lunch.
> - Volatility matters most when you might need the money soon.
> - Past returns are not a promise about the future.
