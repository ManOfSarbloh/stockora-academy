A company is a way for people to work together on a business and share its results. Before a company can sell anything, it usually has to spend money first: on equipment, a workshop or office, materials and staff. Later, if it wants to grow, it needs even more.

## Where the money can come from

Companies have four main sources of money:

1. **The founders' own money.** Most companies start with their founders' savings.
2. **Profits kept in the business.** A profitable company can reinvest its profits instead of paying them out.
3. **Borrowing.** A company can take a bank loan or sell bonds. A bond is a loan that investors give to the company in exchange for interest and the promise to repay it later.
4. **Selling ownership.** A company can sell new shares to investors. The investors pay money and become part-owners.

Companies typically raise money this way to expand, develop new products, enter new markets, build facilities or pay off debt.

## Borrowing or selling shares?

Both options bring in money, but they come with different costs.

| | Borrowing (debt) | Selling shares (equity) |
|---|---|---|
| Must be paid back? | Yes, by a set date | No |
| Ongoing cost | Interest, even in a bad year | No fixed cost; shareholders share future profits |
| Control | Lenders usually have no vote | New shareholders usually get votes |
| If the company fails | Lenders are paid before shareholders | Shareholders are paid last |

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A bakery needs **€300,000** to open two new shops.
>
> **Option 1: a loan at 6% interest per year.** The bakery pays €300,000 × 6% = **€18,000 interest every year**, whether the new shops do well or not, and must repay the €300,000 later.
>
> **Option 2: sell new shares.** The owners sell a **25%** stake to an investor for €300,000. There is no interest and nothing to repay, but from now on **a quarter of all future profits belongs to the investor**, and the investor gets a say in big decisions.
>
> If the new shops are a huge success, the loan was the cheaper choice. If they struggle, the share sale was safer for the bakery.

## When does a company get money from its shares?

Only when it **issues new shares**: when founders put money in, when investors buy into the company privately, or when it sells shares to the public, for example in an initial public offering (IPO). This is called the **primary market**.

After that, shares change hands between investors on the **secondary market**, for example on a stock exchange. The company is not part of those trades and receives nothing from them.

> [!MISTAKE] "When I buy a share on the stock exchange, my money goes to the company"
> Almost always, it does not. When you buy an existing share on an exchange, your money goes to **the investor who sold it to you**. The company only receives money when it issues new shares. A rising share price still matters to the company (for example, it makes raising new money later easier), but it does not put cash in the company's bank account.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Companies need money to start, to grow and to get through difficult times.
> - The main sources are the founders' money, kept profits, borrowing and selling shares.
> - Debt must be repaid with interest; selling shares means sharing future profits and control.
> - A company only receives money from shares when it issues new ones (primary market). Everyday trading on an exchange happens between investors (secondary market).
