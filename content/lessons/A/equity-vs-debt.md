There are two basic ways to put money into a company: you can **own** a piece of it, or you can **lend** it money. Finance has a word for each:

- **Equity** = ownership. You buy shares and become a shareholder.
- **Debt** = lending. You give the company a loan, for example by buying one of its bonds, and become a creditor.

## Lending: the bond

A bond is a debt security, a kind of tradable IOU. When you buy a bond, you lend money to the issuer (a company or a government) for a fixed time. In return, the issuer promises to:

- pay you **interest** at an agreed rate during the life of the bond, and
- **repay the amount you lent** (the face value) when the bond "matures" at the end.

Your upside is limited: even if the company becomes hugely successful, you only get the agreed interest and your money back. But you are paid **before** shareholders.

## Owning: the share

As a shareholder, nobody promises you anything. You get no fixed interest and no repayment date. Instead you own a slice of whatever the company is worth and whatever profits it makes, now and in the future. If the company does very well, there is no upper limit to what your shares can be worth. If it fails, you can lose everything.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> Two people each put **€10,000** into the same company.
>
> - **Lena lends** it the money through a bond paying **5% interest per year** for 5 years.
> - **Max buys shares** for €10,000.
>
> **If the company does very well** over the 5 years: Lena receives €500 interest per year (€2,500 in total) plus her €10,000 back. Max's shares might be worth much more than €10,000, perhaps double, perhaps more.
>
> **If the company has a few bad years but survives:** Lena still gets her interest and her money back. Max's shares may be worth less than he paid.
>
> **If the company goes bankrupt:** Lena is in the queue before Max and may get some or all of her money back. Max, as an ordinary shareholder, only gets what is left after all lenders are paid, which is often nothing.

## Who gets paid first?

If a company goes bankrupt and its assets are sold, the money is paid out in a fixed order. Bondholders and other lenders come first, then holders of preferred shares, and ordinary (common) shareholders come **last**.

That order explains the trade-off: lenders accept a limited return in exchange for more safety; owners accept more risk in exchange for unlimited upside.

## Neither is "safe"

Debt is generally less risky than equity *in the same company*, but bonds can lose money too: the borrower can fail to pay (default), and some bonds, called high-yield or "junk" bonds, offer high interest precisely because the risk is high.

> [!MISTAKE] "Bonds can't lose money"
> They can. If the borrower gets into trouble, interest and repayment may not be paid in full. And if you sell a bond before it matures, its market price can be lower than what you paid. Lower risk than shares is not the same as no risk.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Equity means ownership (shares); debt means lending (loans, bonds).
> - Lenders get agreed interest and their money back, but no share in extra success.
> - Owners get no promises, but share in all future success, with no upper limit.
> - In a bankruptcy, lenders are paid before shareholders; ordinary shareholders come last.
> - Bonds are generally less volatile than shares, but they are not risk-free.
