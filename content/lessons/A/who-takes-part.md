Every trade needs two sides: someone who buys and someone who sells. Who are all these people and organisations, and why do they trade?

## Retail investors

**Retail investors** (also called private or individual investors) use their own money to buy and sell investments for themselves. If you open an account with a broker and buy a few shares, you are a retail investor. Retail investors usually trade in small amounts and rely on brokers to reach the market.

## Institutional investors

**Institutional investors** are professional organisations that pool money from many people and invest it on their behalf, usually in very large amounts. Examples:

- **Investment funds and ETFs:** collect money from many savers and invest it according to a stated strategy (Category H).
- **Pension funds:** invest money that will later pay people's retirement pensions.
- **Insurance companies:** invest the premiums they collect, so they can pay future claims.
- **Banks:** trade for themselves and for their clients.
- **Hedge funds, endowments and foundations:** various other professional investors.

Institutions account for a large share of trading volume and own a large part of many listed companies. That also means many people own shares *indirectly*, through their pension or a fund, without ever buying a single share themselves.

## Market makers and other trading firms

A **market maker** is a firm that stands ready to buy or sell a particular share at publicly quoted prices. By always offering both a buying price and a selling price, market makers make it easier for everyone else to trade at any moment, and they earn the small difference between the two prices. Some exchanges contract market makers specifically to keep trading going in less-traded shares.

## Brokers

A **broker** is the firm you go through to buy and sell. Retail investors usually cannot trade directly on an exchange; the broker passes your order on and holds the shares for you. Category F covers brokers, accounts and orders in detail.

## Companies themselves

Companies take part too: they **issue** new shares to raise money, and some **buy back** their own shares (Category V).

## Governments

Governments appear in several roles. They can be **shareholders**: many states own stakes in companies. They also make the **rules**, and independent **regulators** supervise the markets, such as the FMA in Austria (Category R).

> [!AUSTRIA]
> The Republic of Austria holds stakes in several large Austrian companies, managed by the state holding company **ÖBAG** (Österreichische Beteiligungs AG). Financial markets in Austria are supervised by the **FMA** (Finanzmarktaufsicht).

## Why it matters to you

As a retail investor, you are trading in the same market as professionals with large teams, fast computers and expensive data. That is not a reason to give up, but it is a reason to be humble about trying to beat them at short-term trading (Category N), and to focus on what you can control: costs, diversification and patience.

> [!MISTAKE] "Professional investors always know better"
> Institutions have more resources, but they also make mistakes, follow trends and have their own pressures (for example, to beat a benchmark every quarter). Their size can even be a disadvantage: a huge fund cannot easily buy or sell a small company's shares without moving the price.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Retail investors trade their own money, usually in small amounts, through brokers.
> - Institutional investors (funds, pension funds, insurers, banks) invest large pools of other people's money.
> - Market makers stand ready to buy and sell, which keeps trading possible at any time.
> - Companies and governments take part too, and regulators supervise the market.
> - Many people own shares indirectly through pensions and funds.
