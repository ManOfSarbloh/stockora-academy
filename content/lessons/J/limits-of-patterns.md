After the last two lessons you may feel that charts are full of signals. This lesson is the counterweight: what we actually know about whether patterns help, and why it is easy to fool yourself.

## Why patterns look better than they are

- **Hindsight bias:** on a finished chart, the hammer at the bottom and the shooting star at the top jump out at you. In real time, at the right edge of the chart, you do not know which of the many hammers will turn out to be "the bottom".
- **Confirmation bias:** once you expect a reversal, you notice the patterns that agree and overlook those that do not.
- **Selective memory:** examples in books and videos are chosen because they worked.
- **Too many patterns:** with dozens of named patterns, something always seems to be forming.

## What research has found

> [!EXAMPLE] Technical analysis in general
> Park and Irwin (2007) reviewed the academic literature. Among **95 "modern" studies**, **56** found positive results for technical trading strategies, **20** negative results and **19** mixed results. But they also found that most studies had problems in their testing procedures: **data snooping** (searching many rules until one fits the past), choosing rules **after** seeing the results, and difficulties estimating **risk and transaction costs**. Early studies found profits in currency and futures markets, but not in stock markets.

> [!EXAMPLE] Computer-detected chart patterns
> Lo, Mamaysky and Wang (2000) built an automated method to detect patterns such as head-and-shoulders and double bottoms, and applied it to US stocks from 1962 to 1996. They concluded that several technical indicators **do provide incremental information** and may have some practical value. "Information" is not the same as a profitable trading strategy after costs.

> [!EXAMPLE] Candlestick strategies
> Marshall's thesis (2005) tested candlestick trading strategies on the stocks of the Dow Jones Industrial Average from 1992 to 2002 and found **no evidence** that a trader following candlestick technical analysis would have beaten the market.

Thomas Bulkowski's large tests (shown in lessons 3 and 4) point the same way: several patterns behaved roughly like a coin toss, and some moved in the **opposite** direction to their traditional reading more often than not.

**Fair summary:** the evidence is mixed. Some studies find that charts contain some information; others find no edge, especially for candlestick patterns and after trading costs. Nobody has shown that patterns reliably predict prices.

## The efficient market debate

The 2013 Prize in Economic Sciences went to Eugene Fama, Lars Peter Hansen and Robert Shiller, whose work looks at this question from different angles. As the Nobel committee summarised it, researchers agree that **past prices are of very little use in predicting returns over the immediate future**, yet the broad course of prices over longer periods (such as three to five years) can be predicted to some extent. The idea that prices quickly reflect available information is called the **efficient market hypothesis**. Technical analysts argue that markets are not perfectly efficient and that crowd psychology leaves traces in charts; critics reply that any reliable pattern would be traded away once many people use it.

## Costs eat small edges

Even if a pattern worked slightly better than chance, every trade costs commissions, the bid-ask spread and possibly taxes (Category G). Strategies with many short trades need a large edge just to break even.

## How careful traders use patterns anyway

1. **Context first:** trend, support and resistance, timeframe (next lessons).
2. **Confirmation:** wait for the next candle or a break of a level instead of acting on the pattern alone.
3. **Define where you are wrong:** decide in advance at which price the idea is invalid (Category P covers stop-losses and position size).
4. **Record and review:** a trading journal shows whether your reading of patterns works for you, after costs (Category N).

> [!MISTAKE] "This pattern has a 70% success rate, so I will win 70% of my trades"
> Published figures usually measure direction under specific rules on past data. They ignore costs, slippage and the size of wins and losses, and they may not hold in the future or in other markets.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Patterns look obvious in hindsight; in real time they are uncertain.
> - Research on technical analysis is mixed, and many studies suffer from data snooping and ignore costs.
> - A study of candlestick strategies on Dow Jones stocks found no evidence of beating the market.
> - Short-term price moves are hard to predict from past prices; this is the core of the efficient market debate.
> - If you use patterns, combine them with context, confirmation, a clear exit and honest record-keeping.
