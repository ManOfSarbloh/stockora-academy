Candlestick shapes have colourful names, and each comes with a **traditional reading**: what traders have long believed it signals. This lesson shows what each shape looks like, what traders read into it, and how it commonly fails.

> [!WARNING] Patterns are probabilities, not promises
> No candle forces the price to do anything. Where available, we show how a pattern actually behaved in Thomas Bulkowski's large tests on US stocks. Those figures measure only the **direction** of the next move, not whether trading the pattern made money after costs. Lesson 5 looks at the evidence in more detail.

In the drawings, grey candles show the context (the move before the pattern) and the coloured candles are the pattern itself.

## Indecision candles

### Doji

![Doji: example drawing](./img/pattern-doji.svg)

- **Looks like:** open and close at (almost) the same price; the body is just a thin line.
- **Traditional reading:** indecision. After a long run, some traders treat it as a warning that the move is tiring.
- **How it fails:** dojis are very common and often mean nothing, especially on short timeframes.

**Variants:** the **long-legged doji** has long wicks on both sides; the **dragonfly doji** has a long lower wick and closes at its high; the **gravestone doji** has a long upper wick and closes at its low. In Bulkowski's tests, the gravestone doji reversed a rise only about half the time (51%).

### Spinning top

![Spinning top: example drawing](./img/pattern-spinning-top.svg)

- **Looks like:** a small body in the middle, with wicks on both sides longer than the body.
- **Traditional reading:** indecision, similar to a doji.
- **How it fails:** it is one of the most frequent candles of all; on its own it carries little information (Bulkowski found a reversal only 50% of the time).

## Strong one-sided candles

### Marubozu

![Marubozu: example drawing](./img/pattern-marubozu.svg)

- **Looks like:** a long body with no (or tiny) wicks. A rising marubozu opens at the low and closes at the high.
- **Traditional reading:** one side was in control from the first trade to the last; traditionally read as momentum in the direction of the body.
- **How it fails:** a big candle can also be the final burst of a move. Bulkowski measured continuation 56% of the time.

## Same shape, different context

### Hammer and hanging man

![Hammer after a decline: example drawing](./img/pattern-hammer.svg)

- **Looks like:** a small body at the top, a long lower wick (traditionally at least twice the body) and little or no upper wick.
- After a **decline** it is called a **hammer**: sellers pushed the price down, but buyers brought it back up. Traditionally read as a possible bullish reversal. Bulkowski: bullish reversal 60% of the time.
- After a **rise** the same shape is a **hanging man**: traditionally read as a warning of a bearish reversal. But in Bulkowski's tests the price more often **kept rising** (bullish continuation 59% of the time).

![Hanging man after a rise: example drawing](./img/pattern-hanging-man.svg)

### Inverted hammer and shooting star

![Shooting star after a rise: example drawing](./img/pattern-shooting-star.svg)

- **Looks like:** a small body at the bottom, a long upper wick and little or no lower wick.
- After a **rise** it is a **shooting star**: buyers pushed higher but sellers took over. Traditionally a possible bearish reversal; Bulkowski: bearish reversal 59% of the time.
- After a **decline** it is an **inverted hammer**: traditionally a possible bullish reversal, but in Bulkowski's tests the decline **continued** 65% of the time.

![Inverted hammer after a decline: example drawing](./img/pattern-inverted-hammer.svg)

> [!MISTAKE] "I found a hammer, so the price will go up"
> A hammer tells you that lower prices were rejected **during one period**. Traders who use it typically wait for **confirmation**, such as the next candle closing higher, and they decide in advance where they would admit the idea is wrong (for example below the hammer's low).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Dojis and spinning tops show indecision; a marubozu shows one-sided pressure.
> - Hammer and hanging man are the same shape; only the trend before them changes the name and the reading.
> - The same is true for the inverted hammer and the shooting star.
> - Traditional readings often fail: some patterns behaved the opposite way in large tests.
> - Wait for confirmation and know where your idea would be proven wrong.
