In January 2021, the share price of GameStop, a US video game retailer, became one of the most talked-about events in market history. The SEC staff later published a detailed report on what happened.

## What happened

According to the SEC staff report:

- **Short interest** in GameStop had hovered around **100%** of its public float through most of 2020, peaking at **109%** on 31 December 2020. That means more shares had been sold short than were freely available.
- On 22 January 2021, the price rose from $43 to $72, a **71%** increase, in about three hours.
- By **27 January**, GameStop closed at **$347.51**, more than **1,600%** above its close on 11 January. The next day it touched an intraday high of **$483**.
- From 13 to 29 January, about **100 million** shares traded per day on average.
- On **28 January**, several brokers **restricted trading** in GameStop. The price fell below $100 by 3 February and as low as **$40.59** by 19 February.

(Prices are as stated in the 2021 report.)

The SEC's press release lists the features GameStop combined: large price moves, large volume changes, large short interest, frequent mentions on Reddit and significant coverage in mainstream media.

## Was it a short squeeze?

Many people described the rise as a short squeeze (lesson 4). The SEC staff found that the run-up coincided with buying by short sellers covering their positions, but that this buying was a small fraction of overall buying. It concluded that **positive sentiment, not buying-to-cover, sustained the weeks-long price rise**, whether that sentiment came from a wish to squeeze short sellers or from belief in the company's fundamentals.

By the end of January, some funds had closed their short positions with **significant losses** (SEC staff). Anyone who bought near the late-January peak saw the price fall by more than 80% within weeks.

## Why brokers restricted trading

The report explains that volatility combined with **settlement risk** led some firms to restrict trading temporarily. Clearing houses demand collateral from brokers to cover the risk of unsettled trades (Category D); when volatility explodes, those demands can jump.

## What the SEC staff wanted to study further

The press release names four areas:

- forces that may cause a broker to restrict trading;
- **digital engagement practices** (app design that encourages trading) and payment for order flow (Category F);
- trading in dark pools and by wholesalers;
- the market dynamics of short selling.

## What it teaches

1. **Prices can detach from businesses for a while.** Nothing in GameStop's business changed by 1,600% in two weeks.
2. **Timing is everything, and unknowable.** Buying on 27 January and buying on 11 January led to completely different outcomes.
3. **Social media can amplify moves** in both directions.
4. **Infrastructure matters.** Trading restrictions and margin demands can change what you are able to do at the most important moment.
5. **Short selling a popular share can be very dangerous** (lesson 4).

> [!WARNING] Meme stocks are speculation
> Shares that move because of online excitement can fall as fast as they rise. If you take part, use only money you can afford to lose completely, and never use borrowed money.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - In January 2021, GameStop rose more than 1,600% in about two weeks, then fell sharply.
> - Short interest had been around 100% of the free float.
> - The SEC staff concluded that positive sentiment, not short covering, sustained the rise.
> - Some brokers restricted trading on 28 January because of volatility and settlement risk.
> - Meme-stock moves are speculative and can reverse quickly.
