Private investors in Austria rarely trade exchange options directly. Leverage is more often offered through **securitised** products issued by banks: **warrants** and various **leverage certificates**. They can be bought through an ordinary securities account, which makes them easy to access, and easy to underestimate.

## Warrants (*Optionsscheine*)

The Wiener Börse lexicon explains that warrants, like options, give the right to buy or sell an underlying at a price fixed in advance, within or at the end of a certain period. The difference: options are **strictly standardised**, while warrants from different issuers have **different features**.

Like options (lesson 5), warrants:

- have **leverage**: their price moves by a larger percentage than the underlying (Wiener Börse lexicon, *Hebel*);
- lose **time value** as expiry approaches;
- can **expire worthless**.

## Certificates are bonds of the issuer

The FMA's Zertifikate-Lupe explains that a **certificate** is legally a **bearer bond**: by buying it, you **lend money to the issuer**, and what you get back depends on the performance of the underlying. This applies to warrants and other bank-issued securities too: if the issuer becomes insolvent, you can lose money even if the underlying does well. This **issuer (credit) risk** does not exist when you hold a share directly.

## Leverage certificates

Banks offer many types of leverage certificates, with names and mechanics that vary from issuer to issuer and product to product. The only reliable way to know how a specific product behaves, including whether it can end before its scheduled maturity, is to read its documents:

- the **Key Information Document (KID)**, which every such product for retail investors must have (Category H);
- the **final terms** of the specific issue.

## Using the KID

The FMA's Zertifikate-Lupe explains three parts of the KID that help compare products:

- **Risk indicator** on a scale of **1 to 7**, where 7 is very high risk. It includes both the market risk of the product and the **credit risk of the issuer**, and assumes you hold the product until maturity.
- **Costs**, shown in euros and as a percentage reduction of the possible return, based on an investment of €10,000.
- **Performance scenarios**, from favourable to unfavourable, also based on €10,000.

The FMA's Zertifikate-Lupe also lets you upload a KID and compare it with the Austrian market.

> [!AUSTRIA] The appropriateness check
> Warrants and leverage certificates are not among the "non-complex" instruments listed in MiFID II Article 25(4). When you buy them without advice, the bank or broker generally has to check whether you have the knowledge and experience to understand the risks, and warn you if it considers the product inappropriate (Category F).

> [!WARNING] Small amounts, big swings
> A leverage product costing €2 can be worth €0.20 a week later. Because the price per unit is small, losses can feel smaller than they are. Look at the percentage, not the euro amount per unit.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Warrants are bank-issued, non-standardised securities with option-like rights.
> - Leverage products move much more than their underlying and can expire worthless.
> - Certificates and warrants are debts of the issuer: you carry the issuer's credit risk.
> - Read the KID: risk indicator (1–7), costs and scenarios; the FMA's Zertifikate-Lupe helps compare.
> - Without advice, firms generally must check whether such complex products are appropriate for you.
