Investor.gov defines a **futures contract** as an agreement to buy or sell a specific quantity of a commodity or financial instrument at a specified price on a particular date in the future.

## Futures versus options

The Wiener Börse lexicon calls a future an **unconditional** forward transaction: **both** sides are **obliged** to buy or sell the underlying at the agreed price at the defined time. An option, by contrast, gives the buyer a **right** (lesson 5).

| | Option buyer | Future (either side) |
|---|---|---|
| Obligation? | No, only a right | Yes |
| Upfront payment | Premium | Margin (collateral) |
| Maximum loss | The premium | Can far exceed the margin |

Futures are **standardised** (contract size, expiry dates, underlying) and therefore tradable on exchanges. Similar contracts agreed privately, outside exchanges, are called **forwards**.

## Daily margin

To trade futures you deposit an **initial margin** (*Einschuss*) as collateral. The Wiener Börse lexicon explains that futures also use **variation margin**, which settles gains and losses **every day**.

> [!EXAMPLE] A futures position day by day
> You buy one index future with a contract value of €100,000 and deposit an initial margin of €8,000.
> - **Day 1:** the index falls 3%. The contract is worth €97,000. €3,000 is taken from your margin account.
> - **Day 2:** another 3% fall (€2,910). Your margin is down to about €2,090. The clearing house demands more money, or your position is closed.
>
> After two bad days you have lost about **74%** of the money you deposited, although the index only fell about 6%. Because both sides are obliged, losses can **exceed** the deposit.

## Why futures exist

- **Hedging:** a farmer can fix the price of a future harvest; an airline can fix fuel costs; a fund can quickly reduce its share exposure by selling index futures.
- **Access:** futures exist on indexes, interest rates, currencies and commodities, so one contract can provide exposure to a whole market.
- **Speculation:** traders use the leverage to bet on price moves.

> [!NOTE] Futures behind other products
> Some funds and exchange-traded products, for example on commodities, may use futures or similar contracts instead of holding the underlying. Their returns can then differ from the price of the underlying itself. The prospectus and KID explain how a product gets its exposure (Category H).

> [!WARNING] Not a product for beginners
> Futures combine high leverage, daily cash demands and losses that can exceed the money deposited.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A future obliges both sides to buy or sell at a fixed price on a set date.
> - Futures are standardised and exchange-traded; private equivalents are forwards.
> - Initial margin opens the position; variation margin settles gains and losses daily.
> - Losses can exceed the deposited margin.
> - Futures are used for hedging, price discovery and speculation.
