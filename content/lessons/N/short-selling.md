Most investors profit when prices rise. A **short seller** aims to profit when they fall.

## How it works

1. You **borrow** shares, usually through your broker, which arranges a securities loan (Category F).
2. You **sell** them at today's price.
3. Later you **buy** the same number of shares back, hopefully cheaper.
4. You **return** them to the lender, paying a borrowing fee.

> [!EXAMPLE] A short sale that works, and one that doesn't
> You sell short 100 borrowed shares at **€50**: you receive €5,000.
> - The price falls to **€40**. You buy back for €4,000. Profit: **€1,000** (minus fees).
> - The price rises to **€80**. You buy back for €8,000. Loss: **€3,000**.
> - The price rises to **€200**. Loss: **€15,000**, three times the amount you received.

> [!ANIMATION] short-sale
> The four steps of a short sale, and what buying back at €40, €80 or €200 means for the seller who sold at €50.

## Limited gain, unlimited loss

- The **most** a short seller can gain is 100% (if the price falls to zero).
- A share price has **no upper limit**, so the possible loss is, in theory, **unlimited**.

That is the opposite of buying a share, where the loss is limited to what you paid.

## Costs and practical risks

- **Borrowing fees**, which can become very high for shares that many people want to short.
- **Dividends:** if the company pays a dividend while you are short, you must compensate the lender.
- **Recall:** the lender can ask for the shares back, forcing you to buy at an inconvenient time.
- **Margin:** the broker requires collateral and can close your position if the price rises (lesson 3).

## The short squeeze

When a heavily shorted share starts to rise, short sellers may rush to buy back to limit their losses. That buying pushes the price up further, forcing more short sellers to buy: a **short squeeze**. GameStop in January 2021 is the best-known recent case (lesson 8). The SEC staff found that short interest in GameStop had hovered around **100%** of its public float through most of 2020, reaching **109%** on 31 December 2020.

## Rules in the EU

The EU's Short Selling Regulation, explained by ESMA, sets transparency rules. Significant **net short positions** in shares must be:

- **reported to the regulator** when they reach **0.1%** of the issued share capital, and at every further 0.1%;
- **published** when they reach **0.5%**, and at every further 0.1%.

The regulation also bans **uncovered ("naked") short selling** of shares: a short seller must have borrowed the shares or have arrangements in place to do so. Regulators can also restrict short selling temporarily in exceptional situations.

> [!NOTE] Short sellers' role
> Short sellers can help markets by spotting overvalued or fraudulent companies; allegations by short sellers were part of the Wirecard story (Category I). They are also criticised for adding pressure in falling markets. Both views exist among regulators and researchers.

> [!WARNING] Not for beginners
> Short selling combines unlimited loss potential, borrowing costs and margin calls. Investor.gov lists short-selling among the typical activities of day traders, whose trading it calls extremely risky.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A short seller sells borrowed shares and hopes to buy them back cheaper.
> - The gain is at most 100%; the potential loss is unlimited.
> - Costs include borrowing fees, dividend compensation and margin requirements.
> - Short squeezes can drive prices sharply higher.
> - EU rules require reporting net short positions from 0.1% and publication from 0.5%, and ban naked short selling of shares.
