**Leverage** means controlling a position larger than your own money, using borrowed funds or a derivative. A leverage of 5:1 means €1,000 of your own money controls a €5,000 position.

## Gains and losses are multiplied

> [!EXAMPLE] The same 10% move, with and without leverage
> You have **€1,000**.
>
> | | No leverage | Leverage 5:1 |
> |---|---:|---:|
> | Position size | €1,000 | €5,000 |
> | Price **+10%** | +€100 (+10%) | +€500 (**+50%**) |
> | Price **−10%** | −€100 (−10%) | −€500 (**−50%**) |
> | Price **−20%** | −€200 (−20%) | −€1,000 (**−100%**) |
>
> With 5:1 leverage, a 20% fall wipes out your entire stake. With 20:1, a 5% fall is enough.

The rule: **% change of your money ≈ % change of the price × leverage** (before costs).

On top of this, borrowed money costs **interest**, which is charged whether the trade wins or loses.

## Buying on margin

Investor.gov describes a **margin account** as a brokerage account in which the broker lends you cash, using your account as collateral, to buy securities. It increases your purchasing power, but also exposes you to the potential for **larger losses**.

### The margin call

If prices fall, Investor.gov warns that your broker can require you to deposit more cash or securities **immediately**, or **sell your securities without informing you in advance**. The broker decides which securities to sell. Even if it says you have a few days, it may still sell before then.

> [!EXAMPLE] Forced out at the bottom
> You buy €10,000 of shares with €5,000 of your own money and a €5,000 loan. Your broker requires your own equity to stay at least 30% of the position.
> - Shares fall 30% to €7,000. Your equity is 7,000 − 5,000 = €2,000, only **29%** of the position.
> - Margin call: add money, or the broker sells. If it sells at €7,000, you are left with **€2,000**: a 60% loss on your own money from a 30% fall.
> - If the shares later recover, you no longer own them.

## Margin in derivatives

With futures and other derivatives, **margin** is the collateral you deposit. The Wiener Börse lexicon distinguishes the **initial margin**, deposited to open a futures position, and the **variation margin**, which settles gains and losses **daily**. A losing position requires fresh money every day, which can force you to close it.

## Leverage in history

Federal Reserve History describes how, in the 1920s, buyers of shares typically put down about 10% of the price and borrowed the rest. When prices fell in 1929, the borrowed money turned a decline into a collapse (Category K).

> [!WARNING] Leverage removes time
> An unleveraged investor in a broad index can wait for a recovery. A leveraged investor may be forced to sell before it comes. That difference turns temporary falls into permanent losses.

> [!MISTAKE] "I'll use a stop-loss, so I can't lose more than I plan"
> In fast markets, prices can jump past a stop level, especially overnight or at the open (gaps, Category J). With leverage, a gap can cost more than you planned, and with some products more than you invested.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Leverage multiplies gains and losses: % result ≈ % price move × leverage.
> - At 5:1, a 20% fall wipes out the whole stake.
> - Investor.gov: in a margin call, a broker can sell your securities without notice.
> - In futures, daily variation margin can require new money every day.
> - Leverage can force you to sell before a recovery.
