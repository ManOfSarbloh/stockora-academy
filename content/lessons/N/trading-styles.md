> [!WARNING] Read this category as a risk briefing
> Short-term trading, leverage and derivatives can lose money very quickly. This category explains how they work and what regulators have found, so you can recognise the risks. It is not an invitation to trade.

Everyone who buys a share hopes it will be worth more later. What separates **investing** from **trading** is mainly the time frame and the source of the expected profit.

## The main styles

| Style | Typical holding period | What the trader relies on |
|---|---|---|
| **Day trading** | Seconds to hours; all positions closed by the end of the day | Short price movements |
| **Swing trading** | Days to a few weeks | Price swings, often using charts (Category J) |
| **Position trading** | Weeks to months | Larger trends or events |
| **Buy-and-hold investing** | Years to decades | The growth of businesses and the economy (Category M) |

Investor.gov describes **day traders** as people who rapidly buy, sell and short-sell shares throughout the day, hoping prices keep moving for the seconds or minutes they hold them. It adds plainly: day trading is **extremely risky** and can result in **substantial financial losses in a very short period of time**.

## Why time frame changes the game

- **Costs add up.** Every trade costs spreads, fees and sometimes currency conversion (Category G). A long-term investor might trade a few times a year; a day trader may trade dozens of times a day.
- **The opponent changes.** Over minutes, you compete with professional firms using fast technology and better data (Category D). Over decades, you mainly share in what companies earn.
- **Time and stress.** Active trading is close to a job, with constant attention and emotional pressure (Category O).

> [!EXAMPLE] How trading costs pile up
> A trader buys and sells €5,000 of shares 10 times a day, with total costs (spread plus fees) of 0.1% per round trip.
> - Cost per round trip: €5.
> - Per day: €50. Over 220 trading days: **€11,000**.
>
> The trader must earn more than €11,000 a year from price moves just to break even, more than twice the €5,000 used.

> [!AUSTRIA] Taxes on frequent trading
> For private investors in Austria, realised gains on shares are generally subject to the special income tax rate of 27.5% (§ 27a EStG). Frequent trading means many taxable events, and losses can only be offset within the limits of the law. Category Q explains the rules; check them with official sources.

## "Prop trading" challenges

Online platforms increasingly offer "funded trader" programmes: you pay a fee, pass a trading **challenge**, and are promised a share of profits from trading the firm's capital. The **FMA** warns about such models:

- Traders often have to **pay first**, for training, verification phases or challenges, before they can receive anything.
- With paid challenges, the relationship can resemble that of a **player and a casino**: the company sets the rules, which need not be balanced, and one side's gain is often the other's loss.
- Often no real capital is traded; it is a **simulation**. Traders who lost money reported **spreads far wider** than at real brokers, so positions started with a loss.
- The FMA notes that the business model often lives **primarily from the fees** many traders pay for challenges, not from realistic trading, and that platforms use pressure, gamification and large discounts to bring traders back.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Trading styles range from day trading (hours) to buy-and-hold investing (years).
> - Investor.gov calls day trading extremely risky, with possible substantial losses in a very short time.
> - Shorter time frames mean higher costs, stronger competition and more noise.
> - In Austria, realised gains are generally taxed at 27.5%; frequent trading creates many taxable events.
> - The FMA warns that paid "prop trading" challenges often profit mainly from participants' fees.
