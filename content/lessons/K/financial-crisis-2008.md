The crisis of 2007–2009 began in a corner of the US housing market and spread to banks, stock markets and economies around the world. It shows how debt and complex financial products can turn a housing slump into a global crisis.

## How it built up

Federal Reserve History describes the chain of events:

1. **Easy mortgages.** In the early and mid-2000s, lenders offered mortgages to borrowers who previously would have struggled to get one: people with weak credit histories, small down payments or high payments relative to income (**subprime** borrowers).
2. **Packaging and selling the risk.** Lenders bundled these mortgages into pools and sold them to investors as **mortgage-backed securities**. Some parts were seen as low risk because they were insured or because other parts would absorb losses first.
3. **Rising house prices** made it all seem safe. Borrowers who could not pay could sell at a profit or borrow more against their home. This fed expectations of further price rises and more demand.
4. **Untested assumptions.** Because such a period was unprecedented, the risks of these securities may not have been well understood; many measures of loan quality were based on traditional mortgages.

## How it unravelled

- When house prices peaked, borrowers could no longer refinance or sell their way out, and losses began to rise.
- In **April 2007**, New Century Financial, a leading subprime lender, filed for bankruptcy. Many mortgage securities were downgraded, and several subprime lenders closed.
- Funding for risky mortgages dried up, house prices slid further, and losses spread.
- In the **summer of 2008**, the government-sponsored mortgage giants **Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac** suffered large losses and were seized by the US government.
- In the second half of 2008 the outlook deteriorated sharply; the Fed made most of its interest rate cuts between September and December 2008.

## The damage

According to Federal Reserve History:

- The **Great Recession** lasted from **December 2007 to June 2009**, the longest US recession since the Second World War.
- US real GDP fell **4.3%** from peak to trough; unemployment rose from **5%** to a peak of **10%** in October 2009.
- Home prices fell about **30%** from their mid-2006 peak.
- The **S&P 500 fell 57%** from its October 2007 peak to its trough in March 2009.
- US household net worth fell from about $69 trillion to $55 trillion.

In Damodaran's data, the S&P 500 including dividends returned **−36.6% in 2008**, its worst calendar year since 1931, followed by **+25.9% in 2009**. Using year-end values with dividends, the end-2007 level was regained at the end of **2012**.

## The response

Learning from the 1930s, central banks and governments acted on a large scale:

- The Fed cut its policy rate from **5.25%** in September 2007 to **0–0.25%** in December 2008.
- It promised to keep rates low for an extended period (**forward guidance**).
- It bought large amounts of longer-term bonds and mortgage securities to push down long-term interest rates (**quantitative easing**). Under the first programmes, often called QE1, it bought about **$1.75 trillion** of longer-term assets.
- Governments passed fiscal stimulus programmes.

Europe later faced its own sovereign debt crisis, which Category L covers.

## What it teaches

1. **Leverage everywhere.** Households, banks and investment vehicles all borrowed heavily. When prices fell, debt multiplied the losses.
2. **"Safe" can be wrong.** Securities rated as low risk turned out to be very risky. Ratings and models are not guarantees.
3. **Diversification helps, but risky assets tend to fall together in a panic.** High-quality government bonds behaved differently: in Damodaran's data, 10-year US Treasury bonds returned **+20.1%** in 2008 while US shares lost 36.6% (Category M).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Risky mortgages, packaged into securities and supported by rising house prices, set up the crisis.
> - When house prices fell, losses spread through the financial system in 2007–2008.
> - The S&P 500 fell 57% from October 2007 to March 2009; the US recession lasted from December 2007 to June 2009.
> - Central banks cut rates to near zero and bought bonds on a large scale.
> - Leverage and misjudged "safe" assets turned a housing slump into a global crisis.
