The COVID-19 crash is a case study in how quickly markets can fall, and how quickly they can recover when the shock is sudden and the policy response is massive.

## The fall

As the coronavirus spread beyond China in early 2020, global asset prices dropped. The New York Fed describes what happened in the US:

- The S&P 500 declined steeply, triggering **market-wide circuit breakers that halted trading three times in March**.
- By **23 March 2020**, the stock market had fallen **34%** from its February 2020 high.
- Investors fled to the safety of US government bonds, pushing yields to record lows. Volatility measures rose sharply.
- Usual relationships between markets broke down, amid poor liquidity in the Treasury market and forced selling by leveraged hedge funds.

The Federal Reserve's May 2020 Financial Stability Report adds that for a time markets were "severely dislocated", with historically high volatility and severely strained liquidity.

## The response

The Fed acted fast and on a huge scale (New York Fed):

- It cut its policy rate by 0.5 percentage points on **3 March** and by a further 1 percentage point on **15 March**, to near zero. Both cuts came between scheduled meetings; the second was the first on a weekend since the Fed began announcing rate changes in 1994.
- It announced purchases of government bonds and mortgage securities, and on **23 March** said it would buy them "in the amounts needed".
- It set up lending facilities to keep credit flowing to households and businesses.

## The economy and the recovery

- The NBER dated the US business cycle **peak to February 2020** and the **trough to April 2020**: an extremely short but deep recession. The committee explained that the drop was so great and so widespread that it counted as a recession even though it was brief.
- The stock market bottomed on 23 March, **before** the economy (lesson 2).
- In Damodaran's data, the S&P 500 including dividends ended 2020 with a gain of **18.0%** for the year, despite the crash.

> [!NOTE] Not every crash recovers this fast
> The speed of the 2020 recovery came from a specific mix: a sudden shock from outside the financial system, and a very fast and very large response from the central bank and the government. Lessons 4 and 5 show crashes where recovery took decades.

## What it teaches

1. **Nobody predicted the timing.** The shock came from outside the financial system.
2. **Market plumbing matters.** Circuit breakers paused trading, and even government bond markets became hard to trade.
3. **Panic selling was costly.** Investors who sold near the low on 23 March locked in losses just before a strong rebound.
4. **Policy can change the outcome.** Compare the response with 1929 (lesson 5).

> [!MISTAKE] Concluding that "the central bank will always rescue the market"
> Policy support helped in 2020, but it cannot be relied on. In 2022 the S&P 500 lost 18%, and 10-year US Treasury bonds also lost about 18% in the same year (Damodaran's data), so even a mix of shares and bonds fell. The cause of a fall matters (Category L).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - US stocks fell 34% from their February 2020 high to 23 March 2020; circuit breakers halted trading three times.
> - The Fed cut rates to near zero and bought bonds "in the amounts needed".
> - The US recession lasted from February to April 2020 (NBER).
> - The S&P 500 including dividends still gained 18% in 2020.
> - Fast recoveries are possible but not guaranteed; the cause and the policy response matter.
