The economy grows in waves. Periods of growth (**expansions**) are interrupted by periods of decline (**recessions**). Together they form the **business cycle**. Company profits rise and fall with it, so share prices are linked to it, but less tightly than many people expect.

## What is a recession?

In the US, recessions are dated by the Business Cycle Dating Committee of the **National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER)**, a private research organisation. Its definition: a recession is a **significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months**. It looks at three criteria, depth, diffusion and duration, and treats them as somewhat interchangeable: an extreme drop can count even if it is short.

In Europe and the media you will also hear a simpler rule of thumb: two quarters in a row of falling GDP. That is a convention, not an official definition.

> [!NOTE] Dates are set afterwards
> The NBER announces peaks and troughs only once enough data is available, often many months later. Nobody knows in real time exactly when a recession has started or ended.

## Phases of the cycle

1. **Expansion:** output, jobs and profits grow.
2. **Peak:** growth is at its highest; costs and interest rates are often rising.
3. **Recession:** activity, profits and employment fall.
4. **Trough:** the low point, after which recovery begins.

## Markets look ahead

Share prices reflect expectations of **future** profits. So the stock market often falls **before** a recession is visible in the data and starts to recover **before** the economy does.

> [!EXAMPLE] 2020: the market bottomed before the economy
> The New York Fed notes that by **23 March 2020** US stocks had fallen 34% from their February 2020 high. The NBER later dated the trough of the recession to **April 2020**. Prices had begun to rise while the economy was still shrinking.

> [!EXAMPLE] 2007–2009
> According to Federal Reserve History, the Great Recession lasted from **December 2007 to June 2009**, while the S&P 500 fell 57% from its **October 2007** peak to its trough in **March 2009**: the stock market peaked before the recession began and bottomed before it ended.

## The link is loose

- **Crashes without recessions:** after the 1987 crash, Federal Reserve History notes, there was no recession or banking crisis (lesson 6).
- **Recessions with mild market falls** also happen.
- **Interest rates and valuations** matter too. A market can fall in a good economy if interest rates rise sharply or prices were too high (Category L).

## Sectors and the cycle

Not all companies are hit equally:

- **Cyclical** companies (cars, construction, travel, raw materials, luxury goods) see profits swing strongly with the economy.
- **Defensive** companies (food, household goods, utilities, healthcare) sell things people buy in any economy, so their profits are steadier.

Category C explains cyclical and defensive stocks. Trying to switch between them at the right moment ("sector rotation") requires predicting the cycle, which professionals also find very hard.

> [!MISTAKE] "The economy looks bad, so stocks must fall"
> By the time bad economic news is in the headlines, prices may already reflect it, and the market may be looking ahead to recovery. Equally, a strong economy does not guarantee rising prices.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - The NBER defines a recession as a significant, widespread decline lasting more than a few months.
> - Recession dates are set months after the fact.
> - Stock markets usually look ahead: they often fall before and recover before the economy.
> - Crashes and recessions do not always come together.
> - Cyclical companies swing with the economy; defensive companies are steadier.
