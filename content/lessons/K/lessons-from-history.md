The previous lessons covered individual crashes. This lesson steps back and asks what the long record says, using Aswath Damodaran's yearly data on the US S&P 500 (including dividends) from **1928 to 2025**.

## 1. Losses are normal

The index ended the year lower in **26 of 98 years**. A loss of 10%, 20% or even more in a year is not a rare event; it is part of owning shares. Anyone who invests in shares should expect to live through several bear markets.

## 2. Time has usually helped, in this data

Here is how often the S&P 500 (with dividends, before costs, taxes and inflation) had a positive total return over different holding periods, counting every possible starting year:

| Holding period | Periods in the data | Periods with a gain | Worst average yearly return |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 year | 98 | 72 (73%) | −43.8% |
| 5 years | 94 | 83 (88%) | −12.7% a year |
| 10 years | 89 | 84 (94%) | −1.7% a year |
| 20 years | 79 | 79 (100%) | +2.4% a year |

The longer the holding period, the less likely a loss was. But note the last column: even 20 years produced only a modest gain in the worst case, and these figures ignore inflation.

> [!EXAMPLE] Compounding over almost a century
> In Damodaran's table, $100 invested in the S&P 500 at the start of 1928, with all dividends reinvested, grew to about **$1.16 million** by the end of 2025. This is in nominal dollars, before inflation, costs and taxes, and nobody actually invests for 98 years, but it shows the power of compounding (Category G).

## 3. Recovery times vary enormously

Using year-end values including dividends:

| Peak (year-end) | Back above that level |
|---|---|
| 1928 | end of 1936 |
| 1999 | end of 2006 |
| 2007 | end of 2012 |
| 2021 | end of 2023 |

And outside these yearly figures:

- In price terms, the Dow Jones needed until **1954** to regain its 1929 peak (Federal Reserve History).
- After 1987, US markets surpassed their pre-crash highs in **less than two years** (Federal Reserve History).
- Japan's Nikkei 225 needed more than **34 years** to pass its 1989 high (lesson 4).

## 4. Beware of the success story bias

Most long-run statistics you will see, including the ones above, come from the **US market**: a market that survived and prospered. An investor a century ago could not know which markets would do well, and other markets had very different histories:

- In **Vienna in 1873**, about half of the listed companies disappeared (Wiener Börse).
- **Japan** went more than three decades without regaining its 1989 high.

Drawing conclusions only from the winners is a form of **survivorship bias**.

## 5. What history suggests for an ordinary investor

1. **Expect falls, and plan for them.** Only invest money you will not need for many years; keep an emergency fund in safe assets.
2. **Avoid borrowing to invest.** In 1929 and 2008, leverage turned falls into disasters.
3. **Diversify** across companies, sectors and countries, so that no single failure or "lost decade" can ruin you.
4. **Invest regularly.** A savings plan buys more shares when prices are low (Category G, cost-average effect).
5. **Have a written plan** before the next crash, so you are not deciding in a panic (Category M and O).
6. **Do not assume the future will copy the past.** History shows what is possible, not what will happen.

> [!WARNING] Not investment advice
> These are general lessons from history. They do not tell you what to buy or when. Your own situation, time horizon and ability to bear losses matter.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - In US data for 1928–2025, about one year in four ended with a loss.
> - Longer holding periods produced losses less often, but the worst 20-year period still gained only about 2.4% a year before inflation.
> - Recovery from crashes has taken anything from under two years to several decades.
> - US data comes from a market that prospered; Vienna 1873 and Japan 1989 show the other side.
> - Plan for falls, avoid leverage, diversify and invest regularly.
