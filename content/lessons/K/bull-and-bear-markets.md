Stock markets do not rise in a straight line. They move in long waves of optimism and pessimism. Investors use two animal names for these waves.

## Bull and bear

Investor.gov (the SEC's investor education site) defines them like this:

- A **bull market** is a time when prices are rising and sentiment is optimistic. Generally, it is when a broad market index rises by **20% or more** over at least a two-month period.
- A **bear market** is a time when prices are falling and sentiment is pessimistic. Generally, it is when a broad market index falls by **20% or more** over at least a two-month period.

A smaller fall, often around 10%, is commonly called a **correction**. There is no official definition of a correction; it is market slang.

> [!NOTE] Thresholds are conventions
> Nothing magical happens at exactly −20%. The labels are a convenient shorthand used by the media and investors. What matters to you is the size of the fall, how long it lasts and how long the recovery takes.

## Drawdown: measuring the fall

A **drawdown** is the fall from the highest point (the peak) to a later low (the trough), in percent.

> [!EXAMPLE] A drawdown
> An index peaks at 4,000 points and later falls to 2,600.
> Drawdown = (2,600 − 4,000) ÷ 4,000 = **−35%**.

## Losses and gains are not symmetric

This is one of the most important facts in investing. After a loss, you need a **bigger** percentage gain just to get back to where you started, because the gain is calculated on a smaller amount.

| Loss | Gain needed to break even |
|---:|---:|
| −10% | +11.1% |
| −20% | +25% |
| −33% | +50% |
| −50% | +100% |
| −80% | +400% |
| −90% | +900% |

Formula: gain needed = 1 ÷ (1 − loss) − 1.

> [!EXAMPLE] Losing half
> €10,000 falls by 50% to €5,000. A 50% gain only brings it to €7,500. To get back to €10,000, it must **double** (+100%).

This is why large losses are so damaging, and why risk management (Category P) focuses on avoiding them.

## How often do markets fall?

Aswath Damodaran at NYU Stern publishes the yearly total return of the US S&P 500 index (including dividends) since 1928. Using his data for **1928 to 2025** (98 years):

- The index ended the year **lower in 26 of the 98 years**, roughly one year in four.
- The worst calendar years were **1931 (−43.8%)**, **2008 (−36.6%)** and **1937 (−35.3%)**.
- The best included **1954 (+52.6%)** and **1933 (+50.0%)**.
- Over longer holding periods, losses were rarer: every **20-year period** in this data had a positive total return, although some 10-year periods were negative.

> [!WARNING] The past is not a guarantee
> These figures describe one country, the US, whose market did unusually well in the 20th century. Other markets had much longer bad periods (lesson 4 looks at Japan). The figures are also before costs, taxes and inflation, and in US dollars.

## Why markets fall

Bear markets have very different causes: a bursting bubble (2000), a banking crisis (2008), a pandemic (2020), high inflation and rising interest rates, or war. Some come with a deep recession; some do not. The lessons in this category walk through the most important episodes.

> [!MISTAKE] "I'll sell before the bear market and buy back at the bottom"
> Peaks and troughs are only clear afterwards, and rebounds can be sudden. After the crash of 19 October 1987, the Dow Jones regained 57% of that day's loss within two trading sessions (Federal Reserve History). Investors who sell in a panic can miss such moves. Category O covers the psychology.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Bull market: a rise of 20% or more; bear market: a fall of 20% or more (Investor.gov's general definitions).
> - A drawdown measures the fall from a peak to a later low.
> - Losses need bigger gains to recover: −50% needs +100%.
> - In the US data for 1928–2025, about one year in four ended with a loss.
> - Long-run history from one market is a guide, not a promise.
