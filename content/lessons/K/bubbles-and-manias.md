A **bubble** is a period in which prices rise far above what the underlying assets can reasonably justify, driven by enthusiasm and the expectation of further price rises rather than by income. When the enthusiasm fades, prices can collapse.

Bubbles are easy to recognise afterwards and hard to recognise at the time. There is no reliable formula. But history shows recurring ingredients.

## Five common ingredients

**1. A compelling story.** Something genuinely new: railways and industry in the 1870s, cars and radio in the 1920s, the internet in the 1990s. The story is often true in the long run; the mistake is the price paid for it.

**2. Easy money and borrowing.** Federal Reserve History describes how, in the late 1920s, buyers of shares typically put down only about **10%** of the price and borrowed the rest, using the shares as collateral. Borrowed money poured into the market. Borrowing makes the rise faster and the fall more violent, because falling prices force borrowers to sell.

**3. New investors.** Rising prices attract people who have never invested before. In the 1920s, Federal Reserve History notes, ordinary men and women invested growing sums, helped by new brokerage houses and margin accounts.

**4. Extreme valuations.** Prices lose touch with profits. The Cleveland Fed reported that in March 2000 the largest US technology companies traded at a market-weighted price-earnings ratio of about **126**, far above historical norms, and concluded that the rise and fall of these valuations was compelling evidence of a bubble.

**5. "This time is different."** Experts explain why old rules no longer apply. After prices peaked in 1929, the economist Irving Fisher declared that stock prices had reached "what looks like a permanently high plateau" (quoted by Federal Reserve History). Weeks later the crash began.

> [!EXAMPLE] The same pattern, different centuries
> - **Vienna, 1867–1873:** rapid industrialisation, many new companies raising money on the exchange, speculation that also financed unsound companies. Then the crash of 9 May 1873 (lesson 4).
> - **Japan, late 1980s:** a strong economy and confidence that "every aspect of the economy is benefiting from the strong yen" (Nikkei Indexes). The Nikkei 225 ended 1989 at a record and many expected 50,000 yen.
> - **US housing, 2000s:** easy mortgages and rising house prices fed expectations of further gains (lesson 8).

## How bubbles burst

The trigger is often small: higher interest rates, a disappointing piece of news, or simply running out of new buyers. Once prices stop rising, buyers who only bought because prices were rising have no reason to stay. Borrowers are forced to sell. Selling pushes prices down further.

> [!WARNING] Being early is the same as being wrong
> Even if you correctly suspect a bubble, prices can keep rising for years. Betting against a bubble with borrowed money or short positions (Category N) has ruined many investors who were right about the direction but wrong about the timing.

## What an ordinary investor can do

- **Be honest about why you are buying.** Because of the business and its cash flows, or because the price has been rising?
- **Check valuations** (Category I) against history. Extreme ratios are a warning, not a timing signal.
- **Avoid borrowing to invest** in euphoric markets.
- **Diversify.** A bubble in one sector or country hurts much less if it is only part of your portfolio.
- **Be suspicious of certainty.** "It can only go up" is a red flag.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A bubble is a rise far beyond what income can justify, driven by expectations of further rises.
> - Common ingredients: a real story, borrowed money, new investors, extreme valuations and "this time is different".
> - Bubbles are clear afterwards and hard to time beforehand.
> - Leverage makes both the rise and the crash more extreme.
> - Diversification and avoiding debt limit the damage when a bubble bursts.
