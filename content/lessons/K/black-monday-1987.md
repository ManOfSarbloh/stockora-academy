On **Monday, 19 October 1987**, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell **22.6%** (508 points) in a single trading session. Federal Reserve History calls it the largest one-day stock market decline in history.

## Before the crash

- Markets had risen strongly. By late August 1987, the Dow had gained **44%** in seven months, which raised concerns about a bubble.
- In mid-October, news unsettled investors: a larger-than-expected US trade deficit and a falling dollar.
- From 14 October, markets suffered large daily losses. On Friday 16 October the Dow lost **4.6%**, on a day when options and futures contracts expired together (**triple witching**, Category E).

## The day itself

Over the weekend, the US Treasury Secretary publicly threatened to devalue the dollar. On Monday, markets in Asia fell before New York even opened. Sell orders far outnumbered buyers near previous prices, creating a cascade. Federal Reserve History reports that New Zealand's market fell **60%** in the most severe case.

For the first time, people watched on live television as a financial crisis spread from market to market.

## What caused it

Regulators and economists identified several likely causes (Federal Reserve History):

- **International investors** had become very active in US markets, contributing to the rapid rise.
- **Portfolio insurance**, a then-popular strategy using options and futures, **sold automatically as prices fell**, so initial losses triggered further selling.
- **Structural weaknesses:** stock, options and futures markets cleared and settled trades on different timelines, which could leave trading accounts negative and force sales. Exchanges also had no tools to pause trading during extreme declines.

> [!NOTE] Automatic selling is a recurring theme
> Whenever many investors follow rules that say "sell when prices fall" (stop orders, margin calls, some trading strategies), a fall can feed on itself. The Federal Reserve's May 2020 Financial Stability Report made a similar point: some hedge fund strategies require selling when equity prices fall, which can amplify market moves in stress events.

## The response and the aftermath

- On 20 October 1987, Fed Chairman Alan Greenspan said the Federal Reserve was ready to serve as a **source of liquidity** for the economic and financial system, and the Fed encouraged banks to keep lending.
- Unlike many earlier crises, the crash was **not followed by a recession or a banking crisis**.
- Markets recovered quickly: within two trading sessions the Dow regained **57%** of the Black Monday loss, and US markets surpassed their pre-crash highs in **less than two years**.

## Reforms: circuit breakers

After Black Monday, regulators harmonised clearing across products and introduced **circuit breakers**: rules that halt trading temporarily after exceptionally large declines. Federal Reserve History gives the current US example: the New York Stock Exchange halts trading when the S&P 500 falls **7%, 13% and 20%**. European exchanges use different mechanisms, such as the volatility interruptions on the Vienna Stock Exchange (Category D).

> [!TIP] A one-day crash is not the same as a long bear market
> Investors who held on through October 1987 were back above the pre-crash level within about two years. Investors who held on in 1929 waited far longer. The difference lay largely in what followed: in 1987 the financial system held together and there was no recession.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6% in one day, the largest one-day fall in its history.
> - Rapid gains, bad news, automatic selling (portfolio insurance) and market plumbing problems combined.
> - The Fed provided liquidity; there was no recession or banking crisis.
> - US markets regained their pre-crash highs in under two years.
> - Circuit breakers were introduced to pause trading in extreme falls.
