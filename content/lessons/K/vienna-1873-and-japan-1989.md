Many investors learn that "stocks always recover". In broad, diversified markets over long periods this has often been true, but not always quickly, and not for every company. These two episodes, one Austrian and one Japanese, show the downside.

## Vienna, 9 May 1873

> [!AUSTRIA] The Vienna stock market crash
> According to the Vienna Stock Exchange's own history, the Austro-Hungarian Empire went through rapid industrialisation and growth from 1867 to 1873, an era known as the *Gründerzeit* ("founders' period"). Many companies raised money by issuing shares. Liberal economic policy also encouraged unsound companies, and the wave of speculation ended with a crash on **9 May 1873**.
>
> - About **half of the listed stock corporations disappeared** from trading.
> - It took **years** for the Vienna Stock Exchange to recover.
> - The crash showed the need for new rules. The **Stock Exchange Act of 1 April 1875** introduced one of the first modern stock market laws in Europe.

Lessons from 1873:

- **Individual companies can vanish.** An index of surviving companies hides the shareholders who lost everything in the ones that disappeared.
- **Regulation often follows crises.** Many investor protections we take for granted were written after a crash (Category R).

## Japan, 1989 to 2024

The Nikkei Indexes history describes the late 1980s in Japan as a time of enormous confidence. The Nikkei 225 passed 30,000 yen for the first time in December 1988, rose nearly 8,800 yen in one year, and **ended 1989 at a record high of 38,915 yen**. Many people expected 50,000.

Then the bubble burst:

- In March 1992 the index fell below 20,000 yen, almost **half** its 1989 high.
- In August 1992 it was at the 14,000 yen level.
- In October 1998, amid a banking crisis, it fell to **12,879 yen**.

The index did not close above its 1989 high until **22 February 2024**, more than **34 years** later (Nikkei Asia).

> [!NOTE] Price index versus total return
> The Nikkei 225 is a price index: it does not include dividends. An investor who reinvested dividends did somewhat better than the price index suggests, but still waited a very long time.

## What these cases teach

1. **"Long term" can be longer than your investment horizon.** Someone who invested in Japanese shares at the end of 1989 at age 40 would have been 74 before the price index got back to its starting point.
2. **Valuation at the start matters.** Buying when prices are extremely high relative to profits can mean poor returns for a very long time.
3. **Diversification across countries** protects against a single market's lost decades. An investor spread across many countries did not depend on the fate of one market.
4. **Diversification across companies** protects against individual failures like those of 1873.

> [!MISTAKE] "The index always comes back, so I can hold anything"
> Broad indexes have often recovered because failed companies are replaced by new ones. A single share has no such mechanism. And even a whole national market can take decades.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - The Vienna crash of 9 May 1873 ended the Gründerzeit boom; about half of the listed companies disappeared.
> - Austria's Stock Exchange Act of 1875 followed the crash.
> - Japan's Nikkei 225 peaked at the end of 1989 and only closed above that level again on 22 February 2024.
> - Recovery can take decades, and single companies may never recover.
> - Diversification across companies and countries is the main defence.
