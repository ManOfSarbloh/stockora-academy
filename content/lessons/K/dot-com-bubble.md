In the late 1990s the internet was transforming business and daily life. Investors rushed into anything connected to it. Many of the companies were real and some became giants. But prices ran so far ahead of profits that a crash was, in the Cleveland Fed's later words, "destined" to come.

## The boom

- The technology-heavy **Nasdaq Composite** index rose past **5,000** points (Cleveland Fed).
- The S&P 500 also rose strongly, and US productivity was accelerating, which seemed to support the optimism.
- Valuations became extreme. The Cleveland Fed cites an analysis of the largest US companies in March 2000: the 18 large technology companies among them traded at a market-weighted **price-earnings ratio of 125.9** on 7 March 2000, and half of them had P/E ratios above 100. In other words, investors were paying more than a century of current profits.

## The bust

- In **March 2000** the mood changed and the Nasdaq began to fall.
- By the **end of 2000** it had fallen **44.7%** from its March peak (US Congress Joint Economic Committee).
- The S&P 500, which includes many non-technology companies, fell much less in 2000: about **15%** from its peak to the year-end, according to the Cleveland Fed.
- The decline continued: Damodaran's data shows the S&P 500 (with dividends) losing money three years in a row: **−9.0% in 2000, −11.9% in 2001 and −22.0% in 2002**.

## The economy

The US entered a recession from **March to November 2001**, which the JEC report describes as one of the shortest on record. The San Francisco Fed noted that the economic damage was milder than the average recession, largely because consumers kept spending, but **business investment fell much more than usual**: the investment boom of the late 1990s had overshot.

## What it teaches

1. **A great technology is not the same as a great investment.** Paying 100 times earnings requires decades of exceptional growth just to justify the price.
2. **Concentration hurts.** Investors who held mostly technology shares suffered far more than those holding a broad index.
3. **Index weights can become concentrated.** As one sector booms, it becomes a bigger part of market-cap weighted indexes (Category H), so even "passive" investors become more exposed to it.
4. **Recovery can be slow.** Damodaran's year-end data, with dividends, shows the S&P 500 back above its end-1999 level only at the end of **2006**.

> [!MISTAKE] "This company will change the world, so the share must be a good buy"
> Both can be true and the investment can still be terrible if the price already assumes more success than the company can ever deliver. Category I shows how to compare price and profits.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Late-1990s optimism about the internet pushed technology valuations to extreme levels (P/E around 126 for large tech stocks in March 2000).
> - The Nasdaq peaked in March 2000 and fell 44.7% by the end of that year.
> - The S&P 500 fell in 2000, 2001 and 2002; the US had a short recession in 2001.
> - Concentrated technology investors suffered most; broad diversification softened the blow.
> - A great story does not justify any price.
