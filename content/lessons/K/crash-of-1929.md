The crash of 1929 is the most famous market collapse in history, and the Great Depression that followed was, in the words Federal Reserve History quotes from Ben Bernanke, the "worst economic disaster in American history".

## The boom

Federal Reserve History describes the 1920s as an era of optimism. New technologies such as cars and telephones spread, families prospered, and ordinary people invested growing sums in shares. The **Dow Jones Industrial Average** rose about **six-fold**, from 63 in August 1921 to **381** in September 1929.

Much of the buying was done with borrowed money. Buyers typically paid about **10%** of the price themselves and borrowed the rest, with the shares as collateral (buying **on margin**, see Category N).

## The crash

- The Dow peaked on **3 September 1929**, closing at **381.17**.
- On **Black Monday, 28 October 1929**, it fell nearly **13%**.
- On **Black Tuesday, 29 October**, it fell nearly **12%** more.
- By mid-November it had lost almost **half** its value.
- The slide continued until **8 July 1932**, when the Dow closed at **41.22**, **89% below its peak**.
- In price terms, the Dow did not regain its 1929 high until **23 November 1954**, 25 years later.

> [!EXAMPLE] How margin turned a fall into a wipe-out
> You buy shares worth $1,000, paying $100 yourself and borrowing $900.
> If the shares fall by 10% to $900, your $100 is gone: the shares only cover the loan.
> In practice the lender demands more money (a **margin call**) or sells your shares, adding to the selling pressure. Multiply this by thousands of investors and you get a cascade.

## The Great Depression

The crash was followed by a deep and long depression. Federal Reserve History describes it as lasting a decade, from 1929 until the Second World War, with a series of crises: the stock market crash in 1929, regional banking panics in 1930 and 1931, and national and international financial crises from 1931 to 1933. It hit bottom in **March 1933**, when the commercial banking system collapsed.

The Federal Reserve made mistakes: it let the banking system collapse and money and prices fall. In 2002, Fed governor Ben Bernanke said publicly: "Regarding the Great Depression ... we did it. We're very sorry. ... We won't do it again."

## Did investors really wait 25 years?

The 1954 date refers to the Dow's **price** level. Investors also received dividends, and prices of goods fell sharply during the Depression, so the real picture was less extreme. Damodaran's S&P 500 data, which includes dividends, shows the year-end value of an investment made at the start of 1929 falling for four years in a row (−8.3%, −25.1%, −43.8%, −8.6%). With dividends reinvested, the value reached at the end of 1928 was regained by the end of **1936**, only to be hit again by a fall of 35.3% in 1937.

Seven years of waiting, with a loss of more than half along the way, is still a brutal experience.

## Why it still matters

- **Leverage amplifies crashes.** Margin lending turned a market fall into a financial panic.
- **Bank failures spread the damage** to people who never owned a share.
- **Policy matters.** Central banks and governments learned from 1929; their very different responses in 1987, 2008 and 2020 were shaped by it.
- **Regulation followed.** The US securities laws of the 1930s and the creation of the SEC came out of this period (Category R).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - The Dow rose six-fold in the 1920s, fuelled by borrowed money.
> - Black Monday (28 Oct 1929) and Black Tuesday (29 Oct) started a fall of 89% to July 1932.
> - The Dow's price level took until November 1954 to recover; with dividends the recovery was faster, but still took years.
> - The Great Depression lasted a decade and included waves of bank failures.
> - Leverage and bank failures turned a crash into an economic catastrophe.
