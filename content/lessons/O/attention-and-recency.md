## The availability heuristic

The FMA explains that investors like to rely on information that is **readily available**: current and easy to remember. They therefore consider future scenarios based on easily remembered information **more likely** than scenarios that are harder to imagine. As a result, risks that have not occurred for a long time tend to be **underestimated**. On financial markets, the FMA notes, this heuristic tends to **reinforce the current view** of the market.

> [!EXAMPLE] After years of rising prices
> If markets have risen for five years, a crash feels unlikely because nobody around you has recently experienced one. Yet the history in Category K shows that large falls are a normal part of share investing.

## The recency effect

People also give **more weight to recent information** than to older information. The FMA explains this with the limited capacity of short-term memory: the latest information stays present when nothing new follows.

The FMA also warns that some advisers exploit this: they emphasise the **advantages** of an investment at the **beginning and end** of a conversation and mention the **risks in the middle**, so they are less noticed. Its advice: look at all available information objectively and side by side, and pay particular attention to risks the adviser does not emphasise.

## Attention-grabbing shares

Brad Barber and Terrance Odean ("All That Glitters", 2008) tested how attention affects buying. They found that **individual investors are net buyers of attention-grabbing shares**: shares in the news, shares with unusually high trading volume, and shares with extreme one-day returns.

Their explanation: there are thousands of shares to choose from, and investors cannot study them all. So many only consider shares that have **first caught their attention**. When selling, the problem is smaller, because people mostly sell what they already own.

> [!WARNING] Attention is not information
> A share being in the headlines tells you that it is popular right now, not that it is a good investment. By the time news reaches the public, the price often already reflects it (Category L).

## How to counter these effects

- Use a **checklist** for every investment decision, so risks get the same attention as opportunities.
- Look at **long periods**, not just the last year (Category K).
- Decide your **watch list** by criteria (Category W), not by headlines.
- When an adviser presents a product, ask directly: **"What are the main risks, and what does it cost?"** Check the KID (Category H).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Investors overweight information that is easy to remember and underestimate risks that haven't occurred recently (FMA).
> - Recent information gets more weight than older information (recency effect).
> - Some advisers place risks in the middle of a conversation; ask about risks explicitly.
> - Individual investors are net buyers of attention-grabbing shares (Barber and Odean).
> - Use checklists and long-term data to balance what grabs your attention.
