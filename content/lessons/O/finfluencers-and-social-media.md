**Finfluencers** (financial influencers) share money and investing content on platforms such as YouTube, Instagram and TikTok. The FMA acknowledges that for many people, not only young audiences, they have become an **important source of information** on financial topics, reaching many people with a low-threshold, entertaining approach. It also advises keeping a **critical eye**.

## The FMA's three questions

In its January 2025 information on finfluencers, the FMA advises checking, before following any tip:

1. **Who is behind the recommendation?** What relevant **training and experience** does the person have? Especially for **paid workshops or coaching**, question the qualifications.
2. **Do they have a financial interest** in selling the products they recommend, for example through **affiliate marketing** (earning a commission when followers sign up or buy)?
3. **How credible are the promises?** Question risks and promised profits, as with any other investment tip.

> [!WARNING] "Finfluencer" is not a protected title
> The FMA points out that anyone can call themselves a finfluencer, and that there are **fraudulent black sheep** among them. Category T shows how to check whether a person or firm is authorised.

## Why social media amplifies biases

Social media combines several biases from this category:

- **Herding and FOMO** (lesson 4): visible likes, comments and gains create pressure to join in.
- **Availability and attention** (lesson 5): spectacular wins are shared; quiet losses are not.
- **The halo effect** (lesson 6): a likeable, confident presenter makes the content seem trustworthy.
- **Survivorship bias** (Category J): you see the people who succeeded, not the many who tried the same thing and lost.

The SEC staff noted that GameStop's surge in January 2021 coincided with frequent mentions on Reddit and significant mainstream media coverage (Category N).

## Using social media sensibly

- Treat content as **entertainment or a starting point**, not as advice tailored to you.
- Check claims against **official sources**: the company's reports (Category I), regulators, statistics offices (Category W).
- Look for **disclosures** of sponsorships, affiliate links and the presenter's own positions.
- Be most careful with anyone selling **courses, signals or access** to "secret" strategies (Category N).
- Never send money or personal data because of a social media message (Category T).

> [!TIP] A healthy question
> "How does this person make money from my attention?" If the answer is commissions, course fees or sponsorships, take that into account.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Finfluencers are an important information source for many people, but need a critical eye (FMA).
> - Check qualifications, especially for paid workshops and coaching.
> - Ask whether the finfluencer earns money from the products they recommend, for example via affiliate links.
> - "Finfluencer" is not a protected title; there are fraudsters among them.
> - Social media amplifies herding, attention effects, the halo effect and survivorship bias.
