**Overconfidence** means believing your knowledge or abilities are better than they are. In investing it shows up as the belief that you can pick winning shares or time the market, and as trading more than is good for you.

## The evidence from real accounts

Brad Barber and Terrance Odean studied the accounts of **66,465 US households** at a large discount broker from 1991 to 1996 ("Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth", 2000):

- The average household turned over **75%** of its share portfolio every year.
- The average household earned **16.4%** a year, while the market returned **17.9%**.
- The households that traded the **most** earned only **11.4%** a year.

The authors conclude that overconfidence can explain the high trading levels and the resulting poor performance.

## Men, women and trading

In a second study ("Boys Will Be Boys", 2001), using data for more than **35,000 households**, Barber and Odean tested the prediction that more overconfident investors trade more. Citing psychological research that men tend to be more overconfident than women in areas such as finance, they found:

- Men traded **45% more** than women.
- Trading reduced men's net returns by **2.65 percentage points** a year, compared with **1.72 points** for women.

> [!NOTE] It is about behaviour, not gender
> The point of the study is not that one gender invests better. It is that **more trading, driven by overconfidence, costs money**, whoever does it.

## Why overconfidence costs money

1. **Every trade has costs**, and many trades do not improve the portfolio (Category G).
2. **Selling one share to buy another** only helps if the new one does better by more than the costs. In a study of discount brokerage customers ("Do Investors Trade Too Much?", 1999), Terrance Odean found that, on average, the shares investors **bought did worse** than the shares they sold, even before costs.
3. **More activity, more mistakes.** The AMF found that the CFD and forex clients who traded most lost the most (Category N).

## Forms of overconfidence

- **Overestimating knowledge:** "I've read a lot about this company, so I know where its price is going."
- **Illusion of control:** feeling that frequent monitoring and action improve results.
- **Self-attribution:** crediting yourself for gains and blaming bad luck for losses, which prevents learning.

> [!TIP] A simple test
> Keep a record of every buy and sell, with your reason. After a year, compare your actual result with what you would have earned by doing nothing, or by holding a broad index fund. Honest numbers are the best cure for overconfidence.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Overconfidence means overestimating your knowledge and skill.
> - In 66,465 US households, the most active traders earned 11.4% a year while the market returned 17.9%.
> - Men traded 45% more than women in Barber and Odean's data, and trading cut their returns more.
> - Every unnecessary trade adds costs and chances for mistakes.
> - Track your decisions and compare them honestly with a simple alternative.
