## Mental accounting

Richard Thaler, Nobel laureate in 2017, developed the theory of **mental accounting**. The Nobel committee summarises it: people simplify financial decisions by creating **separate accounts in their minds**, focusing on the narrow impact of each individual decision rather than its overall effect.

> [!EXAMPLE] Typical mental accounts
> - "Play money" from a bonus, spent on risky bets, while the same person keeps savings in a low-interest account.
> - Treating a dividend as "free money" to spend, although a dividend reduces the value of the share by about the same amount (Category G).
> - Refusing to sell a losing share because that "account" would close at a loss, while ignoring how the whole portfolio is doing.

Mental accounts are not always bad. Separate pots for separate goals can help planning (Category M). The problem arises when the boxes stop you from seeing the **whole picture**: the overall risk, cost and return of everything you own.

## Home bias

The FMA describes **home bias** as investors' tendency to prefer investments from their home market. Reasons given include high international transaction costs, information gaps, and avoiding exchange rate risk by staying in the same currency area. But, the FMA notes, investors overlook that investments in the home market are **more correlated** with each other than internationally diversified investments. It suggests considering whether sufficient international diversification can **reduce risk** and **improve returns**.

> [!AUSTRIA] Home bias in Austria
> For many people in Austria, their job, home and pension already depend on the Austrian economy. A portfolio of mostly Austrian shares adds to that concentration. Category S looks at world markets and how to access them.

## The halo effect

The FMA explains the **halo effect**: an overall impression, or one strong characteristic, colours the perception of other features. A company with a famous brand, a charismatic CEO or an exciting product can seem to be a good investment in every respect.

The FMA's advice: because of this dazzling effect, **check the source** of an investment idea, and if a recommendation comes with positive descriptions, **look for negative information too**.

> [!EXAMPLE] A great product, an expensive share
> Everyone loves a company's products, so its shares feel safe. But a great company can be a poor investment if the share price already assumes perfection (Category I).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Mental accounting: people judge each decision in its own mental box, not by its overall effect (Thaler).
> - Look at your whole portfolio, not just each position or pot.
> - Home bias: preferring home-market investments, which are more correlated with each other (FMA).
> - Halo effect: one positive feature colours the whole judgement; look for the negatives too.
> - International diversification can reduce the concentration of risk in one economy.
