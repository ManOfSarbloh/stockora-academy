Traditional economics often assumed that people make rational decisions. **Behavioural finance** studies how real people actually behave with money. The Austrian Financial Market Authority (FMA) summarises its central insight: investors are only **partly able to act rationally** and are often subject to errors and distortions of judgement when making investment decisions.

## Two Nobel prizes

- **Daniel Kahneman** (Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences, 2002) showed, together with **Amos Tversky**, how human judgement takes **heuristic shortcuts** that systematically depart from the rules of probability. They formulated **prospect theory** as an alternative to standard theory that better accounts for observed behaviour. The Nobel committee noted that his work inspired a new generation of researchers in economics and finance.
- **Richard Thaler** (Nobel Prize, 2017) showed how **limited rationality**, social preferences and **lack of self-control** systematically affect individual decisions and market outcomes. He developed the theory of **mental accounting** and coined the term **nudge**.

## Heuristics: useful shortcuts that can mislead

A **heuristic** is a mental shortcut. In everyday life it saves time: you do not calculate probabilities before crossing a street. In investing, the same shortcuts can lead to systematic errors, called **biases**.

The FMA lists the most widespread traps for investors:

| Trap | In one sentence | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Loss aversion | Losses hurt more than equal gains please | 2 |
| Herding | Copying what everyone else does | 4 |
| Availability heuristic | Overweighting information that is easy to remember | 5 |
| Recency effect | Overweighting the latest information | 5 |
| Home bias | Preferring investments from your own country | 6 |
| Halo effect | One positive feature colours the whole picture | 6 |

Research adds **overconfidence**, which leads to too much trading (lesson 3).

## Why it matters for your money

Biases are not just curiosities. In a study of 66,465 US households with accounts at a large discount broker in 1991–1996, Brad Barber and Terrance Odean found that the households that **traded the most earned 11.4% a year**, while the market returned **17.9%**. Their conclusion: "trading is hazardous to your wealth". They quote Benjamin Graham: the investor's chief problem, and even his worst enemy, is likely to be himself.

> [!NOTE] Everyone is affected
> Biases are not a sign of low intelligence. Experts and professionals show them too. Knowing about them does not make you immune, but it helps you build rules that protect you from yourself (lesson 8).

> [!TIP] The FMA's advice
> Before you make an investment decision, the FMA recommends becoming aware of these traps, to at least soften serious wrong decisions or ideally avoid them.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Behavioural finance studies how real people decide; investors are only partly rational (FMA).
> - Kahneman and Tversky showed how heuristic shortcuts lead to systematic errors (prospect theory).
> - Thaler showed the effects of limited rationality and lack of self-control, and coined "nudge".
> - The most common traps include loss aversion, herding, availability, recency, home bias, halo effect and overconfidence.
> - The most active traders in Barber and Odean's study earned 11.4% a year while the market returned 17.9%.
