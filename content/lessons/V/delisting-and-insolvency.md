## Delisting

The Wiener Börse defines **delisting** as the removal of a listed security from the exchange. Reasons include:

- a **takeover**, after which a main shareholder no longer needs a listing (lesson 5);
- a **squeeze-out**, after which there are no minority shareholders left (Category R);
- the company's **decision** to leave the exchange, for example to save costs;
- **insolvency** or failure to meet listing requirements.

After a delisting, your shares still exist, but you can **no longer trade them on the exchange**. Selling becomes difficult, and information may become scarcer.

## Protection for shareholders in Austria

> [!AUSTRIA] The delisting offer
> For offers made in connection with ending a share's admission to trading on the Wiener Börse (under § 38 (6) to (8) of the Stock Exchange Act 2018), the Takeover Act (§ 27e ÜbG) applies the rules for **mandatory offers**:
> - The offer must cover **all** shares to be delisted that the bidder does not already hold.
> - The offer document must state that a **delisting is intended**.
> - The price must meet the usual minimum price rules (§ 26 ÜbG) and also be at least the **volume-weighted average price of the last five trading days** before the delisting intention was announced.
> - If that price is **obviously below** the company's actual value, the price must be set appropriately.

This gives shareholders a chance to sell at a regulated minimum price before the listing ends.

## Insolvency

When a company cannot pay its debts, it may enter insolvency proceedings. Investor.gov describes the two main US routes:

- **Reorganisation** (Chapter 11): the company tries to restructure and become profitable again. Common shareholders are **frequently replaced** by new investors as part of the plan.
- **Liquidation** (Chapter 7): the company stops operating; its assets are sold and the money is used to pay debts.

Austria has its own insolvency law, but the principle is the same: **creditors are paid before shareholders**. Shareholders receive something only if money is left after all debts, which is rare (Category A, equity versus debt).

> [!WARNING] Trading suspensions and "cheap" shares
> Around important announcements or capital measures, trading can be suspended, and all orders in the order book are deleted (Wiener Börse). When trading resumes after bad news, the price may have fallen sharply. Shares of insolvent companies can trade at a few cents and swing wildly; buying them is pure speculation.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - After a delisting, shares still exist but can no longer be traded on the exchange.
> - In Austria, a delisting offer must cover all shares and follows mandatory-offer rules.
> - The price must be at least the five-day volume-weighted average before the announcement, and appropriate if that is obviously too low.
> - In insolvency, creditors come first; shareholders often lose everything.
> - Trading suspensions delete open orders; prices can gap sharply afterwards.
