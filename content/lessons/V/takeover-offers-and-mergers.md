Category R explains the legal framework for takeovers in Austria: the 30% threshold for a mandatory offer, the minimum price rules and the squeeze-out. This lesson looks at the same events from your side as a shareholder.

## How a tender offer works

Investor.gov describes a **tender offer** as an active, widespread solicitation by a bidder to buy a substantial percentage of a company's securities. Its key features:

- It is open for a **limited period** (the acceptance period).
- It is made to **each shareholder individually**; each decides whether to sell ("tender") their shares.
- The **terms are fixed**, including the price.
- The price is usually at a **premium** to the current market price, to encourage shareholders to sell.
- It may have conditions, such as a **minimum acceptance** level; if not enough shares are tendered, the bidder may not have to buy any.

## Cash or shares?

| Offer type | What you receive | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash offer** | A fixed amount per share | Is the premium fair compared with the company's value (Category I)? |
| **Share (exchange) offer** | Shares of the bidder at a fixed **exchange ratio** | You become a shareholder of the bidder; the value of the offer moves with the bidder's share price |

> [!EXAMPLE] An exchange ratio
> The bidder offers **0.5 of its own shares** for each Alpenrad share (fictional). The bidder's share trades at €60, so the offer is worth 0.5 × €60 = **€30** today. If the bidder's share falls to €50, the offer is worth only €25.

## Why the market price stays below the offer

After a cash offer of €30 is announced, the target's shares can trade a little **below** €30, for example at €29.40. The gap (here 2%) reflects:

- the **time** until payment;
- the **risk** that the deal fails, for example because conditions such as the minimum acceptance or regulatory approvals are not met. If it fails, the premium can disappear.

You can either **sell on the market now** (certain, slightly lower price) or **tender** and wait (full price, if the deal completes).

## What the target company must tell you

> [!AUSTRIA] Reasoned statements
> In Austria, the target's management board and supervisory board must publish reasoned statements on the offer, including whether they consider the price appropriate (§ 14 ÜbG). The Takeover Commission publishes all takeover offers on its website (takeover.at). Read the offer and the statements before deciding.

## If you don't accept

- You keep your shares, but after a successful takeover there may be **fewer shares in free float**, lower trading volume and wider spreads (Category D).
- The new main shareholder may later pursue a **squeeze-out** (at 90%) or a **delisting** (lesson 7; Category R).

> [!TIP] Check your bank's deadline
> To tender, you instruct your bank. Its deadline can be earlier than the end of the offer's acceptance period (lesson 1).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A tender offer is made to each shareholder, for a limited time, on fixed terms, usually at a premium.
> - In a share offer, the value depends on the bidder's share price via the exchange ratio.
> - The market price usually stays slightly below a cash offer, reflecting time and deal risk.
> - Read the target boards' reasoned statements before deciding.
> - Not accepting can leave you in a less liquid share, and later a squeeze-out or delisting may follow.
