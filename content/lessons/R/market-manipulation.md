## What counts as manipulation

The FMA explains that market manipulation can take several forms. Under Article 12(1)(a) of the **Market Abuse Regulation (MAR)**, it includes transactions or orders that:

- give, or are likely to give, **false or misleading signals** about the supply of, demand for or price of a financial instrument, or
- secure the price at an **abnormal or artificial level**,

unless there are legitimate reasons and the conduct complies with accepted market practice.

It also covers:

- transactions or orders using **fictitious devices** or other forms of **deception**;
- **spreading information, rumours or news** through the media that gives false or misleading signals;
- providing false information or input data for a **benchmark**, or otherwise manipulating its calculation.

## Examples listed in the rules

The FMA points to practices listed in Article 12 MAR, Annex I of MAR and Delegated Regulation (EU) 2016/522, including:

- securing a **dominant position** that fixes prices or creates other unfair trading conditions;
- **buying or selling at the close** of the market so that investors who act on the closing price are misled (Category D explains closing auctions);
- using **access to the media** to give an opinion on a financial instrument and then profiting from the effect on its price.

> [!EXAMPLE] Pump and dump
> A group buys a cheap, thinly traded share, then promotes it with exaggerated or false claims on social media. As others buy, the price rises; the group sells at the top, and the price collapses. This combines misleading signals and the spreading of misleading information. Category T shows how to recognise such schemes.

> [!EXAMPLE] Talking up your own position
> Someone with a large following recommends a share they own without saying so, then sells after followers have pushed the price up. Using media access to give an opinion and then profiting from the price effect is among the practices the rules list. The FMA also advises investors to check whether finfluencers have financial interests in what they recommend (Category O).

## Penalties in Austria

The FMA summarises:

- As an **administrative offence**, the FMA can impose fines of up to **€5 million** or up to **three times the benefit** gained (including a loss avoided), and declare any financial gain forfeited.
- In **criminal proceedings** (§ 164 BörseG 2018), the penalty is **6 months to 5 years** imprisonment.
- **Companies** can be fined up to **€15 million** or **15% of annual net turnover**.

Where courts are competent, the FMA must report a justified suspicion to the public prosecutor.

> [!TIP] Report suspicions
> If you notice suspicious promotions or price movements, you can inform the FMA (lesson 8).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Manipulation includes trades or orders giving false or misleading signals, or setting artificial prices.
> - Spreading false or misleading information or rumours that affect prices is also manipulation.
> - Listed practices include manipulating closing prices and profiting from your own public recommendations.
> - In Austria: fines up to €5 million or three times the benefit, prison of 6 months to 5 years in criminal cases, and company fines up to €15 million or 15% of turnover.
> - Pump-and-dump schemes are a typical form; be sceptical of hyped penny stocks.
