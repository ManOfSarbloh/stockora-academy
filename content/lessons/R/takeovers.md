When an investor or company gains **control** of a listed company, the remaining shareholders face a new situation: decisions will now be made by someone else. Takeover law protects them by giving them the chance to sell at a fair price.

## The Austrian Takeover Commission

The **Übernahmekommission** describes itself as an **independent authority, not bound by instructions**, established at the Vienna Stock Exchange under the Takeover Act (*Übernahmegesetz*, ÜbG). It has twelve part-time members and an office. Its main tasks are to **supervise the entire offer process** and to decide whether a **mandatory offer** must be made. It publishes all takeover offers on its website.

## The mandatory offer: crossing 30%

Under § 22 ÜbG:

- Anyone who acquires a **controlling stake** in a target company must notify the Takeover Commission **without delay**.
- Within **20 trading days** of gaining control, they must announce an **offer for all shares** of the target company.
- A direct controlling stake means a stake carrying **more than 30%** of the voting rights of the permanently voting shares. Indirect control, for example through a holding company, can also count.

> [!EXAMPLE] Why 30% matters
> An investor holds 28% of an Austrian listed company and buys another 3%, reaching 31%. It must now notify the Takeover Commission and, within 20 trading days, announce an offer to buy the shares of all other shareholders. Along the way, its stake crossed the reporting thresholds of 30% (and earlier ones), which must be reported under the major holdings rules (lesson 2).

## A fair minimum price

§ 26 ÜbG sets **minimum price rules** for mandatory offers and voluntary offers to gain control. The price must be at least:

1. the **highest price** the bidder (or anyone acting in concert with it) paid or agreed for these shares in the **12 months** before the offer was announced, and
2. the **volume-weighted average stock exchange price** during the **six months** before the intention to make an offer was announced.

These rules stop a bidder from paying one large shareholder a premium and offering the small shareholders less.

## Accept or not?

When an offer is made, each shareholder decides whether to sell. Consider:

- the offer price compared with the current price and your own view of the company's value (Category I);
- the reasoned statements that the target's management board and supervisory board must publish on the offer (§ 14 ÜbG), including whether the price is appropriate for all shareholders;
- what may happen if you stay: fewer tradable shares, possibly delisting (Category B).

## The squeeze-out

Once a main shareholder holds at least **90%** of the share capital, the Austrian Shareholder Exclusion Act (*Gesellschafter-Ausschlussgesetz*) allows the general meeting, at the main shareholder's request, to resolve that the remaining shareholders' shares are **transferred to the main shareholder** in exchange for **appropriate cash compensation**. Minority shareholders cannot refuse, but they can have the compensation reviewed.

> [!NOTE] A predictable path
> Takeovers often follow a sequence: stake building with major holdings reports, a mandatory or voluntary offer, possibly a squeeze-out and a delisting. The disclosure rules let you follow it step by step.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - The Übernahmekommission, an independent authority at the Vienna Stock Exchange, supervises takeover offers.
> - Gaining more than 30% of the voting rights triggers a mandatory offer for all shares, announced within 20 trading days (§ 22 ÜbG).
> - The price must be at least the bidder's highest price in the last 12 months and the 6-month volume-weighted average price (§ 26 ÜbG).
> - At 90% or more, the main shareholder can squeeze out the rest for appropriate cash compensation.
> - Major holdings reports let investors follow stake building early.
