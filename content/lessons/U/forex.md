## The biggest market of all

**Forex** (FX, foreign exchange) is the market where currencies are exchanged. Most of it is **over the counter (OTC)**: banks and other dealers trade directly with each other and with clients, not on a single exchange.

Every three years, the **Bank for International Settlements (BIS)** surveys central banks around the world about FX trading. Its 2025 survey, covering 52 jurisdictions and more than 1,100 banks and dealers, found:

- Trading reached **$9.6 trillion per day** in April 2025, up 28% from $7.5 trillion in 2022.
- The **US dollar** was on one side of **89.2%** of all trades; the **euro** on one side of **28.9%**, the yen 16.8% and sterling 10.2%. (Each trade involves two currencies, so the shares add up to 200%.)
- About **46%** of trading was between dealers, and **50%** with other financial institutions.
- Sales desks in the **United Kingdom, United States, Singapore and Hong Kong SAR** accounted for **75%** of trading.

Trading includes spot deals, forwards, swaps and options. Cryptocurrencies are not included in the survey's FX figures (BIS).

## Who uses the currency market

- **Companies** pay for imports and receive export earnings in foreign currencies.
- **Investors** buy foreign shares and bonds, and may hedge the currency risk (Category L).
- **Travellers** exchange money.
- **Central banks** manage reserves.
- **Speculators** try to profit from rate changes.

## Currency pairs

Exchange rates are quoted as pairs. The ECB publishes daily reference rates with the euro as the base currency: **USD 1.10** means 1 euro buys 1.10 US dollars, written as **EUR/USD 1.10** (Category L).

> [!EXAMPLE] A rate change
> EUR/USD moves from 1.10 to 1.12. The euro has **strengthened** against the dollar: one euro now buys more dollars. For a euro investor, US dollar assets are now worth slightly fewer euros.

Exchange rates move with interest rates, inflation, trade, economic news and risk sentiment (Category L). Short-term moves are very hard to predict.

## Retail forex trading

Private individuals who "trade forex" usually do so through **CFDs** or similar leveraged products, not by exchanging currencies (Category N).

- Since 2018, EU rules have limited leverage for retail clients to **30:1 for major currency pairs** and **20:1 for other currency pairs** (ESMA; Category N).
- France's regulator **AMF** studied 14,799 active forex and CFD clients over four years: **more than 89% lost money**, with an average loss of **€10,887** (AMF 2014).

> [!WARNING] Leverage and small moves
> At 30:1, a position of €30,000 needs just €1,000 of margin. A 1% move against you costs €300, or **30% of your margin**; a 1.7% move wipes out half of it, the point at which EU rules require the provider to close the position (Category N).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Forex is the world's largest financial market: $9.6 trillion per day in April 2025 (BIS).
> - The US dollar is on one side of almost 9 in 10 trades.
> - Rates are quoted as pairs; the ECB quotes currencies per 1 euro.
> - Retail forex trading is usually done with leveraged CFDs, limited in the EU to 30:1 for major pairs.
> - The AMF found that more than 89% of retail forex and CFD clients lost money over four years.
