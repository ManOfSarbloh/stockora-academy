Property is the largest asset many households own. As an investment, there are three main routes.

## 1. Owning property directly

Buying a flat or house to live in or to rent out:

- **Income:** rent, minus maintenance, vacancies, management and taxes.
- **Large and concentrated:** one building, one location, often most of a household's wealth.
- **Leverage:** most purchases use a mortgage. Borrowing magnifies gains and losses on the money you put in (Category N).
- **Slow and expensive to buy and sell:** agents, notaries, taxes and registration fees, and a sale can take months.

> [!EXAMPLE] Leverage works both ways
> You buy a flat for €300,000 with €60,000 of your own money and a €240,000 mortgage.
> - If the price rises 10% to €330,000, your equity grows from €60,000 to €90,000: **+50%**.
> - If the price falls 10% to €270,000, your equity falls to €30,000: **−50%**, and the mortgage stays the same.

## 2. Open-ended property funds

Property funds pool investors' money to buy many buildings. They spread the risk across properties, but buildings cannot be sold quickly when many investors want their money back.

> [!AUSTRIA] Holding and notice periods
> Under § 11 of Austria's Real Estate Investment Fund Act (ImmoInvFG), an investor who wants to redeem units must:
> - show that the units have been held for **at least 12 months**, and
> - give **irrevocable notice**, with a **12-month notice period**, to redemption dates set at least quarterly.
>
> In exceptional circumstances, payouts can be **temporarily suspended**. The fund rules set the details; check the fund documents before buying.

## 3. REITs and listed property companies

A **REIT** is a company that owns and typically operates income-producing real estate, such as offices, flats, warehouses or hotels (Investor.gov). Publicly traded REITs and property shares trade on exchanges, so you can buy and sell them like any share. The trade-off: their prices move with the stock market day to day. Investor.gov warns that **non-traded REITs** are illiquid, hard to value and often have high up-front fees. Category C covers REITs in detail.

## How property has behaved

In Damodaran's US data for 1928–2025 (lesson 1), real estate returned **4.2% a year** with **6.2%** volatility and **15** losing years; its worst year was **−12.0% in 2008**. These are figures for a broad price series, without leverage. An individual property, bought with a mortgage, can do much better or much worse.

## Comparing the routes

| | Direct property | Open-ended fund | Listed REIT / property shares |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum amount | Very large | Small | Small |
| Diversification | One property | Many properties | Many properties |
| Selling | Months, high costs | Notice periods (Austria: 12 months) | Daily on the exchange |
| Price information | Only when valued or sold | Periodic valuations by the fund | Continuous market prices, can swing like shares |

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Direct property is large, concentrated, leveraged and slow to sell.
> - A mortgage magnifies gains and losses on your own money.
> - Austrian property funds require a 12-month holding period and a 12-month notice period.
> - Listed REITs trade daily but move with the stock market; non-traded REITs are illiquid.
> - Long-run US real estate data shows modest returns and low volatility, without leverage.
