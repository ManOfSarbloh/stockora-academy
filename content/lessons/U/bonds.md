## What a bond is

Investor.gov describes a **bond** as a **debt security, like an IOU**. Borrowers issue bonds to raise money from investors. When you buy a bond, you **lend money** to the issuer, which may be a government, municipality or company. In return, the issuer promises to pay a specified **interest rate** during the bond's life and to **repay the principal** (face value) when the bond **matures**.

> [!EXAMPLE] A simple bond
> You buy a 5-year bond with a face value of €1,000 and a 3% coupon.
> - Each year you receive €30 in interest.
> - After five years you receive €1,000 back, if the issuer can pay.

## Why investors buy bonds

According to Investor.gov, bonds:

- provide a **predictable income stream**, typically on a regular schedule;
- return the **entire principal** if held to maturity (and the issuer pays), helping to preserve capital;
- can help **offset** the volatility of share holdings.

## Types of bonds

- **Government bonds:** issued by states. Investor.gov describes US Treasuries as backed by the full faith and credit of the US government.
- **Corporate bonds:** issued by companies. **Investment-grade** bonds have higher credit ratings and lower credit risk; **high-yield** bonds have lower ratings, higher credit risk and therefore higher interest rates.

> [!AUSTRIA] How Austria borrows
> The Republic of Austria issues federal bonds (*Bundesanleihen*), interest-bearing debt securities of the federal government, through the **Austrian Treasury (OeBFA)** (BMF). The OeBFA describes itself as the debt management office of the Republic, responsible for issuing government debt and managing the federal debt portfolio. Since April 2024, it has also offered **Bundesschatz**, an online savings product of the Republic for private individuals.

## Why bond prices move

Investor.gov explains that if you hold a bond to maturity you receive the face value plus interest; if you sell before maturity, it may be worth more or less than the face value. Prices change because of:

- **Interest rates:** when market rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons lose value, and vice versa (Category L).
- **Credit quality:** if the issuer's finances worsen, the risk of default rises and the bond's price falls.
- **Time to maturity:** long-term bonds react more strongly to rate changes (Category L).

## Main risks

| Risk | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Interest rate risk** | Prices fall when rates rise; in 2022, 10-year US Treasury bonds lost 17.8% (lesson 1). |
| **Credit (default) risk** | The issuer may not pay interest or principal in full (Category R, bail-in for bank bonds). |
| **Inflation risk** | Fixed payments lose purchasing power (Category L). |
| **Liquidity risk** | There may be no buyer when you want to sell before maturity (Category P). |
| **Call risk** | The issuer may repay early, for example when rates fall, so you have to reinvest at lower rates. |

## Bonds and taxes in Austria

Interest from bonds held at an Austrian bank is subject to KESt at **27.5%**, while interest on bank deposits is taxed at 25% (BMF; Category Q).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A bond is a loan to an issuer that pays interest and repays the face value at maturity.
> - Government and investment-grade bonds have lower credit risk than high-yield bonds.
> - Bond prices fall when interest rates rise; long bonds react most.
> - Austria borrows through the OeBFA, which also offers the retail product Bundesschatz.
> - Bond interest is taxed at 27.5% in Austria; bank deposit interest at 25%.
