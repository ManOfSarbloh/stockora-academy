## For a one-off investment, order does not matter

If you invest €10,000 once and never add or withdraw, the final amount depends only on the returns, not their order. +20% then −20% gives the same result as −20% then +20%: 10,000 × 1.2 × 0.8 = 10,000 × 0.8 × 1.2 = **€9,600**.

## With withdrawals, order matters a lot

> [!EXAMPLE] Two retirees, same returns, different order
> Each starts with **€100,000** and withdraws **€10,000** at the start of every year for three years. The yearly returns are −30%, +10% and +30%, in different order.
>
> | | Retiree A: bad year first | Retiree B: bad year last |
> |---|---:|---:|
> | Year 1 | (100,000 − 10,000) × 0.70 = 63,000 | (100,000 − 10,000) × 1.30 = 117,000 |
> | Year 2 | (63,000 − 10,000) × 1.10 = 58,300 | (117,000 − 10,000) × 1.10 = 117,700 |
> | Year 3 | (58,300 − 10,000) × 1.30 = **62,790** | (117,700 − 10,000) × 0.70 = **75,390** |
>
> Same three returns, same withdrawals, but A ends with **€12,600 less**, because A sold investments to fund withdrawals right after the crash, when prices were low.

This is **sequence-of-returns risk**: the risk that bad years arrive at the wrong time, typically just before or just after you start withdrawing money.

## For savers, the opposite

Someone who invests regularly benefits from low prices **early** (they buy more units cheaply, Category M) and is hurt most by a crash **just before** they need the money, when the pot is largest.

> [!EXAMPLE] A crash at the worst moment
> In Damodaran's US data, the S&P 500 lost 36.6% in 2008. For a saver with €300,000 in shares who planned to start withdrawing in 2009, that meant about **€110,000** less, just when the money was needed. For someone who started saving in 2008, the same year meant buying cheaply.

## How investors manage sequence risk

- **Reduce risk as the goal approaches.** The SEC notes that most people investing for retirement hold fewer shares and more bonds and cash as retirement approaches (glide paths, Category M).
- **Keep a cash buffer** covering one to several years of planned withdrawals, so you do not have to sell shares after a crash.
- **Be flexible:** withdrawing less after bad years reduces the damage.
- **Avoid leverage** near the time you need the money.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - For a single investment with no additions or withdrawals, the order of returns does not change the result.
> - With regular withdrawals, bad years early can permanently reduce the pot: sequence-of-returns risk.
> - For savers, a crash just before the goal date hurts most.
> - Glide paths, cash buffers and flexible withdrawals reduce sequence risk.
