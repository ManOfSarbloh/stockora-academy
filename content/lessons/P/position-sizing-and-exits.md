Which share you buy matters. **How much** you buy often matters more. Position sizing decides how much damage one wrong decision can do.

## Concentration limits

The simplest rule caps the share of your portfolio in any single company:

> [!EXAMPLE] A 5% cap
> Portfolio €40,000. Rule: no single share above 5%, i.e. **€2,000**.
> If that company fails completely, as Wirecard did in 2020 (ESMA; Category I), you lose 5% of your portfolio, painful but survivable.
> Without the rule, with €20,000 in that one share, you would lose half.

The SEC's guide notes that four or five shares are not a diversified portfolio (Category M). Broad funds remove most single-company risk in one step.

## Sizing by the amount at risk

Active investors sometimes size positions by how much they are prepared to lose **if they are wrong**, using an exit level decided in advance.

**Position size = amount you accept to lose ÷ (buy price − exit price) per share**

> [!EXAMPLE] Risk 1% of the account
> Account: €20,000. You decide that one idea may cost at most 1%, i.e. **€200**.
> You buy at €50 and plan to exit at €45: a risk of €5 per share.
> Position size: 200 ÷ 5 = **40 shares** (€2,000).
>
> If the exit level were €48 (risk €2 per share), you could buy 100 shares. A wider exit means a smaller position.

The percentage is a personal choice, not a rule from any regulator. The point is that the maximum loss is decided **before** buying, in calm conditions.

## Exit rules and their limits

A **stop-loss order** can implement an exit level automatically: the FMA describes how it sends an order once the price falls to the stop price, and the position is sold at the next executable price. But:

- Investor.gov warns that the stop price is **not a guaranteed** selling price. If the price **gaps** below it, the sale can be much worse (Category F).
- A **stop-limit** order may **not execute at all** in a fast fall.
- Stops on long-term investments in broad funds can sell you out at the bottom of a temporary fall, the opposite of the long-term plan (Category M).

> [!EXAMPLE] The gap problem
> Your stop is at €45. Overnight, the company issues a profit warning and the share opens at **€38**. Your stop triggers and sells at about €38. Your planned €5 risk per share became **€12**, and your €200 risk became **€480**.

## Other exit rules

- **Time-based:** review a position after a set period; if the reason you bought no longer holds, sell.
- **Thesis-based:** write down why you bought (Category O). If that reason is invalidated, sell, regardless of the price.
- **Rebalancing:** trim positions that have grown far above your limits (Category M).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Position size decides how much a single mistake can cost.
> - A cap per company (for example 5%) limits the damage of a total failure.
> - Size = accepted loss ÷ risk per share, using an exit level decided in advance.
> - Stop prices are triggers, not guarantees; gaps can cause much larger losses.
> - Exit rules based on your original reasons often work better for long-term investors than tight stops.
