Another way to sort stocks is by the size of the company, measured by its **market capitalisation** (share price × number of shares). You will often see the labels large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap and microcap.

## Size classes

| Label | Rough meaning |
|---|---|
| **Large cap** | The biggest listed companies, often household names |
| **Mid cap** | Medium-sized companies |
| **Small cap** | Smaller listed companies |
| **Microcap** | Very small companies; in the US, often those worth less than about $250–300 million |
| **Nanocap** | The very smallest; in the US sometimes used for companies worth less than $50 million |

> [!NOTE]
> There is **no single official definition** of these size classes. Index providers and fund companies each set their own thresholds, and they change as markets grow. The microcap and nanocap figures above are the ones used on the SEC's investor education site.

## Why size matters

- **Large caps** tend to be more established, more widely followed by analysts, and easier to trade in large amounts.
- **Small caps** can grow faster, but their prices tend to swing more, and less information is available.
- **Microcaps** carry special risks, which the SEC highlights:
  - **Limited information:** current, publicly available financial information about many microcap companies is lacking, so it is hard to check the facts.
  - **Low liquidity:** few shares trade, so they can be hard to sell, and even small trades can move the price a lot.
  - **Fraud:** microcaps are a favourite target of "pump-and-dump" schemes (Category T).

## Penny stocks

The very lowest-priced stocks are called **penny stocks**. According to the SEC, such companies may have little or no earnings, do not pay dividends, and are highly speculative.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A share costs **€0.05**. Someone tells you: "If it just goes to €0.10, you double your money!"
>
> True, but a low share price says nothing about value (remember: price × number of shares = market cap). A company with 2 billion shares at €0.05 is valued at €100 million, and it may earn nothing at all. Cheap per share is not the same as cheap.

## Meme stocks

In January 2021, the share prices of several companies rose dramatically as excited individual investors piled in, coordinating on social media. These became known as **meme stocks**; the most famous was GameStop. According to the SEC, these stocks shared large price moves, large swings in trading volume, high short interest, frequent mentions on Reddit and heavy media coverage. As prices then fell, many late buyers lost a lot of money. (The GameStop short squeeze is a case study in Category N.)

> [!WARNING]
> When a stock's price is driven mainly by online excitement rather than by the business, it can collapse just as fast as it rose. If a share is being promoted more heavily than the company's products, that is a warning sign.

> [!MISTAKE] "Small, cheap shares have more room to grow"
> A low share price or a small company does not make big gains more likely, only bigger swings in both directions. Many microcaps and penny stocks never grow, and some are frauds.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Size classes (large, mid, small, micro, nano cap) are based on market capitalisation, with no single official threshold.
> - Microcaps often come with little information, low liquidity and a higher fraud risk.
> - Penny stocks are highly speculative; a low price per share does not mean cheap.
> - Meme stocks are driven by social-media hype and can collapse as quickly as they rise.
