Buying an office tower or a shopping centre is far beyond most people's budget. A **real estate investment trust (REIT)** makes it possible to own a small slice of many large properties, in the form of shares.

## What a REIT is

According to the SEC, a REIT is a company that owns, and typically operates, **income-producing real estate** or related assets. That can include office buildings, shopping malls, apartments, hotels, warehouses, self-storage facilities, and mortgages or loans. Unlike other real estate companies, a REIT generally does not develop properties to sell them; it buys and operates them to earn income.

REITs are known for paying out most of their income to shareholders: the SEC notes that most REITs pay out at least 100 percent of their taxable income. That makes them popular with investors looking for regular dividends. In the US, those dividends are generally taxed as ordinary income.

> [!NOTE]
> "REIT" is originally a US legal structure. Many other countries have similar listed property companies or their own REIT-like regimes, with different rules and tax treatment. Check the rules of the country concerned.

## Listed, non-traded and private REITs

| Type | Traded on an exchange? | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Publicly traded REIT** | Yes | Can be bought and sold easily; prices are visible at all times |
| **Non-traded REIT** | No | Registered with the SEC, but no market price; it can take many years before you can sell, and upfront fees can be high |
| **Private REIT** | No | Not registered, hard to value and to sell |

> [!WARNING]
> The SEC warns that with **non-traded REITs**, you may have to wait more than 10 years for a chance to get your money back, redemption programs can be limited or stopped, and upfront fees of around 9–10% significantly reduce the value of the investment.

## Listed REITs and the property market

A listed REIT's share price moves every trading day, unlike the value of a building, which is only estimated now and then. Its price reacts to interest rates, the economy and investor mood, so it can be more volatile than property values appear to be.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You want exposure to property with **€2,000**. Buying a flat is impossible with that amount, but you could buy shares of a listed REIT that owns hundreds of buildings. You get diversification across many properties, can sell any trading day, and receive a share of the rent income as dividends, but you also accept daily price swings.

> [!MISTAKE] "REITs are as safe as owning a home"
> A REIT is a company with shares: its price can fall sharply, especially when interest rates rise or a property type falls out of favour (for example offices or shopping malls).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A REIT owns and operates income-producing real estate or related assets.
> - Most REITs pay out most of their income as dividends.
> - Listed REITs are easy to trade; non-traded REITs can lock your money up for years and charge high fees.
> - Listed REIT prices can be volatile and react strongly to interest rates.
