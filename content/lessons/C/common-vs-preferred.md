Not all shares of a company are the same. The two main kinds are **common shares** (also called ordinary shares) and **preferred shares** (also called preference shares). They split the rights of ownership differently.

## Common shares

**Common shares** are the standard kind. When people say "I bought shares", they almost always mean common shares. They usually give you:

- a **vote** at the general meeting;
- a share in **dividends**, if the company pays them;
- the **residual claim**: whatever is left after everyone else is paid.

## Preferred shares

**Preferred shares** trade some rights for others. Typically:

- **No vote** (or only a limited one).
- **Dividends first:** preferred shareholders receive their dividend before common shareholders get anything.
- **Earlier in the queue in a liquidation:** if the company is wound up, preferred shareholders are paid after lenders but before common shareholders.

| | Common shares | Preferred shares |
|---|---|---|
| Vote at the general meeting | Usually yes | Usually no |
| Dividends | Only after preferred shareholders | Paid first |
| If the company is liquidated | Last in line | After lenders, before common shares |
| Upside if the company does very well | Full | Often more limited |

> [!AUSTRIA]
> In Austria, a preferred share is called a **Vorzugsaktie** (often abbreviated "Vz"). Under § 12a of the Aktiengesetz, a company may issue preferred shares **without voting rights** only up to **one third of its share capital**. If the preferred dividend is not paid in full in one year and the arrears are not made up together with the next year's full preferred dividend, the preferred shareholders get voting rights until the arrears are paid.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company has made a profit and decides to distribute **€1,000,000**. It has 100,000 preferred shares with a preferred dividend of **€2** each, and 400,000 common shares.
>
> - Preferred shareholders first: 100,000 × €2 = **€200,000**
> - Left for common shareholders: €1,000,000 − €200,000 = €800,000, which is **€2 per common share**
>
> In a bad year with only €150,000 to distribute, the preferred shareholders would take all of it and common shareholders would get nothing.

## Preferred shares in start-ups

In venture capital rounds (Category B), investors usually receive preferred shares too, but with a much richer set of rights, such as a liquidation preference and special votes on major decisions. "Preferred" can therefore mean quite different things depending on the company.

> [!MISTAKE] "Preferred shares are simply better"
> They are *different*, not better. You give up your vote and often some of the upside in exchange for priority. Whether that suits you depends on what you want, and the exact terms differ from company to company.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Common (ordinary) shares are the standard kind: vote, dividends after preferred shares, last in line.
> - Preferred shares usually have no vote but get dividends first and rank ahead of common shares in a liquidation.
> - In Austria, non-voting preferred shares (Vorzugsaktien) may make up at most one third of the share capital.
> - Always check which share class you are buying.
