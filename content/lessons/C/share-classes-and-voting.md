In the simplest case, every share of a company is identical: one share, one vote, the same dividend. But some companies create **several classes** of shares, often called **Class A** and **Class B** (or other letters), with different rights.

## What can differ between classes

- **Voting rights:** one class may have one vote per share, another ten votes, another none.
- **Dividends:** one class may get a higher or earlier dividend (as with preferred shares).
- **Tradability:** one class may be listed on an exchange, another held privately by founders.

The letters themselves mean nothing: "Class A" has more votes at one company and fewer at another. Only the company's articles and its prospectus tell you what each class gets.

## Dual-class structures

The most debated case is a **dual-class structure**: founders and insiders hold shares with **more votes per share** than the shares sold to the public.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company has **100 million** shares:
>
> - **90 million Class A shares** owned by the public, **1 vote** each = 90 million votes
> - **10 million Class B shares** owned by the founder, **10 votes** each = 100 million votes
>
> If both classes have the same rights to dividends, the founder owns only **10%** of the economic value, but controls 100 ÷ 190 ≈ **53%** of the votes, a majority.

## Why it is controversial

- **For:** supporters argue that visionary founders can focus on the long term without being pushed around by short-term shareholders.
- **Against:** critics, including the SEC's Investor Advocate in a 2019 speech, argue that it lets insiders control a company with a small stake, reduces accountability, and leaves public shareholders with little say if things go wrong.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> The Vienna Stock Exchange's top segment, the **prime market**, basically admits only **common stock**. Preferred shares, for example, are not included in it.

> [!MISTAKE] "I own shares, so I have a say"
> In a dual-class company you may own a meaningful part of the economic value but almost none of the voting power. Check the share classes in the prospectus or annual report before assuming you have influence.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Companies can issue several share classes with different votes, dividends or tradability.
> - Class letters have no standard meaning; read the company's documents.
> - Dual-class structures let founders control a company with a minority of the shares.
> - They are debated: long-term focus versus weaker accountability to public shareholders.
