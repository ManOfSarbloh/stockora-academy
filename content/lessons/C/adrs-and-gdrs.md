Suppose a company's shares are listed only in its home country, in its home currency. Investors elsewhere might want to own it, but buying shares on a foreign exchange can be complicated. **Depositary receipts** are one solution.

## How an ADR works

An **American Depositary Receipt (ADR)** is a certificate traded in the United States that represents shares of a **non-US company**. The structure works like this:

1. A US **depositary bank** holds actual shares of the foreign company in custody.
2. The bank issues receipts (ADRs, also called American Depositary Shares, ADSs) that represent those shares.
3. The ADRs trade in the US in **US dollars**, so US investors do not have to deal in a foreign currency themselves.

One ADR does not have to equal one share: a fixed ratio is set, for example one ADR = two shares or one ADR = one tenth of a share, often so that the price looks similar to typical US share prices.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company's shares trade at the equivalent of **$4** in its home market. The depositary bank sets a ratio of **1 ADR = 10 shares**.
>
> One ADR should therefore trade at around 10 × $4 = **$40**, apart from small differences due to fees, exchange rates and timing.

## Sponsored and unsponsored ADRs

- **Sponsored:** the foreign company itself works with the depositary bank, which handles things like passing on dividends and shareholder communications.
- **Unsponsored:** the ADR is set up without the company's cooperation, for example by a broker-dealer that wants to create a US trading market. Because the company is not involved, services such as forwarding shareholder communications may not be arranged.

## GDRs

**Global depositary receipts (GDRs)** follow the same idea, but are generally aimed at investors in more than one country rather than just the US.

## Risks and costs to keep in mind

- **Currency risk** stays: the ADR is priced in dollars, but the underlying company earns its money in its own currency. If that currency falls against the dollar, the ADR price falls too (Category L).
- **Fees:** depositary banks charge ADR holders custody fees (also called depositary services fees) for their work.
- **Taxes:** dividends may be taxed in the company's home country first (Category Q).
- **Rights:** your voting and other rights depend on the depositary agreement.

> [!MISTAKE] "An ADR is a US company"
> An ADR trades in the US, but the business behind it is foreign, with foreign accounting rules, a foreign economy and a foreign currency.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - An ADR represents shares of a non-US company held by a US depositary bank, traded in US dollars.
> - A ratio links ADRs to the underlying shares.
> - Sponsored ADRs involve the company; unsponsored ones are set up without its cooperation.
> - GDRs work similarly for investors in several countries.
> - Currency risk, fees and foreign taxes remain.
