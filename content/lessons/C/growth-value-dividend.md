Besides legal types (common, preferred), investors use everyday labels to describe what kind of company a stock represents and what they hope to get from it. You will see these words everywhere in financial news.

## The main styles

| Label | Typical features | What investors hope for |
|---|---|---|
| **Growth stock** | Earnings growing faster than the market average; rarely pays dividends; reinvests profits | Rising share price (capital appreciation) |
| **Value stock** | Low price compared with earnings (low P/E); may be out of favour | That the market has overreacted and the price recovers |
| **Income / dividend stock** | Pays dividends consistently, often an established business | Regular income |
| **Blue chip** | Large, well-known company with a long, solid history; generally pays dividends | Stability and reliability |

These descriptions follow the SEC's investor education site, which uses a young technology company as a typical growth stock and an established utility company as a typical income stock.

A stock can carry more than one label: a large blue chip can also be a dividend stock, and a value stock can be either.

## Cyclical and defensive stocks

Another common way to sort stocks is by how much their business depends on the state of the economy:

- **Cyclical stocks:** companies whose sales and profits rise strongly in good times and fall in bad times, because customers can postpone their purchases. Typical examples are car makers, airlines, building materials or luxury goods.
- **Defensive stocks:** companies whose products people keep buying even in a recession, such as food, household products, healthcare or utilities. Their profits tend to be steadier.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> In a recession, people may delay buying a new car for two years, but they still buy toothpaste and pay their electricity bill.
>
> So a car maker's profit might fall sharply (cyclical), while a toothpaste maker's profit barely changes (defensive).

## Styles are not grades

> [!MISTAKE] "Value stocks are bargains and growth stocks are overpriced" (or the other way round)
> A low price compared with earnings can mean a bargain, or it can mean the business is in real trouble. A high price can mean the market is too optimistic, or that the company really will grow fast for years. The labels describe; they do not judge. Which style does better changes over time and cannot be predicted reliably.

Style labels matter later in your learning: many funds and indexes are built around them ("value ETF", "dividend index"), which is covered in Category H. How to measure value (P/E and other ratios) is covered in Category I.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Growth stocks reinvest to grow fast and rarely pay dividends; value stocks look cheap compared with earnings.
> - Income stocks pay steady dividends; blue chips are large, established companies.
> - Cyclical stocks swing with the economy; defensive stocks are steadier.
> - These are descriptions, not quality ratings, and a stock can fit several labels.
