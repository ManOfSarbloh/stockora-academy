Around ordinary shares, a whole family of related securities exists. Some can **turn into** shares, some give the **right to buy** shares, and some only **track** a share price. They often look similar in a broker's search results, but they work very differently.

## Convertible securities

A **convertible security** is usually a bond or a preferred share that can be **converted** into a different security, typically the company's common shares.

- In most cases, the **holder** decides whether and when to convert; sometimes the **company** decides.
- Until conversion, a convertible bond pays interest like a normal bond.
- If the share price rises a lot, converting can be worth more than getting the bond's money back.

> [!WARNING]
> The SEC warns about convertibles whose conversion terms depend on the market price at the time of conversion. If the share price falls, more shares are issued on conversion, which can push the price down further and dilute existing shareholders heavily. Such deals are sometimes called "toxic" or "death spiral" convertibles.

## Warrants

A **warrant** (German: Optionsschein) gives the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset, such as a share, at a price fixed in advance, within or at the end of a set period. Like options (Category N), warrants can move far more than the share itself and can **expire worthless**. Warrants issued by a company on its own shares can lead to new shares being created when they are exercised; warrants issued by banks are bets on the price.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A warrant gives the right to buy one share at **€50** until the end of next year. The share now trades at **€45**.
>
> - If the share rises to **€60** before expiry, the right to buy at €50 is worth about **€10**.
> - If the share stays below €50 until expiry, nobody would use the right, and the warrant expires **worthless**.

## Subscription rights

When a company issues new shares in a capital increase, existing shareholders often receive **subscription rights** (German: Bezugsrechte): the right to buy the new shares before anyone else, in proportion to what they already own. This protects them from dilution. Subscription rights can often be traded, so shareholders who do not want to buy can sell their rights instead (Category V).

## Certificates

**Certificates** (German: Zertifikate), sometimes called structured products or tracker certificates, are issued by many Austrian banks. According to the FMA, a certificate is a **bearer bond**: by buying it, you **lend money to the issuer**, usually a bank. How much you get back depends on the performance of an underlying asset, such as a share or an index. You do not own the underlying asset itself.

> [!WARNING]
> Because a certificate is a debt of the issuer, you carry the **issuer's credit risk**: if the issuing bank fails, you can lose money even if the underlying share has done well. Issuers must provide a key information document (KID) that shows a risk indicator (1 to 7), costs and performance scenarios. The FMA's "Zertifikate Lupe" tool lets you compare certificates on the Austrian market.

> [!MISTAKE] "This certificate on a share is the same as owning the share"
> It is not. With a share, you are a part-owner of the company. With a certificate, you are a lender to a bank, with a payout linked to the share. You usually have no vote and no dividend, you carry the bank's risk, and costs are built in.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Convertibles are bonds or preferred shares that can be turned into common shares; some convertible structures can dilute shareholders heavily.
> - Warrants give the right to buy or sell at a fixed price and can expire worthless.
> - Subscription rights let existing shareholders buy new shares first and protect against dilution.
> - Certificates are bank bonds whose payout tracks an underlying asset; you carry the issuer's credit risk.
