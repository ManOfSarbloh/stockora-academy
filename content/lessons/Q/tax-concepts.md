> [!VERIFY] Tax rules change
> This category is a simplified educational overview, mainly for private individuals resident in Austria who hold investments as private assets. Tax law changes, and individual situations differ. Check the current rules with the Federal Ministry of Finance (BMF), your bank or a tax adviser before you act.

## Two kinds of investment income

Most countries distinguish:

- **Current income:** interest and dividends paid to you.
- **Gains from selling:** the difference between what you sold for and what you paid.

Austria taxes both as **income from capital assets** (*Einkünfte aus Kapitalvermögen*). The BMF lists, among others, income from providing capital (such as savings interest and dividends), from **selling** capital assets ("realised increases in value"), from derivatives and from cryptocurrencies.

## Realised and unrealised

- A gain is **unrealised** while you still hold the investment: it exists only on paper.
- It becomes **realised** when you sell (or redeem) it.

In Austria, the BMF describes the taxable gain on a sale as the **difference between the sale proceeds and the acquisition cost**. As a rule, no tax is due while you simply hold a share. Some events count as a sale even without one, for example certain transfers to another person's account or moving abroad (lesson 3).

> [!EXAMPLE] Paper gain and real gain
> You buy a share for €1,000. A year later it is worth €1,300: an **unrealised** gain of €300. If you sell for €1,300, the €300 becomes **realised** and taxable. If you hold on and the price falls back to €1,100, only €100 would be realised when you sell.

## Withholding or tax return?

There are two ways tax can be collected:

- **At source (withholding):** a bank or company deducts the tax before paying you. In Austria this is the **capital gains tax (KESt)**, withheld by a domestic bank or paying agent (lesson 2).
- **Through your tax return (assessment):** you declare the income yourself, for example when your securities are held by a **foreign** broker (lesson 6).

## Residence and double taxation

Countries usually tax their **residents** on worldwide income, and may also tax income that **arises** in their territory. A dividend from a US company paid to an Austrian resident could therefore be taxed twice: in the US (withholding tax) and in Austria.

**Double taxation agreements (DTAs)** between countries limit this. The BMF explains that foreign tax can be **credited** against Austrian tax according to the relevant DTA (lesson 4).

## Costs and taxes

In many countries, investment costs reduce taxable income. **Not in Austria** for income taxed at the special rates: the BMF states that expenses such as financing costs, transaction costs and custody fees **cannot be deducted**. Purchase fees are not part of the acquisition cost either (lesson 3).

> [!MISTAKE] "No tax until I take the money out of my account"
> Selling a share and keeping the money in your account is still a sale. The gain is realised when you sell, not when you withdraw the cash.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Investment income consists of current income (interest, dividends) and gains from selling.
> - Gains are generally taxed when realised, i.e. when you sell; some events are treated like a sale.
> - Tax is collected either at source (withholding) or through a tax return.
> - Double taxation agreements limit taxation of the same income by two countries.
> - In Austria, costs such as fees and custody charges cannot be deducted from income taxed at the special rates.
