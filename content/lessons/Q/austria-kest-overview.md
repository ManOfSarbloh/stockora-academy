> [!VERIFY] Tax rules change
> This is a simplified educational overview for private individuals resident in Austria who hold investments as private assets. Rates and rules change. Check the current rules with the Federal Ministry of Finance (BMF), your bank or a tax adviser before acting.

## The special tax rates

According to § 27a EStG and the BMF, most income from capital assets is taxed at a **special rate**, separately from your other income, so it does **not push up** the progressive income tax rate on your salary:

| Income | Special rate |
|---|---:|
| Interest on savings accounts and current accounts at banks | **25%** |
| Most other capital income: dividends, interest on bonds, gains from selling shares, funds and securitised derivatives | **27.5%** |

> [!EXAMPLE] A dividend from an Austrian company
> You receive a gross dividend of **€100** from an Austrian stock corporation. **€27.50** KESt is withheld; **€72.50** is paid to you. Nothing more needs to be declared for this dividend.

## KESt: tax withheld at source

The BMF explains that if a **domestic** custodian or paying agent, such as an Austrian bank, is involved, the tax is usually collected by **deduction**: the bank withholds the **capital gains tax (Kapitalertragsteuer, KESt)** and pays it to the tax office. For Austrian dividends, the distributing company withholds it.

For most such income, withholding **settles** the income tax (*Endbesteuerung*): you do not need to include it in your tax return. According to the BMF this applies in particular to:

- interest on bank deposits (25%) and on bonds (27.5%);
- dividends from shares (27.5%);
- gains from selling shares and securitised derivatives (27.5%).

Since 1 April 2012, gains from selling capital assets have also been subject to KESt (for the details on older holdings, see lesson 3).

## When you must declare income yourself

If no KESt is deducted, the income must be included in your tax return, still at the special rate. The BMF names in particular:

- interest from savings at a **foreign** bank;
- gains on investments held in a **foreign securities account** (lesson 6);
- the sale of a share in a limited company (GmbH), which is not held in a securities account.

Some capital income, such as private loans or genuine silent partnerships, is always taxed at the **normal progressive rate** instead.

## The option for low incomes (Regelbesteuerungsoption)

If your total income is low, the normal progressive tax rate on all your income may be lower than 27.5% on the capital income. The BMF explains that you can then apply, in your tax return, to have your capital income taxed at the **normal rate** instead (§ 27a Abs. 5 EStG). The option applies only to **all** your capital income together, and special rules apply in some cases, for example for partners of sole earners.

## What cannot be deducted

The BMF emphasises that for income taxed at the special rates, **expenses cannot be deducted**, not even if you use the option for low incomes. Examples: financing costs, transaction costs of the seller, custody fees, travel costs to a general meeting.

> [!MISTAKE] Thinking 27.5% applies to everything
> Bank deposit interest is taxed at 25%, and some capital income (such as private loans) at the normal progressive rate. Cryptocurrencies have been included in capital income at 27.5% since 1 March 2022 (lesson 7).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Most capital income in Austria is taxed at a special rate of 27.5%; bank deposit interest at 25%.
> - A domestic bank or company usually withholds the tax as KESt, which generally settles it.
> - Without KESt withholding (for example a foreign account), you must declare the income in your tax return.
> - Low earners can apply to have all capital income taxed at the normal progressive rate.
> - Costs such as custody and transaction fees are not deductible.
