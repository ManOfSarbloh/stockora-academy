Austrian investors can buy shares from almost anywhere. The question is **how**, because the route affects costs, currency, trading hours and taxes.

## 1. Directly on the foreign exchange

Many brokers can route your order to exchanges such as New York, London or Tokyo.

- **Pros:** trading in the main market with its full liquidity.
- **Cons:** possibly higher fees and currency conversion costs; trading only during that market's hours (see the [Trading Times tool](/times) and Category E).

## 2. Foreign shares listed in Vienna or elsewhere in Europe

Many foreign shares also trade on European venues. On the Vienna Stock Exchange, the **global market** segment trades shares that are in the Vienna MTF **and listed on at least one other stock exchange**, with at least one participant assuming **market making** obligations (Wiener Börse).

- **Pros:** trading in euros during European hours.
- **Cons:** liquidity and spreads can be worse than on the home exchange, especially when that market is closed (Category D).

## 3. Depositary receipts

**ADRs** and **GDRs** are certificates representing foreign shares, traded on another market, often in US dollars (Category C). They give access to companies that are not directly available, but add currency risk and possibly fees.

## 4. Funds and ETFs

A fund or ETF can hold hundreds or thousands of foreign shares in one product (Category H), for example a global, regional or single-country fund.

- **Pros:** diversification, simple access, often low cost.
- **Cons:** you do not choose individual companies; fund costs and tax rules for funds apply (Category Q).

> [!EXAMPLE] Special access: China's A-shares
> Some markets limit foreign access. Mainland Chinese shares (A-shares) can be traded by international investors through the **Stock Connect** programmes between Hong Kong and Shanghai and Shenzhen, run with Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing (HKEX). HKEX publishes the list of eligible securities.

## What changes when you go abroad

| Factor | What to consider |
|---|---|
| **Currency** | Returns in euros depend on the exchange rate (Category L). |
| **Trading hours** | Different time zones and holidays (Category E, [Trading Times tool](/times)). |
| **Costs** | Foreign exchange fees, higher commissions, possibly foreign transaction taxes. |
| **Taxes** | Foreign withholding tax on dividends; Austrian banks credit up to 15% (BMF; Category Q). |
| **Information** | Reports in other languages and accounting standards (Category I). |
| **Investor protection** | Rules differ by country; emerging markets can have weaker minority shareholder protection (lesson 3). |

> [!TIP] For most beginners
> Broad international funds are usually the simplest way to diversify across countries (Category M). Buying individual foreign shares adds complexity and costs.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - You can buy foreign shares directly abroad, on European venues, as depositary receipts or through funds.
> - The Vienna global market segment trades foreign shares listed elsewhere, with market makers.
> - Currency, trading hours, costs, taxes and investor protection all change when you invest abroad.
> - Some markets, like mainland China, have special access routes such as Stock Connect.
> - Broad international funds are the simplest route for most beginners.
