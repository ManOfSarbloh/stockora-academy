**Emerging markets** are countries whose economies and capital markets are developing towards the standards of developed markets. **Frontier markets** are smaller and less accessible still. Investors are drawn by growth potential and diversification, but the risks are different from those at home.

## What the classification criteria tell you

The criteria index providers use to separate developed from emerging markets (lesson 1) are, in effect, a list of risks. FTSE Russell's Quality of Markets matrix checks, among other things:

- whether **regulators actively monitor** the market;
- **fair treatment of minority shareholders**;
- **foreign ownership restrictions**;
- restrictions on **investing and repatriating capital**;
- the market's **creditworthiness** and the country's income level.

MSCI's framework similarly weighs **market accessibility** as experienced by international institutional investors, along with size and liquidity. A market that falls short on such criteria can even be moved to MSCI's **standalone** category after a **severe deterioration** in accessibility.

## The main risks

| Risk | What can happen |
|---|---|
| **Currency** | A falling local currency can wipe out share gains in euro terms (Category L). |
| **Capital controls** | Restrictions on moving money in or out can trap investments. |
| **Political and legal** | Sudden policy changes, weak rule of law, sanctions. |
| **Governance** | Weaker protection of minority shareholders; large state or family owners (Category R). |
| **Liquidity** | Fewer buyers and sellers; wider spreads; difficulty selling in a crisis (Category P). |
| **Information** | Different accounting standards, less analyst coverage. |
| **Concentration** | An emerging market index can be dominated by a few countries or sectors. |

## Volatility and long periods of underperformance

Emerging markets can swing sharply, and any single market can disappoint for a long time. Even a developed market can: Japan's Nikkei 225 needed more than 34 years to regain its 1989 high (Category K). Diversifying across many countries reduces dependence on any one of them (Category M).

## Ways to get exposure

- **Broad emerging market funds** spread risk across many countries (Category H).
- **Global "all-world" funds** include emerging markets at their market weight.
- **Single-country funds or shares** concentrate the risk and require more research.

> [!WARNING] Growth is not the same as returns
> A fast-growing economy does not guarantee good returns for shareholders. Share prices may already reflect expected growth, profits may go to non-listed firms, and new share issues can dilute existing shareholders (Category B).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Emerging and frontier markets offer diversification and growth potential, with different risks.
> - Classification criteria (regulation, minority rights, foreign ownership, capital flows) highlight those risks.
> - Key risks: currency, capital controls, politics, governance, liquidity and information.
> - Single markets can underperform for decades; diversify across countries.
> - Economic growth does not automatically mean high shareholder returns.
