## The long con

Investor.gov describes **relationship investment scams**: scammers build a relationship with the target over **weeks or months**, as a friend or romantic partner. They may suggest meeting in person but always find an excuse. In romance scams, they often pledge their love quickly.

Once trust is established, they:

- offer advice on trading, or claim to know about **profitable opportunities**;
- may **impersonate** legitimate investment professionals or firms, or claim to have an "insider";
- often steer the target towards **crypto asset** investments.

## Fake platforms and fake profits

Investor.gov explains what happens next:

- Victims are directed to a **legitimate-looking but fake website**, or to an app. Being available in a well-known app store does **not** prove that an app is legitimate.
- Scammers send **fake screenshots** or manipulate the online account so the "investments" appear to be growing.
- Victims are told to send money by wire transfer or to buy crypto assets such as bitcoin, ether or tether, for example at a **bitcoin ATM**. In reality, they are often sending money **directly to the scammers' wallets or accounts**.
- They are encouraged to invest **ever larger sums**.

## The trap closes: "fees" to withdraw

When the victim tries to withdraw, there is always an obstacle. Investor.gov describes how this turns into **advance fee fraud**: the victim is told to pay a fee, tax, commission or "margin repayment" to release the funds. Victims are often persuaded to pay **more to unlock the account than they deposited in the first place**. Any further payment only increases the loss.

> [!EXAMPLE] A typical sequence
> Week 1–6: friendly chats with a stranger met online.
> Week 7: "My uncle is a crypto trader; I made 30% last month."
> Week 8: first deposit of €500 on an app; the balance shows €650 a week later.
> Week 10: deposits of €20,000; the balance shows €38,000.
> Week 12: withdrawal requested. "First pay 20% tax: €7,600." The contact disappears after the payment.

## Protect yourself

Investor.gov recommends:

- **Ignore** messages from senders you don't know, and block and report them.
- Make investment decisions **independently** of advice from someone who contacted you unsolicited online or by text.
- **Research** opportunities thoroughly and ask questions.
- **Protect** personal and financial information: account details, tax forms, ID documents.
- **Never pay money** to recover an investment or to release funds.
- If you suspect a scam, **stop communicating** immediately and send no more money.

> [!AUSTRIA] In Austria
> Check any platform with the FMA's company database and warning list (lesson 3). If you have been affected, contact your bank immediately and report to the police, as the FMA advises (lesson 7).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Relationship scams build trust for weeks or months before any investment talk.
> - Fake platforms and apps show invented profits; app store presence proves nothing.
> - Money sent, often as crypto, goes directly to the scammers.
> - Requests for "fees" or "taxes" to withdraw are advance fee fraud; never pay them.
> - Stop all contact and payments as soon as you suspect a scam.
