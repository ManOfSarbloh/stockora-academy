## Ponzi schemes

Investor.gov defines a **Ponzi scheme** as an investment fraud that **pays existing investors with funds collected from new investors**. Organisers often promise to invest the money and generate **high returns with little or no risk**, but in many cases they do not invest it at all. They use it to pay earlier investors and keep some for themselves.

Because there are few or no real earnings, a Ponzi scheme needs a **constant flow of new money**. When recruiting new investors becomes hard, or when many investors want their money back at once, it **collapses**. The name comes from **Charles Ponzi**, who defrauded investors in the 1920s with a postage stamp speculation scheme.

> [!EXAMPLE] The arithmetic of collapse
> A scheme promises 10% a month. 100 people invest €1,000 each: €100,000. Paying 10% a month costs €10,000 a month. Without real profits, after 10 months the original money is gone, unless more and more new investors join. The bigger it grows, the more new money it needs, and the bigger the loss when it stops.

## The SEC's red flags

Investor.gov lists warning signs shared by many Ponzi schemes:

- **High returns with little or no risk.** Be highly suspicious of anything "guaranteed".
- **Overly consistent returns,** regardless of market conditions.
- **Unregistered investments** and **unlicensed sellers.**
- **Secretive, complex strategies** you cannot fully understand or get information about.
- **Issues with paperwork,** such as errors in account statements.
- **Difficulty receiving payments** or cashing out; promoters may offer even higher returns for staying in.

## Pyramid schemes

In a **pyramid scheme**, money paid by new recruits is used to pay earlier participants. Investor.gov notes that promoters may go to great lengths to make it look like a legitimate business, such as a multi-level marketing programme, and promote it through social media, ads, websites and presentations. Eventually the scheme gets too big, there is not enough new money, and **most investors lose their money**. Hallmarks listed by Investor.gov:

- **Emphasis on recruiting:** you earn more for signing up others than for selling products.
- **No genuine product or service,** or "products" that are hard to value.
- **Promises of high returns in a short time.**

> [!TIP] The key question
> Where exactly does the money for my returns come from? If the honest answer is "from people who join after me", it is a scheme that must eventually fail.

## In Austria

Anyone offering banking, financial or investment services in Austria needs permission from the FMA, which publishes warnings about providers without it (lesson 3). Check before you invest; a warning is a clear stop sign.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A Ponzi scheme pays earlier investors with new investors' money and collapses when inflows stop.
> - Red flags: high returns with little risk, overly steady returns, unlicensed sellers, paperwork problems, trouble cashing out.
> - Pyramid schemes reward recruitment rather than real products or returns.
> - Ask where the returns come from; "from new members" means eventual collapse.
> - Check providers in Austria with the FMA's database and warning list.
