## Clone firms

The FMA describes **clone firms** as a common fraud method: fraudsters misuse the **name and register data of a genuine, authorised firm** to win victims' trust. In reality, the clone has nothing to do with the firm it pretends to be.

> [!EXAMPLE] How a clone is built (FMA example)
> A genuine Austrian insurance intermediary registers to offer services in the UK; the UK regulator lists it in its public register. A fraudster takes these public details, builds an elaborate website under that name, and contacts people aggressively. As "proof", the fraudster points to the genuine firm's register entry. The "offer" soon extends to banking and investment business.

When the FMA learns of such practices, it publishes a warning naming the clone and its contact details, prohibits the activities and reports the case to the public prosecutor.

## Invented regulators

Some fraudsters go further and invent a supervisory authority. The FMA reported a case in which a provider claimed to be licensed by a "Financial Supervisory Authority (FINSA)" with an address in Vienna and its own website and register documents. No such authority exists. Austria's financial supervisor is the **FMA**.

## Fake FMA staff and documents

In February 2022, the FMA warned that fraudsters were posing as **FMA employees**, using fake email addresses, phone numbers, copied logos and signatures, and even forged FMA decisions, to obtain personal data, account details or payments. The FMA's advice:

- Be especially critical if supposed FMA staff contact you **unsolicited** by phone, email or social media.
- Compare contact details with those on the FMA's website. **Genuine FMA email addresses end only in "@fma.gv.at".**
- If in doubt, contact the FMA's consumer information service yourself.

## Impersonating brokers and advisers

Investor.gov warns that fraudsters also impersonate **real investment professionals and firms**, and recommends contacting a firm only through phone numbers or websites from official records. The SEC has issued a series of alerts about impersonators of the SEC itself, of brokers and advisers, and about AI-assisted fraud.

> [!TIP] The golden rule
> Never trust contact details given to you by the person contacting you. Look up the firm or authority yourself, in an official register or on the official website, and contact them through those channels.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Clone firms copy real, authorised firms' names and register entries (FMA).
> - Some fraudsters invent regulators with fake registers; Austria's financial supervisor is the FMA.
> - Fraudsters pose as FMA staff with forged documents; genuine FMA emails end only in @fma.gv.at.
> - Impersonation of brokers, advisers and regulators is common internationally (SEC).
> - Always look up contact details yourself in official sources.
