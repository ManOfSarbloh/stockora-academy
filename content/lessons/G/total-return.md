Looking only at the share price can be misleading. A company that pays high dividends may show a flat share price while its shareholders are doing quite well. The **total return** combines everything:

**Total return = price change + dividends (or other income) received**

**Total return in % = (end value − start value + income) ÷ start value × 100**

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You buy a share for **€80**. A year later it is worth **€86**, and you received a **€2** dividend.
>
> Total return = (€86 − €80 + €2) ÷ €80 = €8 ÷ €80 = **10%**. Looking at the price alone, you would see only 7.5%.

## Reinvesting income

If you **reinvest** dividends (buy more shares with them), future returns are earned on a larger amount. Over many years, this makes a big difference: it is the compound effect explained in the next lesson. Many ETFs reinvest automatically ("accumulating" funds, Category H).

## Price indexes and total return indexes

Indexes come in two versions: a **price index** only follows share prices, while a **total return** (or performance) index assumes dividends are reinvested. Comparing an investment that pays dividends with a price index makes the index look worse than it really was.

> [!MISTAKE] "This share went nowhere for ten years"
> Check the dividends. A share that stayed at the same price but paid 4% a year still delivered a meaningful total return.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Total return = price change + income (dividends, interest).
> - Reinvesting income lets future returns compound on a larger base.
> - Compare like with like: total return with total return, price with price.
