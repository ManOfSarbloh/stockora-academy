## Dividend yield

The **dividend yield** tells you how much dividend you get per year compared with the share price:

**Dividend yield = annual dividend per share ÷ share price × 100**

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A share costs **€200** and pays **€6** per year in dividends.
>
> Dividend yield = €6 ÷ €200 × 100 = **3%**.

The yield changes every day with the share price, even if the dividend stays the same: if the price falls to €150, the same €6 dividend is a 4% yield.

## Payout ratio

The **payout ratio** tells you how much of its profit a company pays out as dividends:

**Payout ratio = dividends ÷ net profit × 100** (per share: dividend per share ÷ earnings per share)

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company earns **€4.00** per share and pays a dividend of **€1.60** per share.
>
> Payout ratio = €1.60 ÷ €4.00 × 100 = **40%**. The other 60% stays in the company to invest, repay debt or build reserves.

A payout ratio above 100% means the company pays out more than it earned, which it cannot do forever without using reserves or borrowing.

## Beware the "high-yield trap"

A very high dividend yield can be a warning sign rather than a bargain. Often, the yield is high because the **share price has fallen sharply**, for example because investors expect problems. If the company then cuts its dividend, the high yield disappears and the price may fall further.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A share fell from €50 to €20 while last year's dividend was €2. Its yield now looks like 10% (€2 ÷ €20). But if the company cuts the dividend to €0.50 because profits collapsed, the actual yield is only 2.5%.

> [!MISTAKE] "The higher the yield, the better the investment"
> The yield is based on the past dividend and today's price. Look at whether the dividend is covered by profits (payout ratio), how stable the business is, and why the price is where it is.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Dividend yield = annual dividend ÷ share price.
> - Payout ratio = dividends ÷ profit; it shows how much of the profit is paid out.
> - A payout ratio above 100% is not sustainable in the long run.
> - A very high yield often reflects a falling price and a risk of dividend cuts.
