The global financial crisis of 2008 (Category K) was followed in Europe by a second, home-grown crisis: doubts about whether some euro area governments could repay their debts, and even whether the euro would survive.

## How it started

The European Stability Mechanism (ESM) summarises the start of the crisis: already struggling after the US subprime crisis of 2008–09, Europe faced a new crisis in **2010**. Markets began to mistrust a number of countries and demanded ever higher interest rates when they borrowed. They judged them increasingly risky because governments had let their **deficits balloon**, lost **competitiveness**, or allowed **lax oversight of banks**.

Some countries then **lost market access**: they could no longer finance their spending at acceptable borrowing costs. **Greece** was the first to ask for help, receiving bilateral loans from other euro area countries.

## Rescue funds

- **June 2010:** the **European Financial Stability Facility (EFSF)** was set up as a temporary solution.
- **8 October 2012:** the **European Stability Mechanism (ESM)** was established as its permanent successor.

## "Whatever it takes"

On **26 July 2012**, at an investment conference in London, ECB President **Mario Draghi** said:

> "Within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough."

In the same remarks he stressed that the ECB viewed the euro as **irreversible**, and described the main short-term problem as **financial fragmentation**: investors had retreated within their national boundaries.

On **6 September 2012**, the ECB announced the technical features of **Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT)**: purchases of government bonds in the secondary market, subject to a strict condition. A country would first need an appropriate EFSF/ESM programme with its conditions.

> [!NOTE] Words as a policy tool
> The promise itself changed market expectations. A credible central bank commitment can calm a market even before any money is spent, which links to the role of expectations in lesson 3.

## Rates went to zero and below

In the years that followed, the ECB cut its rates further. Its deposit facility rate fell to **0.00%** in July 2012 and below zero from **June 2014** (ECB key interest rates), and it started large-scale asset purchases in mid-2014 (lesson 3).

## What investors can learn

1. **Government bonds can be risky.** Bonds of a euro area government are not automatically safe.
2. **Banks and governments are linked.** Weak banks can burden governments, and weak governments can burden the banks that hold their bonds.
3. **Politics matters for markets.** The euro's future depended on political decisions as well as on economics.
4. **Home bias is a risk.** Investors who held mainly shares and bonds of their own country were especially exposed if that country was in trouble. Diversifying across countries reduces this risk (Category M).

> [!AUSTRIA] Why this matters for Austrian investors
> Austria uses the euro, so the same ECB decisions, rescue mechanisms and euro area risks apply.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - From 2010, markets lost trust in several euro area governments; Greece was the first to need help.
> - The EFSF (June 2010) and the permanent ESM (October 2012) were created as rescue funds.
> - On 26 July 2012, Mario Draghi said the ECB was ready to do "whatever it takes to preserve the euro".
> - The ECB's OMT programme (September 2012) allowed bond purchases under strict conditions.
> - Government bonds can carry real risk; diversification across countries helps.
