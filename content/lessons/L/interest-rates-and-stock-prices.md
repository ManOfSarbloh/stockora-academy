Few things move stock markets as reliably as interest rates. When markets wait nervously for a central bank decision, it is because interest rates touch almost every part of a share's value.

The ECB describes how a change in its official rates spreads through the economy: to money-market rates, to the rates banks charge and pay, to expectations, and to **asset prices such as stock market prices** and the exchange rate. For shareholders there are three main channels.

## Channel 1: company profits

- **Borrowing costs.** Companies with debt pay more interest when rates rise, which reduces profit (Category I, lesson 6).
- **Demand.** The ECB explains that higher rates make loans for consumption or investment less attractive, so households and firms spend less. Lower demand means lower sales for many companies.
- **The reverse** applies when rates fall: cheaper borrowing and, often, more spending.

## Channel 2: competition from safe investments

When savings accounts and government bonds pay 0%, shares look attractive by comparison. When safe investments pay 4%, investors demand more from shares before they take the extra risk.

> [!EXAMPLE] The earnings yield against a bond yield
> A share has a P/E of 20, so its **earnings yield** is 1 ÷ 20 = 5%.
> - If safe government bonds yield 1%, shares offer 4 percentage points more (with more risk).
> - If bonds yield 4%, the extra is only 1 percentage point. Some investors switch to bonds, which can push share prices down until the earnings yield looks attractive again.

This is a rough comparison, not a valuation rule: earnings can grow, while a bond's interest is fixed.

## Channel 3: the value of future cash

Berkshire Hathaway's *Owner's Manual* defines intrinsic value as the **discounted** value of the cash a business can produce, and notes that the estimate must change when interest rates change. A higher interest rate means a higher discount rate, so cash expected in the future is worth less today (Category I, lesson 8).

> [!EXAMPLE] Near cash and far cash
> Two companies each expect to pay out €100.
> - **Company A** pays it in 1 year. At a 4% discount rate it is worth 100 ÷ 1.04 = **€96.15**; at 6% it is worth 100 ÷ 1.06 = **€94.34**. A fall of about **2%**.
> - **Company B** pays it in 20 years. At 4%: 100 ÷ 1.04²⁰ = **€45.64**; at 6%: 100 ÷ 1.06²⁰ = **€31.18**. A fall of about **32%**.
>
> The same rise in rates hurts distant cash flows much more.

This is why **growth companies**, whose profits are expected mostly in the future, are often more sensitive to interest rates than mature companies paying steady dividends today.

## Bonds and shares can fall together

When interest rates rise sharply, existing bonds lose value (their fixed interest becomes less attractive), and shares can fall too. In Damodaran's US data, **2022** was such a year: the S&P 500 lost **18.0%** and 10-year US Treasury bonds lost **17.8%**. In the euro area, the ECB raised its deposit facility rate from **−0.50%** to **2.00%** during 2022, in four steps from July to December (ECB key interest rates).

> [!NOTE] Expectations move prices, not just decisions
> The ECB stresses that expectations of future rate changes affect medium and long-term interest rates. Markets often move when the **outlook** for rates changes, before any decision is taken. A rate rise that everyone expected may barely move prices.

> [!MISTAKE] "Rate cuts are always good for shares"
> Central banks often cut rates because the economy is weakening. Between September 2007 and December 2008 the Fed cut its policy rate from 5.25% to 0–0.25%, yet the S&P 500 kept falling until March 2009 (Federal Reserve History; Category K). Why rates change matters as much as the direction.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Interest rates affect share prices through profits, through competition from safe investments, and through the discounting of future cash.
> - Higher rates reduce the present value of distant cash flows most, so growth stocks are often more rate-sensitive.
> - Bonds and shares can fall at the same time when rates rise sharply, as in 2022.
> - Markets react to changes in expected rates, often before decisions.
> - Rate cuts during a downturn do not guarantee rising prices.
