If you live in Austria and buy shares listed in New York, Tokyo or London, you are making two investments at once: one in the shares, and one in the foreign currency. The exchange rate can add to your return or take away from it, sometimes by more than the shares themselves moved.

## How exchange rates are quoted

The ECB publishes daily **euro reference rates**, with all currencies quoted against the euro as the base currency. A rate of **USD 1.10** means: **1 euro = 1.10 US dollars**. Market shorthand for this is EUR/USD 1.10.

- If the rate **rises** (for example to 1.20), the **euro has strengthened**: one euro buys more dollars. Dollar assets are worth **fewer** euros.
- If the rate **falls** (for example to 1.00), the **euro has weakened**: dollar assets are worth **more** euros.

> [!NOTE] Reference rates are for information
> The ECB states that its reference rates are published for information purposes only and strongly discourages using them for transactions. Your bank or broker uses its own rates, usually with a spread (Category F).

## The currency effect, step by step

> [!EXAMPLE] Shares up, euro return down
> You invest **€10,000** in a US share when EUR/USD is **1.10**. You get $11,000 worth of shares.
>
> One year later the share is up **10%**: it is worth **$12,100**. But the euro has strengthened to **1.21**.
> In euros: 12,100 ÷ 1.21 = **€10,000**. Your return in euros is **0%**, although the share gained 10% in dollars.

> [!EXAMPLE] Shares flat, euro return up
> Same start: €10,000 at 1.10 = $11,000. The share price does not change. The euro weakens to **1.00**.
> In euros: 11,000 ÷ 1.00 = **€11,000**, a **+10%** return, purely from the currency.

The general formula:

**Return in euros = (1 + return in foreign currency) × (1 + change in the foreign currency against the euro) − 1**

## Hidden currency exposure

- **Fund currency is not exposure currency.** An ETF on US shares may be quoted in euros on a European exchange, but it holds dollar assets. Its euro price moves with the dollar (Category H).
- **Company sales.** An Austrian company that sells much of its output abroad is also affected by exchange rates, through its revenues and costs.
- **Monetary policy.** The ECB notes that its decisions can move the exchange rate, which in turn affects import prices and inflation (lesson 3).

## Currency hedging

Some funds offer **currency-hedged** share classes. They use derivatives to offset most of the effect of exchange rate changes, so the euro return comes close to the return in the local currency.

- **Advantage:** less fluctuation from currency moves.
- **Disadvantages:** hedging has costs, and it also removes currency **gains**.

Whether to hedge is a personal decision; there is no universally right answer. Check a fund's documents (KID, Category H) to see whether and how it hedges.

> [!MISTAKE] Comparing returns in different currencies
> A fund factsheet may show the return in dollars, while your account shows it in euros. Check which currency a performance figure is in before comparing it with anything else.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Buying foreign shares is also a bet on the foreign currency.
> - The ECB quotes currencies per 1 euro; a rising EUR/USD rate means dollar assets are worth fewer euros.
> - Euro return = (1 + local return) × (1 + currency change) − 1.
> - A euro-quoted fund can still carry dollar exposure.
> - Currency-hedged share classes reduce currency swings but cost money and remove currency gains.
