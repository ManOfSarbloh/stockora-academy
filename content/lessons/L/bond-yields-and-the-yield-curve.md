Share investors watch the bond market closely. Bond yields set the "risk-free" benchmark against which shares are judged (lesson 1), and the shape of the yield curve says something about what investors expect for the economy.

## Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions

A bond pays fixed interest (the coupon). If market interest rates rise, new bonds pay more, so existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive and their prices fall until their **yield** matches the market.

> [!EXAMPLE] A one-year bond
> A bond pays €103 in one year (€100 repayment + €3 interest). If investors require 3%, its price is 103 ÷ 1.03 = **€100**.
> If market rates rise to 5%, the price falls to 103 ÷ 1.05 = **€98.10**.

The longer a bond's remaining life, the more its price reacts to a change in rates (the same logic as for distant cash flows in lesson 1). This is why long-term government bonds lost heavily in 2022: in Damodaran's US data, 10-year Treasury bonds returned −17.8% that year.

## The yield curve

The **yield curve** plots the yields of government bonds of the same issuer against their remaining life: 3 months, 1 year, 2 years, 10 years, 30 years.

- **Normal (upward sloping):** long-term yields are higher than short-term ones. Investors usually want extra return for lending for longer.
- **Flat:** short and long yields are similar.
- **Inverted (downward sloping):** short-term yields are **higher** than long-term ones.

The ECB explains that longer-term rates depend partly on market expectations about the future course of short-term rates. An inverted curve therefore often means markets expect short-term rates to **fall**, typically because they expect the economy to weaken and the central bank to cut.

## The famous recession signal

The **term spread** is the difference between a long-term and a short-term yield, for example the 10-year yield minus the 1-year yield.

The New York Fed's Liberty Street Economics blog (May 2025) describes the US term spread as having an unparalleled track record of predicting US recessions since the 1950s: the 10-year minus 1-year spread **fell below zero before every recession** and only rarely did so without a recession following. In its model, the estimated probability of a recession within a year rose well above 50% in the run-up to each recession since 1972.

## The caveats

- **One model, wide uncertainty.** The same New York Fed post stresses that its figure is just an estimate from one model, not an official forecast, and that the uncertainty around such estimates is large.
- **Other measures may tell a different story.** A 2018 Federal Reserve note argued that a "near-term forward spread", which captures market expectations of monetary easing, is a more economically intuitive indicator than the usual long-term spread.
- **Timing is vague.** Even when the signal works, the recession can start many months later, and stock markets can rise in the meantime.
- **Mostly US evidence.** The track record comes mainly from US data.

> [!MISTAKE] Selling everything when the curve inverts
> An inverted yield curve is a warning about the economy, not a precise timing signal for share prices. Category K showed that markets usually turn before the economy, sometimes in unexpected directions.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - When yields rise, existing bond prices fall; long-term bonds react most.
> - The yield curve shows yields across maturities; it is usually upward sloping.
> - An inverted curve suggests markets expect rate cuts, often because of an expected slowdown.
> - In US data since the 1950s, the 10-year minus 1-year spread turned negative before every recession.
> - The signal is uncertain and says little about the timing of share price moves.
