The ECB calls the process by which its decisions affect the economy and prices the **transmission mechanism**. Understanding it helps you see why markets react to central banks the way they do.

## The chain, step by step

The ECB's own description of the main channels:

1. **Official interest rates change.** Because the central bank is the monopoly issuer of money, it can fully determine the rate it charges banks.
2. **Market and bank rates follow.** Money-market rates change directly; banks then adjust the rates on loans and deposits for their customers.
3. **Expectations shift.** Expectations of future official rates affect medium and long-term interest rates. A credible central bank also anchors expectations of future inflation.
4. **Asset prices and the exchange rate adjust.** Changed financing conditions and expectations can move **stock market prices** and the **exchange rate**. A change in the exchange rate can affect inflation directly through the prices of imported goods.
5. **Saving and investment decisions change.** Higher rates make borrowing for consumption or investment less attractive. Asset prices also matter through **wealth effects**: when share prices rise, share-owning households feel richer and may spend more, and the reverse when prices fall.
6. **Demand and, finally, prices change.**

> [!NOTE] Long, variable and uncertain lags
> The ECB stresses that transmission is characterised by **long, variable and uncertain time lags**, which makes it difficult to predict the precise effect of a decision. This is why the ECB targets inflation over the **medium term** rather than month by month.

## Why markets react immediately

The economy responds slowly, but markets respond in seconds, because prices reflect expectations. A surprise rate change, or a change in what the central bank signals about the future, instantly changes the discount rates investors apply (lesson 1) and expectations of growth.

> [!EXAMPLE] Same decision, different reactions
> - The ECB raises rates by 0.25 percentage points, exactly as analysts expected, and signals no further rises. Share prices may **rise**, because some investors feared more increases.
> - The ECB leaves rates unchanged but signals that rates will stay high for longer than expected. Share prices may **fall**, although nothing was "done".

## When rates hit zero: buying bonds

When short-term rates are already at or near zero, central banks have used other tools:

- **Asset purchases** (quantitative easing): the ECB explains that its **asset purchase programme (APP)** began as part of a package of non-standard measures initiated in **mid-2014**, to support the transmission of monetary policy and provide the policy accommodation needed for price stability. Buying bonds pushes their prices up and long-term yields down.
- **Forward guidance**: promising to keep rates low for longer, as the Fed did after 2008 (Federal Reserve History).

Lower long-term yields make borrowing cheaper and, through channel 3 of lesson 1, tend to support the prices of shares and other assets. Reversing these policies works in the opposite direction.

> [!TIP] Read the statement, not just the number
> After each monetary policy meeting, the ECB publishes a statement and holds a press conference. Investors read them closely for hints about future decisions. The OeNB also explains decisions from an Austrian perspective on its website.

> [!MISTAKE] Expecting an immediate effect on inflation
> If inflation does not fall the month after a rate rise, the policy has not necessarily failed. The ECB itself says the effects take time and are uncertain.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Rate changes pass through money-market rates, bank rates, expectations, asset prices, the exchange rate and spending to inflation.
> - Effects on the economy come with long, variable and uncertain lags; markets react instantly to surprises.
> - What the central bank signals about the future can matter more than today's decision.
> - At the zero lower bound, central banks used asset purchases and forward guidance.
> - Share prices are one channel of monetary policy, not its target.
