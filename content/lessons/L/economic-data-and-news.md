Every week brings new economic numbers: inflation, growth, unemployment, business surveys. Some move markets strongly, most barely. The key to understanding why is the word **expectation**.

## The most watched figures

| Figure | What it measures | Why markets care |
|---|---|---|
| **GDP** (gross domestic product) | The value of everything an economy produces | Growth drives company sales and profits |
| **Inflation** (HICP, VPI, US CPI) | Change in consumer prices | Drives central bank decisions (lesson 2) |
| **Unemployment and jobs** | How many people are working | Signals the strength of demand and wage pressure |
| **Business and consumer surveys** | How optimistic firms and households are | Early hints about the direction of the economy |
| **Central bank decisions** | Interest rates and guidance | Affect valuations directly (lesson 1) |

Official statistics are published on **pre-announced dates**. Eurostat publishes a release calendar for euro area figures; Statistik Austria and other national offices announce theirs too.

## Expected versus actual

Before a release, economists publish forecasts, and markets price in the average expectation. When the number comes out, prices move on the **surprise**: the difference between the actual figure and what was expected.

> [!EXAMPLE] Good news, falling prices
> Analysts expect euro area inflation of 2.5%. The figure comes in at 2.9%.
> Investors now expect the ECB to keep rates higher for longer. Bond yields rise and share prices fall, although nothing has changed in the companies themselves.
>
> If the figure had come in at 2.5%, as expected, prices might barely have moved.

This also explains why "bad" economic news sometimes makes shares **rise**: weak data can raise hopes of interest rate cuts.

## First estimates get revised

Many figures are first published as quick estimates and revised later as more data arrives.

> [!AUSTRIA] A flash estimate and the final figure
> On 31 July 2026, Statistik Austria's **flash estimate** put July inflation at **2.7%**. On 19 August 2026, the final figure was **2.8%**, 0.1 percentage points higher than the flash estimate.

Some judgements come only with long delays: the NBER dates US recessions months after they start or end (Category K).

## News about companies

For individual shares, company news usually matters more than macro data: results, profit warnings, takeovers, management changes. Important company news, such as a profit warning, is published as an **ad hoc announcement** to investors (Wiener Börse lexicon); Category R covers the legal rules. This is why prices sometimes jump the moment an announcement appears.

## Reading the news calmly

- **Headlines explain moves after the fact.** "Shares fell on inflation fears" is often an attempt to give a reason for a move with many causes.
- **One number is not a trend.** Look at several months, and remember revisions.
- **Long-term investors rarely need to react.** A monthly inflation surprise barely changes the value of a company over 20 years.
- **Check the source.** Official releases from Statistik Austria, Eurostat or the ECB are more reliable than social media summaries (Category W).

> [!MISTAKE] Trading on headlines
> By the time you read a headline, professional traders using automated systems have usually already reacted (Category D). Trying to trade on public news is a race most private investors lose.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - GDP, inflation, jobs, surveys and central bank decisions are the most watched macro figures.
> - They are published on pre-announced dates; check official release calendars.
> - Markets move on surprises against expectations, not on whether news is "good" or "bad".
> - First estimates are revised; Austria's July 2026 inflation went from 2.7% (flash) to 2.8% (final).
> - Long-term investors rarely need to react to individual data releases.
