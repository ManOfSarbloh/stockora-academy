**Inflation** is the general rise in prices. Category G explained how it eats into your real returns. This lesson looks at how inflation affects the stock market itself.

## How inflation is measured in Austria

> [!AUSTRIA] VPI and HICP
> **Statistik Austria** publishes two consumer price indexes:
> - the national **Verbraucherpreisindex (VPI)**, the figure Statistik Austria headlines as "the inflation rate" in its monthly releases;
> - the **Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP, German: HVPI)**, calculated in the same way across the EU. The ECB's 2% target refers to the HICP for the euro area as a whole.
>
> According to Statistik Austria, VPI inflation in Austria was **7.8% in 2023, 2.9% in 2024 and 3.6% in 2025**. It described 2022 to 2024 as a phase of high inflation and noted that in 2025 inflation in Austria was markedly higher than in almost all other euro area countries, driven especially by electricity prices after the end of the electricity price brake.

## Why high inflation tends to hurt shares in the short run

1. **Central banks raise rates** to fight it (lesson 2). Higher rates lower the present value of future profits (lesson 1). The ECB raised its deposit rate from −0.50% to 4.00% between July 2022 and September 2023.
2. **Costs rise.** Wages, energy and materials become more expensive. Companies that cannot pass these costs on see their margins squeezed.
3. **Uncertainty rises.** Investors find it harder to estimate future profits and demand a higher return for the risk.
4. **Bonds suffer too.** Fixed interest payments lose real value and yields rise. In Damodaran's US data, both shares (−18.0%) and 10-year Treasury bonds (−17.8%) lost money in 2022.

## Why shares can protect against inflation in the long run

Shares are claims on real businesses. Over long periods, companies can raise prices, so their revenues and profits tend to grow with inflation, at least in nominal terms. A bond's coupon, by contrast, is fixed in euros. This is why shares have often been seen as a better long-term protection against inflation than fixed-income investments, although the protection is imperfect and can fail for years at a time.

## Pricing power

How well a company copes with inflation depends on whether it can raise prices without losing customers: its **pricing power**. This links to the idea of a competitive "moat" (Category I, lesson 9).

> [!EXAMPLE] Two companies, same cost shock
> Both companies have revenue of €100 m and costs of €90 m (profit €10 m). Costs rise by 10% to €99 m.
> - **Company A** has a strong brand and raises prices by 10%: revenue €110 m, profit €11 m. Profit kept pace with inflation.
> - **Company B** is in a fiercely competitive market and can only raise prices by 3%: revenue €103 m, profit **€4 m**. Profit fell by 60%.

## Deflation is not good news either

The ECB's target is symmetric because **falling** prices are also harmful: debts become heavier in real terms and people postpone spending. Federal Reserve History describes how deflation in the Great Depression increased debt burdens, reduced consumption and pushed banks, firms and individuals into bankruptcy (Category K).

> [!MISTAKE] Looking only at nominal returns
> A share portfolio that gained 5% in a year with 8% inflation lost purchasing power. Always compare returns with inflation (Category G).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Austria measures inflation with the national VPI and the EU-harmonised HICP; the ECB targets 2% HICP for the euro area.
> - Austrian VPI inflation was 7.8% in 2023, 2.9% in 2024 and 3.6% in 2025 (Statistik Austria).
> - Unexpectedly high inflation tends to hurt shares and bonds at the same time, mainly through higher interest rates and costs.
> - Over long periods, companies can raise prices, which gives shares some inflation protection.
> - Pricing power decides which companies cope best.
