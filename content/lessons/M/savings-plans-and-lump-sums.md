A common way to invest regularly is a **savings plan** (*Sparplan*): a fixed amount, for example €100, is invested automatically every month into a fund or ETF. Investor.gov calls this **dollar-cost averaging**; the Wiener Börse lexicon calls it the **cost-average effect** (*Durchschnittskosteneffekt*).

## How it works

Investor.gov explains: by investing the same amount at regular intervals regardless of market ups and downs, you buy **more units when prices are low and fewer when prices are high**. The Wiener Börse lexicon adds that when prices fluctuate, this lowers the average cost per unit.

> [!EXAMPLE] Four months of €100
> | Month | Price per unit | Units bought |
> |---|---:|---:|
> | 1 | €50 | 2.00 |
> | 2 | €40 | 2.50 |
> | 3 | €25 | 4.00 |
> | 4 | €50 | 2.00 |
> | **Total** | | **10.50 units for €400** |
>
> Average cost per unit: 400 ÷ 10.5 ≈ **€38.10**.
> Average of the four prices: (50 + 40 + 25 + 50) ÷ 4 = **€41.25**.
>
> Because more units were bought at low prices, the average cost is below the average price. At the end, with the price back at €50, the holding is worth 10.5 × 50 = **€525**, a gain of 31% although the price only returned to where it started.

> [!ANIMATION] cost-average
> Each month €100 buys more units when the price is low and fewer when it is high, so the average cost (€38.10) ends up below the average price (€41.25).

## What a savings plan does well

- **Discipline:** money is invested automatically, without needing to decide each month.
- **No market timing:** you do not have to guess the "right" moment.
- **Small amounts:** you can start with modest sums, as income allows.
- **Emotional help:** falling prices become a chance to buy more cheaply instead of only a source of fear.

## What it does not do

- It does **not** guarantee a profit or protect against loss. If prices fall and stay low, the plan loses money too.
- Costs matter: a fixed fee per purchase eats a larger share of small amounts (Category G). Compare savings plan fees.

## A lump sum: all at once or bit by bit?

If you already have a larger amount, for example from an inheritance, you face a different question: invest it all now, or spread it over several months?

- **Investing at once** puts the money to work immediately. Because shares have risen in most years historically (in Damodaran's US data, 72 of 98 years ended with a gain), being invested earlier has more often than not been an advantage.
- **Spreading it out** reduces the risk of investing everything just before a big fall, and the regret that follows. The price is that money waiting in cash may miss gains.

There is no universally correct answer. What matters most is choosing an approach you will actually stick with, writing it down, and not abandoning it when markets move.

> [!MISTAKE] Stopping the plan in a crash
> The cost-average effect works precisely because you keep buying when prices are low. Stopping a savings plan during a fall, and restarting after prices have recovered, turns the method on its head.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A savings plan invests a fixed amount at regular intervals.
> - It buys more units at low prices, which lowers the average cost per unit when prices fluctuate.
> - It provides discipline but does not guarantee profits; fees per purchase matter.
> - For a lump sum, investing at once and spreading it out each have advantages; consistency matters most.
> - Keep the plan running in falling markets.
