There are two broad ways to invest in shares through funds:

- **Active:** a fund manager selects shares, trying to do better than the market or a benchmark index.
- **Passive:** the fund simply tracks an index, such as the MSCI World or the ATX, buying the shares it contains (Category H).

Active management sounds attractive: who would not want an expert to pick the winners? The question is whether it works **after costs**.

## The cost gap

The European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) publishes yearly data on the costs and performance of funds sold to retail investors in the EU. Its 2024 report (covering data up to the end of 2023) shows the average **ongoing costs** of equity funds held by retail investors over a one-year horizon:

| Equity UCITS, 2023 | Ongoing costs per year |
|---|---:|
| Active funds | 1.30% |
| Passive funds (not ETFs) | 0.37% |
| ETFs | 0.22% |

ESMA summarises that passive funds are on average about **60–80% cheaper** than active funds.

## Performance after costs

ESMA's report states that, despite falling costs, **active equity funds continued to underperform**, after fees, both passive non-exchange-traded equity funds and equity ETFs.

Why is it so hard? Before costs, all investors together **are** the market. If some active managers beat it, others must lag behind it. After costs, the average active investor must fall behind the market by roughly the extra costs. A manager must therefore be clearly better than the average just to break even with a cheap index fund.

> [!EXAMPLE] What a cost gap of 1 percentage point means
> €10,000 invested for 30 years.
> - At 6% a year after costs: 10,000 × 1.06³⁰ ≈ **€57,435**
> - At 5% a year after costs: 10,000 × 1.05³⁰ ≈ **€43,219**
>
> One percentage point of extra yearly cost reduces the final amount by about **€14,200**, or about a quarter.

## Can you pick the winners in advance?

Some active funds do beat the market in a given period. The challenge is identifying them **beforehand**. A fund that did well in the last five years may have been lucky, may have taken extra risk, or may have grown so large that its strategy no longer works. Comparisons that look only at funds that still exist can also overstate success, because funds that were closed or merged drop out of the statistics (survivorship bias, Category J).

## Passive is not risk-free

- An index fund falls when the market falls. Passive investing removes **manager risk**, not **market risk**.
- Market-cap weighted indexes can become concentrated in a few large companies or one sector (Category H).
- "Passive" funds come in many flavours, including narrow thematic and leveraged products, which can be very risky (Category H).

## Making your own decision

Neither approach is "always right". Questions to ask:

- What exactly does the fund do, and does it match your plan?
- What does it cost in total, including entry fees and trading costs? (See the KID, Category H.)
- If it is active: what is the reason to believe it will beat its benchmark after costs, in the future?

> [!MISTAKE] Choosing a fund by its star rating or last year's return
> Past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results. Costs, by contrast, are almost certain to be paid every year.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Active funds try to beat the market; passive funds track an index.
> - In ESMA's 2023 data, active equity funds cost 1.30% a year on average, ETFs 0.22%.
> - ESMA found that active equity funds continued to underperform passive funds and ETFs after fees.
> - Small cost differences compound into large sums over decades.
> - Passive funds still carry full market risk.
