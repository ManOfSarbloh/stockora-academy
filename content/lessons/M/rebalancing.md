You chose a mix, say 60% shares and 40% bonds. A year later shares have risen strongly and your portfolio is 70% shares. Without doing anything, you now take more risk than you decided to. **Rebalancing** brings the portfolio back to its original mix (Investor.gov).

## Why portfolios drift

> [!EXAMPLE] Drift after a good year for shares
> Start: **€60,000 in shares** and **€40,000 in bonds** (60/40, total €100,000).
> Shares rise 25%, bonds stay flat:
> - Shares €75,000, bonds €40,000, total €115,000.
> - New mix: 75 ÷ 115 = **65% shares**.
>
> To return to 60/40: target shares = 0.60 × 115,000 = €69,000. Sell **€6,000** of shares and buy **€6,000** of bonds.

> [!ANIMATION] rebalancing
> The 60/40 mix drifts to 65% shares after shares rise 25%; moving €6,000 from shares to bonds restores 60/40.

The SEC's guide uses a similar example: a target of 60% shares that grew to 80% after a market rise.

## Three ways to rebalance

The SEC describes three methods:

1. **Sell** some of the over-weighted category and buy the under-weighted one.
2. **Buy** more of the under-weighted category with new money.
3. If you invest regularly, **redirect your contributions** to the under-weighted category until the mix is back on target.

Methods 2 and 3 avoid selling, which can avoid transaction costs and, in many cases, taxes on gains.

## When to rebalance

The SEC mentions two common approaches:

- **By the calendar:** for example every six or twelve months.
- **By thresholds:** whenever a category drifts more than a set amount from its target, for example 5 percentage points.

It adds that rebalancing tends to work best when done on a relatively **infrequent** basis.

## Buy low, sell high, automatically

The SEC points out that rebalancing forces you to cut back on recent "winners" and add to recent "losers", in other words to **buy low and sell high**. That is emotionally hard. It feels wrong to sell what is going up and buy what has fallen. A rule decided in advance helps you do it anyway.

> [!AUSTRIA] Costs and taxes
> Before rebalancing by selling, check the transaction costs and the tax effect. In Austria, realised gains on shares and funds held as private assets are generally subject to capital gains tax (KESt). Category Q explains the rules; check them with official sources or a tax adviser.

> [!MISTAKE] Rebalancing too often
> Adjusting the portfolio every week adds costs and taxes without much benefit. Small deviations from the target are fine.

## Rebalancing within categories

The SEC also suggests reviewing the investments **within** each category. If one share or sector has grown to dominate your share portfolio, the same logic applies.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Portfolios drift as some investments grow faster than others; rebalancing restores the target mix.
> - Rebalance by selling, by buying with new money, or by redirecting regular contributions.
> - Use a calendar rule or a threshold rule, and do it infrequently.
> - Rebalancing means trimming winners and adding to losers, which takes discipline.
> - Consider costs and taxes before selling.
