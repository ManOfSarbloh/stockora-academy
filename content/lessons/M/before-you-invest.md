Most investment mistakes are not about choosing the wrong share. They happen because money was invested without a clear purpose, or because it had to be taken out at the worst possible moment. This category is about building a plan. It does not recommend any particular product.

## Step 1: what is the money for?

FINRA, the US investor protection body, puts it simply: the first step before opening an investment account is to **define your goals**. Typical goals:

- a safety cushion for emergencies,
- a large purchase in a few years (a car, a deposit for a flat),
- retirement in 20 or 30 years,
- a child's education.

Each goal can have its own pot of money and its own strategy.

## Step 2: when will you need it?

Your **time horizon** is the number of months, years or decades you need to invest to reach a goal (Investor.gov). It is the single most important input for how much risk you can take:

| Time horizon | What history suggests |
|---|---|
| Under 3 years | Shares can easily be down when you need the money (Category K). Safer options usually fit better. |
| 3–10 years | Some share exposure may fit, but a bad decade is possible. |
| More than 10 years | More room to ride out falls, although losses over long periods are still possible. |

The SEC's guide explains that an investor with a longer horizon may be more comfortable with riskier investments because they can wait out the ups and downs, while someone saving for a goal a few years away would likely take less risk. It adds that a portfolio heavily weighted in shares would be inappropriate for a short-term goal such as a family holiday.

## Step 3: an emergency fund first

> [!TIP] Why an emergency fund comes first
> FINRA explains that an emergency fund lets you cover a large unexpected expense or a temporary loss of income **without taking on debt or selling your investments**. Financial planners often recommend **three to six months of living expenses**, and more for people with variable income. FINRA suggests keeping it in an easily accessible, interest-bearing account.

Without a cushion, a broken washing machine or a lost job can force you to sell shares, perhaps in the middle of a crash.

> [!AUSTRIA] Deposit protection
> In Austria, bank deposits are protected by statutory deposit guarantee up to a limit per person and bank. Category F explains how it works and where to check the current rules.

## Step 4: deal with expensive debt

FINRA calls it a smart move to pay off high-interest debt, such as credit card balances, before investing, because the interest on such debt is often higher than the potential return on an investment.

> [!EXAMPLE] Paying off debt is a guaranteed return
> You owe €3,000 on an overdraft at 12% a year. Paying it off "earns" you 12% a year with certainty, by saving the interest. No share investment can promise that.

## Step 5: know yourself

Before choosing investments, be honest about:

- how much loss you could **afford** without changing your life plans, and
- how much loss you could **bear** without panicking.

Lesson 2 covers this risk tolerance in detail.

> [!MISTAKE] Investing the rent money
> Money you will need within the next year or two, for rent, bills or a planned purchase, does not belong in shares. A 30% fall in the wrong month cannot be "waited out" if the money is due.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Start with clear goals; each goal can have its own pot and strategy.
> - The time horizon of a goal is the key input for how much risk fits.
> - Build an emergency fund first; planners often suggest three to six months of expenses.
> - Paying off high-interest debt is often the best "investment".
> - Money needed soon should not be in shares.
