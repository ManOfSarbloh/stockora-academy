The SEC's beginners' guide opens with a street vendor who sells both **umbrellas and sunglasses**. On rainy days umbrellas sell, on sunny days sunglasses do. By selling both, the vendor reduces the risk of a bad day. That is **diversification**: spreading money among different investments to reduce risk.

## Two levels

The SEC says a diversified portfolio should be diversified at two levels:

1. **Between asset categories:** shares, bonds, cash and possibly others (lesson 3).
2. **Within each asset category:** many different companies, sectors and countries.

The aim is to hold investments that may perform differently under different market conditions.

## How many shares are enough?

The SEC's guide says a share portfolio of only four or five individual shares is **not** diversified, and that you need at least a dozen carefully selected shares to be truly diversified. Because that is hard to achieve, it notes that many investors find it easier to diversify through **funds**: a total stock market index fund, for example, may own shares in thousands of companies.

> [!EXAMPLE] One failure, two portfolios
> You invest €20,000 in shares. One company goes bankrupt and its shares become worthless.
> - Portfolio A holds **5 shares** equally (€4,000 each): the loss is **€4,000**, or 20%.
> - Portfolio B holds a fund with **500 companies** in equal amounts (€40 each): the loss is **€40**, or 0.2%.

Single companies really can fail completely. Wirecard, a member of Germany's DAX 30 index, filed for insolvency in 2020 after announcing that €1.9 billion of its reported cash did not exist (ESMA; Category I). In 1873 about half of the companies listed in Vienna disappeared (Wiener Börse; Category K).

## Diversifying across countries

A market where you live is only a small part of the world. Holding mainly shares of your own country, called **home bias**, ties your savings to one economy that may already determine your job and the value of your home. Japan's market needed more than 34 years to regain its 1989 peak (Category K). Funds that cover many countries spread this risk.

## Watch out for false diversification

- **Many funds, same contents.** Five funds that all hold the same large companies are not five times as diversified. Check the top holdings (Category H).
- **Sector funds.** The SEC notes that a fund does not automatically diversify, especially if it focuses on one industry.
- **Costs.** The SEC also points out that adding more investments can mean more fees, which lower returns.

## The limits of diversification

Diversification reduces the risk of individual companies or sectors. It cannot remove the risk of the whole market falling: a fund holding the entire S&P 500 still lost 57% from October 2007 to March 2009 (Federal Reserve History). And in 2022, even shares and bonds lost money at the same time (Damodaran's US data). Diversification limits damage; it does not guarantee against loss.

> [!MISTAKE] "I only invest in companies I know, so I'm safe"
> Knowing a company's products is not the same as knowing whether its shares are fairly priced or its accounts are sound. Concentrating in a few familiar names increases risk.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Diversification spreads money across investments that react differently, to reduce risk.
> - Diversify between asset categories and within them (companies, sectors, countries).
> - The SEC says four or five shares are not diversified; broad funds can hold thousands.
> - Beware of overlap between funds and of narrow sector funds.
> - Diversification cannot prevent market-wide falls.
