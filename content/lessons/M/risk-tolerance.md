Investor.gov defines **risk tolerance** as an investor's **ability and willingness** to lose some or all of an investment in exchange for greater potential returns. Those two words point to two different questions.

## Ability: can you afford the loss?

This is about your finances, sometimes called **risk capacity**:

- How long until you need the money (lesson 1)?
- Is your income secure? Do you have an emergency fund?
- What share of your total wealth is invested?
- Do you have obligations such as a mortgage or children?

A 30-year-old with a stable job, savings and decades to retirement can objectively afford bigger temporary losses than someone who needs the money in two years.

## Willingness: can you stand the loss?

This is about psychology. Many people overestimate their tolerance when markets are rising. The real test comes in a crash, when the news is frightening and your account shows a large loss.

> [!EXAMPLE] A stress test with real history
> Imagine you have **€50,000** invested entirely in shares, and markets repeat their worst years from Damodaran's US data:
> - A year like **2008** (−36.6%): your €50,000 becomes about **€31,700**.
> - A year like **1931** (−43.8%): it becomes about **€28,100**.
>
> Would you hold on, sell, or buy more? If you honestly think you would sell in panic, a lower share of stocks may suit you better, because selling at the bottom turns a temporary fall into a permanent loss.

## Matching risk and return

The SEC's guide is blunt: all investments involve some risk, and the reward for taking on risk is the potential for greater return. An aggressive investor accepts the chance of losses for better results; a conservative investor prefers to preserve the original investment.

There is no "correct" level of risk. The aim is a mix that has a good chance of reaching your goal **at a level of risk you can live with**, in the SEC's words.

> [!AUSTRIA] Questionnaires at banks and brokers
> When a bank or investment firm gives you investment advice in the EU, it must assess whether its recommendations are **suitable** for you, including your financial situation, your objectives and your ability to bear losses. For execution-only services, it may assess whether a complex product is **appropriate** given your knowledge and experience. Category F explains these MiFID II tests. Answer the questions honestly: they protect you.

> [!WARNING] Beware of questionnaires with a sales motive
> The SEC's guide notes that online risk questionnaires can be a useful starting point, but that their results may be biased towards the products sold by the companies that run them.

## Your tolerance changes over time

Changes in your life, such as a new job, children, an inheritance, or approaching retirement, can change both your ability and your willingness to take risk. Review your answers every few years and after big life events (lesson 9).

> [!MISTAKE] Judging your tolerance in a bull market
> After several good years, almost everyone feels comfortable with risk. Decide your mix by thinking through a severe fall in advance, not by looking at recent gains.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Risk tolerance = ability (can you afford losses?) + willingness (can you stand them?).
> - Use real historical falls, like 2008 (−36.6%), to test your reaction in advance.
> - Selling in panic turns temporary falls into permanent losses.
> - Advisers in the EU must check that recommendations suit your situation; answer honestly.
> - Reassess your tolerance after major life changes.
