Professional investors often work with a written **investment policy statement**. Private investors benefit from the same idea in a simple form: one or two pages that say what you are doing and why. Its main value shows up in bad times, when emotions push you to abandon a sensible plan (Category O).

## A template

> [!NOTE] My investment plan (example structure)
> **1. Goals**
> - Goal, amount needed, date needed. For example: "Retirement top-up, from 2055."
>
> **2. Safety first**
> - Emergency fund: ___ months of expenses, kept in ___.
> - High-interest debt: none / paying off by ___.
>
> **3. Risk tolerance**
> - The largest temporary fall I can accept without selling: ___%.
>
> **4. Target mix** (per goal)
> - Shares ___% / bonds ___% / cash ___%.
> - How the mix will change as the goal approaches (glide path).
>
> **5. How I invest**
> - Type of products (for example broad index funds), maximum costs per year, broker.
> - Savings plan: €___ per month on day ___.
>
> **6. Rebalancing rule**
> - Every ___ months, or when a category drifts more than ___ percentage points.
>
> **7. What I do in a crash**
> - "I keep my savings plan running. I do not sell because of falling prices. I rebalance according to rule 6."
>
> **8. When I review the plan**
> - Once a year, and after major life events.

## Rules for checking any new investment

The SEC's **Five Questions to Ask Before You Invest** make a good permanent section of any plan:

1. Is the seller licensed?
2. Is the investment registered (in the EU: is there an approved prospectus or a key information document)?
3. How do the risks compare with the potential rewards?
4. Do you understand the investment? (The SEC cites the rule of thumb: never invest in something you don't understand.)
5. Where can you turn for help?

## Keep it simple

- **Fewer, broader products** are easier to monitor than many narrow ones (lesson 4).
- **Automate** what you can: savings plans, reminders for rebalancing.
- **Write down the reasons** for any change. If the reason is "prices fell" or "everyone is buying", read section 7 again.

> [!EXAMPLE] Using the plan in a crash
> Markets fall 30%. Your share portion drops from 60% to about 51% of your portfolio. Your plan says: rebalance when the drift exceeds 5 percentage points. So instead of selling shares in panic, you **buy** some with money from the bond portion, at lower prices.

> [!WARNING] Not a substitute for advice
> This template helps you organise your own thinking. It does not consider your individual situation, taxes or legal circumstances. For those, consider independent professional advice (Category F explains how to check advisers).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A short written plan covers goals, safety, risk tolerance, target mix, products, rebalancing and crash behaviour.
> - Decide in calm times what you will do in turbulent times.
> - Use the SEC's five questions before any new investment.
> - Keep products few and broad, and automate where possible.
> - Review the plan yearly and after major life changes.
