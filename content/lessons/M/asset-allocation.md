**Asset allocation** means dividing a portfolio among different asset categories, such as shares, bonds and cash (SEC). The SEC calls choosing the mix "a very personal one" that depends largely on your **time horizon** and **risk tolerance** (lessons 1 and 2).

## The three main asset categories

The SEC's beginners' guide describes them like this:

| Category | Character |
|---|---|
| **Shares (stocks)** | Historically the greatest risk and the highest returns. Very risky in the short term; investors who rode out the swings over long periods have generally been rewarded. |
| **Bonds** | Generally less volatile than shares, with more modest returns. High-yield ("junk") bonds can offer higher returns but carry higher risk. |
| **Cash and equivalents** | The safest, with the lowest return. The main danger is **inflation**: returns may not keep up with rising prices. |

Other categories, such as property, gold and other commodities, exist too (Category U).

## Why the mix matters

The SEC explains that the returns of the major asset categories have historically **not moved up and down at the same time**. When one does badly, another may do better, so combining them gives a smoother ride. At the same time, the mix decides whether you are likely to reach your goal: too little risk and the return may be too low, too much and the money may not be there when you need it.

## How different mixes behaved: 1928–2025

The table below uses Damodaran's yearly US data for the S&P 500 (with dividends) and 10-year US Treasury bonds. Each mix is rebalanced back to its target at the start of every year. Returns are in US dollars, before inflation, costs and taxes.

| Shares / bonds | Average yearly return | Years with a loss (of 98) | Worst calendar year | Worst fall from a year-end peak | 2008 | 2022 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 / 0 | 10.0% | 26 | −43.8% (1931) | −64.8% | −36.5% | −18.0% |
| 80 / 20 | 9.3% | 25 | −35.6% (1931) | −53.5% | −25.2% | −18.0% |
| 60 / 40 | 8.3% | 21 | −27.3% (1931) | −40.1% | −13.9% | −18.0% |
| 40 / 60 | 7.2% | 20 | −19.1% (1931) | −25.6% | −2.6% | −17.9% |
| 20 / 80 | 6.0% | 15 | −17.9% (2022) | −17.9% | +8.8% | −17.9% |
| 0 / 100 | 4.5% | 20 | −17.8% (2022) | −21.5% | +20.1% | −17.8% |

![For each share/bond mix, an upward bar for the average yearly return (10.0% down to 4.5%) and a downward bar for the worst calendar year (−43.8% up to −17.8%)](./img/mixes-return-vs-worst-year.svg)

*Own calculation from Damodaran's annual returns. "Average yearly return" is the compound annual growth rate. "Worst fall" is measured on year-end values only; falls within a year were deeper.*

What the table shows:

1. **More shares, higher long-run return, deeper falls.** Moving from 60% to 100% shares raised the average return by under 2 percentage points a year, but deepened the worst fall from −40% to −65%.
2. **Mixing helped most of the time.** In 2008, bonds rose while shares fell, cushioning a mixed portfolio.
3. **But not always.** In 2022, shares and bonds fell together, so every mix lost about 18%.
4. **Bonds alone were not risk-free.** An all-bond portfolio also had losing years and a fall of more than 20%.

> [!WARNING] One country, one period
> This is US history in dollars. Other countries, periods and currencies give different numbers, and the future may differ again. Use the table to understand trade-offs, not to predict returns.

## Choosing a mix

The SEC notes that determining the right allocation is complicated: you are trying to pick a mix with the **highest probability of meeting your goal at a level of risk you can live with**, and to adjust it as the goal approaches. Some common ideas:

- **Separate pots by goal.** Short-term goals in cash, long-term goals with more shares.
- **Reduce risk as the goal approaches.** The SEC notes that most people investing for retirement hold fewer shares and more bonds and cash as they get closer to retirement age. Target-date funds do this automatically (lesson 8).
- **Only change the mix for good reasons:** a change in your time horizon, risk tolerance, financial situation or goal, not because one category has done well recently (SEC).

> [!MISTAKE] Chasing last year's winner
> The SEC warns that savvy investors typically do not change their asset allocation based on recent performance, for example buying more shares when the market is hot. That is when they rebalance instead (lesson 5).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Asset allocation is the split between shares, bonds, cash and other categories; it depends on your horizon and risk tolerance.
> - In US data for 1928–2025, more shares meant higher long-run returns and much deeper falls.
> - Combining asset categories usually smoothed returns, but in 2022 shares and bonds fell together.
> - Choose a mix you can stick with through a severe fall.
> - Change the mix when your situation changes, not because of recent performance.
