An index is just a calculation; it pays no fees, holds no cash and never trades. A fund that tracks it lives in the real world, so its return will differ from the index. FINRA notes that this deviation can affect investors negatively or positively.

## Why funds deviate from their index

- **Running costs:** the fund's TER is deducted from its assets every year (Category G).
- **Trading costs:** when index members change, the fund must buy and sell.
- **Cash:** dividends may sit in cash for a short time before they are reinvested or paid out.
- **Sampling:** funds that do not hold every index member can deviate a little.
- **Taxes on dividends:** withholding taxes on foreign dividends can affect the fund differently from the index assumptions.
- **Securities lending income:** can partly offset costs.

## Two ways to measure the gap

- **Tracking difference:** the difference between the fund's return and the index return over a period. This is what you actually gained or lost compared with the index.
- **Tracking error:** in the strict sense, how much that difference **fluctuates** over time (a measure of consistency). A fund can have a small tracking error but a consistently negative tracking difference.

In everyday use, and in some investor materials, "tracking error" is used loosely for any deviation from the index. When you read a figure, check which measure is meant.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> Over a year, an index returns **+8.0%**. The ETF tracking it returns **+7.8%**.
>
> Tracking difference = 7.8% − 8.0% = **−0.2 percentage points**. With a TER of 0.2%, the fund did roughly what you would expect.
>
> If another ETF on the same index returned +7.4%, its tracking difference of −0.6 points would cost you more, even if its TER looked similar.

## Why it matters

For index funds, the job is simple: follow the index as cheaply and closely as possible. Over many years, a few tenths of a percent per year add up because of compounding. When comparing funds on the same index, the **tracking difference over several years** often tells you more about real costs than the TER alone.

> [!MISTAKE] "The cheapest TER always means the best fund"
> The TER is an important clue, but not the full story. Trading costs, lending income and tax handling also affect the result. Look at the tracking difference over several years too.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Index funds deviate from their index because of costs, cash, trading, sampling and taxes.
> - Tracking difference = fund return − index return; tracking error = how much that gap varies.
> - Small yearly differences compound over time.
> - Compare funds on the same index by tracking difference over several years, not only by TER.
