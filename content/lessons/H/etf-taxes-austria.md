> [!VERIFY]
> This is a simplified educational overview for private investors resident in Austria who hold fund units as private assets. Tax law changes and individual situations differ. Check the current rules with the Federal Ministry of Finance, your bank or a tax adviser before acting. Category Q covers Austrian taxes in more detail.

## The tax rate

Income from funds, both actual distributions and income treated as distributed, is subject to the special tax rate of **27.5%** (§ 27a EStG), the same rate as for dividends and share price gains. When your securities account is held at a custodian in Austria, the tax is generally withheld as **capital gains tax (KESt)**.

## Three kinds of fund income

1. **Distributions:** cash the fund actually pays out. Taxable when paid.
2. **Deemed distributed income ("ausschüttungsgleiche Erträge"):** income the fund keeps and reinvests. Under § 186 InvFG, the fund's retained interest and dividends, plus **60% of the positive balance of its realised gains**, count as if they had been distributed to you, so they are taxed even though you received no cash. This applies to accumulating ETFs, and to distributing funds that do not pay out everything.
3. **Gain when you sell:** the difference between your selling price and your acquisition cost is taxed when you sell.

To avoid taxing the same income twice, deemed distributed income that has already been taxed **increases your acquisition cost** (§ 186 Abs. 3 InvFG), so the taxable gain when you sell is lower.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You bought accumulating ETF units for **€10,000**. For one year, the fund's reported deemed distributed income is **€200** for your units.
>
> - Tax on deemed income: €200 × 27.5% = **€55**, payable even though no cash was paid.
> - Your acquisition cost for tax purposes rises to **€10,200**.
> - If you later sell for €12,000, the taxable gain is €12,000 − €10,200 = **€1,800**, not €2,000.
>
> (Simplified: ignores fees, currency effects and later years.)

## Reporting funds and the OeKB

How does your bank know the deemed income? Funds can report their tax data to the **Oesterreichische Kontrollbank (OeKB)**, which is the official reporting office for fund tax data. The OeKB determines the tax treatment based on the Ministry of Finance's rules and publishes the data, which banks use to calculate KESt. Funds that do this are often called **reporting funds** ("Meldefonds").

## What if a fund does not report?

For funds without the required reports, § 186 Abs. 2 Z 3 InvFG sets out a flat-rate approach:

- Distributions are **fully taxable**.
- The deemed distributed income is **estimated at 90% of the difference between the first and last redemption price of the calendar year, but at least 10% of the year-end redemption price**, and treated as received on **31 December**.
- The holder can prove the actual amounts with the necessary documents.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A non-reporting fund's price is €100 at the start of the year and €101 at the end.
>
> - 90% of the difference: 90% × €1 = €0.90
> - Minimum: 10% × €101 = **€10.10**
>
> The higher figure applies: €10.10 per unit is treated as income and taxed, even though the price rose only €1. At 27.5%, that is about €2.78 of tax per unit.

This is why Austrian investors usually check whether a fund is a reporting fund before buying. The status of a fund can be looked up in the data the OeKB publishes.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> Under § 95 EStG, the party that withholds KESt on securities is generally the **domestic (Austrian) custodian** ("inländische depotführende Stelle"). If your account is held abroad and no Austrian KESt is withheld, you generally have to declare fund income yourself in your tax return, including deemed distributed income from accumulating funds. Category Q explains this in detail.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Fund income in Austria is taxed at 27.5%, including reinvested ("deemed distributed") income in accumulating funds.
> - Deemed income already taxed increases your acquisition cost, which reduces the taxable gain on sale.
> - Reporting funds send their tax data to the OeKB; banks use it to calculate KESt.
> - Non-reporting funds face a flat-rate estimate (at least 10% of the year-end price per year), which can be very costly.
> - Always verify current rules with official sources or a tax adviser.
