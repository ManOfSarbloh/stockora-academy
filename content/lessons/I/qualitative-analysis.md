Financial statements show where a company has been. Whether it will still earn good profits in ten years depends on things that do not appear in a table: what it sells, why customers choose it, how hard it is for rivals to copy, and who runs it. This is **qualitative analysis**.

## 1. Understand the business model

Start with plain questions. If you cannot answer them in a few sentences, you do not yet understand the company.

- **What does it sell, and to whom?** Consumers, businesses, governments?
- **How does it make money?** One-off sales, subscriptions, commissions, licences, interest?
- **What drives revenue?** Number of customers × price × how often they buy.
- **What are the main costs?** Materials, staff, energy, research?
- **How cyclical is it?** Do sales collapse in a recession, or do people keep buying?

The *Business* section of a 10-K (Investor.gov calls it a good place to start) or the business description in a European annual report answers many of these questions.

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad AG in one paragraph
> Our made-up Alpenrad AG designs and assembles bicycles and e-bikes in Austria and sells them through independent dealers in Europe. Revenue depends on the number of bikes sold and their average price; e-bikes are more expensive and more profitable. The main costs are components (often imported), staff and marketing. Demand is somewhat cyclical: people delay buying an expensive e-bike when money is tight.

## 2. Competitive advantage: the "moat"

In his 2007 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, Warren Buffett wrote that a truly great business must have an **enduring "moat"** that protects excellent returns on invested capital, because competitors will repeatedly attack any business that earns high returns. His examples of such barriers were being the **low-cost producer** or owning a **powerful worldwide brand**. He also warned that a moat which must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all.

Common types of competitive advantage:

| Type | Idea | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Brand | Customers trust it and pay more | Would customers switch for a slightly lower price? |
| Cost advantage | Scale or processes make it cheaper | Can a rival match its costs? |
| Switching costs | Changing supplier is painful | How costly is it for a customer to leave? |
| Network effects | Each new user makes it more valuable | Does the product get better as it grows? |
| Licences, patents | Legal protection | When do they expire? |

The numbers should confirm the story: a real moat usually shows up as **stable, high margins and ROIC over many years** (lesson 5). If the company claims a strong brand but its margins are falling, ask why.

## 3. The industry: five forces

Michael Porter's well-known framework (Harvard Business Review) says that profitability in an industry is shaped by **five forces**: rivalry among existing competitors, and four more: **customers**, **suppliers**, **potential new entrants** and **substitute products**. Industries where buyers are powerful, entry is easy and substitutes are plentiful tend to be tough places to earn high returns, however good the management.

> [!EXAMPLE] Five forces for Alpenrad
> - **Rivalry:** many bicycle brands in Europe. High.
> - **Customers:** dealers can choose between brands. Medium.
> - **Suppliers:** a few large component makers. Their power can be high.
> - **New entrants:** online brands can start without a dealer network. Medium.
> - **Substitutes:** cars, public transport, e-scooters. Medium.

## 4. Management and capital allocation

Look at what management does with the cash, not just what it says:

- **Track record:** did past targets and promises come true?
- **Capital allocation:** how is free cash flow used: reinvestment, acquisitions, dividends, buybacks, debt repayment? Did past acquisitions create or destroy value (look for goodwill impairments)?
- **Alignment:** do managers own meaningful amounts of shares? Is pay linked to long-term results or to short-term targets that can be gamed?
- **Communication:** is bad news reported openly and early, or hidden in footnotes?

## 5. Corporate governance

Governance is the system of checks on management: the supervisory board, auditors, and shareholder rights.

> [!AUSTRIA] The Austrian Corporate Governance Code
> The **Austrian Code of Corporate Governance** was presented to the public on 1 October 2002 and has been updated several times. It is a voluntary self-regulation framework, developed by the Austrian Working Group for Corporate Governance. For companies in the Vienna Stock Exchange's **prime market**, complying with a corporate governance code is a listing obligation. Austrian stock corporations whose shares are admitted to a regulated market must also prepare a **corporate governance report** that names the code they follow (§ 243c UGB). You usually find it with the annual report on the investor relations website.

Points to check: an independent supervisory board, clear disclosure of related-party transactions, fair treatment of minority shareholders (for example, dual-class structures, Category C), and an auditor without long-running controversies.

## 6. Risks and the bigger picture

- **Risk factors**: the 10-K lists significant risks, generally in order of importance (Investor.gov); EU management reports must describe the main risks and uncertainties (§ 124 BörseG 2018).
- **Dependence**: one big customer, one supplier, one country, one product?
- **Regulation and technology**: could a law or a new technology make the product obsolete?
- **Sustainability**: the management report may include a sustainability statement. Environmental and social issues can become financial risks, for example through carbon costs or supply chain problems.

> [!MISTAKE] Falling in love with the story
> A great product does not automatically make a great investment, and a great company can be a poor investment if the price already assumes perfection (lesson 8). Write down the facts that would prove your view wrong, and check them regularly.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Understand how the company makes money before looking at ratios.
> - A durable competitive advantage ("moat") should show up as high, stable returns over many years.
> - Industry structure (Porter's five forces) limits how profitable even a well-run company can be.
> - Judge management by its record and capital allocation, not its presentations.
> - In Austria, prime market companies must comply with a corporate governance code and report on it.
