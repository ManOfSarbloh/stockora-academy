A share price on its own says nothing about whether a company is expensive. A €200 share can be cheaper than a €5 share. **Valuation ratios** put the price in context by comparing it with what the company earns, owns or generates.

The examples use the made-up Alpenrad AG: share price **€24**, **20 million** shares, EPS €1.50, book value per share €10, revenue €500 m, EBITDA €80 m, EBIT €50 m, net debt €120 m, free cash flow €15 m, dividend €0.60 per share.

## Market capitalisation

**Market capitalisation** = share price × number of shares outstanding (Investor.gov). It is what the stock market currently values the company's **equity** at.

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's market cap
> €24 × 20 m = **€480 m**.

## Price-to-earnings (P/E)

**P/E = share price ÷ earnings per share**. The SEC's beginners' guide gives the example of a $20 share with $2 earnings per share: a P/E of 10, meaning the stock sells at 10 times earnings. In German it is the *Kurs-Gewinn-Verhältnis* (KGV).

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's P/E
> 24 ÷ 1.50 = **16**. Investors pay €16 for each €1 of last year's profit.

Variants you will see:

- **Trailing P/E** uses the last reported 12 months of earnings.
- **Forward P/E** uses analysts' forecast earnings for the next year. If analysts expect EPS of €1.62, Alpenrad's forward P/E is 24 ÷ 1.62 ≈ **14.8**. Forecasts can be wrong.
- **Earnings yield** is the P/E turned upside down: EPS ÷ price = 1.50 ÷ 24 = **6.25%**. It can be compared, carefully, with bond yields.

> [!WARNING] When the P/E misleads
> - **Losses:** with negative earnings the P/E has no meaning.
> - **Cyclical companies** (steel, chemicals, car makers) often show their **lowest** P/E at the peak of the cycle, when profits are temporarily high and about to fall, and a very high P/E at the bottom.
> - **One-off gains** inflate EPS and make the P/E look low.

## PEG ratio

The **PEG ratio** divides the P/E by the expected yearly earnings growth rate (in percent). It tries to account for the fact that faster-growing companies usually deserve a higher P/E.

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's PEG
> P/E 16 with expected growth of 8% a year → PEG = 16 ÷ 8 = **2.0**.

The PEG depends entirely on a growth forecast, which makes it fragile.

## Price-to-book (P/B)

**P/B = share price ÷ book value per share** (German: *Kurs-Buchwert-Verhältnis*, KBV). The Wiener Börse lexicon notes that it varies strongly by industry and business model and should be seen in relation to the company's profitability.

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's P/B
> 24 ÷ 10 = **2.4**.

P/B is most useful for companies whose assets are mostly financial and close to market value, such as banks and insurers. For software or brand companies, much of the value is not on the balance sheet at all (lesson 2), so P/B says little.

## Price-to-sales (P/S)

**P/S = market cap ÷ revenue**. Alpenrad: 480 ÷ 500 = **0.96**. It is used for companies that are not yet profitable. Its weakness: sales are not profits. A company can sell a lot and still lose money on every sale.

## Enterprise value and EV/EBITDA

Market cap values only the shares. A buyer of the whole company would also take over its debt and get its cash. **Enterprise value (EV)** captures this:

**EV = market value of equity + debt − cash**

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's enterprise value
> EV = 480 + 160 − 40 = **€600 m**.
> EV/EBITDA = 600 ÷ 80 = **7.5**
> EV/EBIT = 600 ÷ 50 = **12**
> EV/Sales = 600 ÷ 500 = **1.2**

EV-based ratios let you compare companies with different amounts of debt more fairly, because both sides of the ratio refer to the whole business (shareholders and lenders). Do not mix them up: **P/E belongs with market cap; EV/EBITDA belongs with EV**.

## Yields: cash back to you

- **Dividend yield** = dividend per share ÷ share price. Alpenrad: 0.60 ÷ 24 = **2.5%** (see Category G for details).
- **Free cash flow yield** = free cash flow ÷ market cap. Alpenrad: 15 ÷ 480 ≈ **3.1%**.

## Summary table

| Ratio | Formula | Alpenrad |
|---|---|---:|
| P/E (trailing) | price ÷ EPS | 16 |
| Forward P/E | price ÷ forecast EPS | 14.8 |
| Earnings yield | EPS ÷ price | 6.25% |
| PEG | P/E ÷ growth in % | 2.0 |
| P/B | price ÷ book value per share | 2.4 |
| P/S | market cap ÷ revenue | 0.96 |
| EV/EBITDA | EV ÷ EBITDA | 7.5 |
| Dividend yield | dividend ÷ price | 2.5% |
| FCF yield | FCF ÷ market cap | 3.1% |

## How to use ratios sensibly

1. **Compare like with like.** Industries differ hugely. Aswath Damodaran's industry tables (NYU Stern) show average P/E ratios differing several-fold between industries.
2. **Compare over time.** How does today's ratio compare with the company's own history?
3. **Ask why.** A low ratio can mean the market is too pessimistic, or that it correctly expects falling profits. A high ratio can mean strong expected growth, or excessive optimism.
4. **Use several ratios together.** Each one has blind spots.

> [!MISTAKE] "Low P/E means cheap, so buy"
> The Wiener Börse lexicon itself puts "cheaper" in quotation marks when explaining that a lower KGV means a share is cheaper. A low ratio can be a **value trap**: the business is shrinking, and the price is low for good reason. A ratio is a starting point for questions, never an answer.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Market cap = price × shares; EV = market cap + debt − cash.
> - P/E = price ÷ EPS; forward P/E uses forecasts; PEG divides by growth.
> - P/B suits banks and insurers better than asset-light companies.
> - Match ratios correctly: equity value with net income, EV with EBITDA or EBIT.
> - Compare within an industry and over time; a low ratio can be a value trap.
