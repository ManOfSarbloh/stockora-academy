The SEC's beginners' guide sums up the purpose of the **cash flow statement** neatly: while an income statement can tell you whether a company made a profit, a cash flow statement can tell you whether the company **generated cash**. A company needs cash to pay suppliers, staff, interest and dividends. Profits that never turn into cash cannot do that.

## The three parts

Under IFRS (IAS 7) and US GAAP, cash flows are split into three groups:

1. **Operating activities**: cash from the day-to-day business. Most companies start from net income and adjust it (the "indirect method"): they add back non-cash costs such as depreciation, and adjust for changes in working capital, such as more money tied up in inventory.
2. **Investing activities**: buying or selling long-term assets such as factories, machines and other companies, and investment securities. Buying a machine is a cash **outflow**; selling investments is an inflow.
3. **Financing activities**: cash from or to the providers of capital: issuing or buying back shares, borrowing or repaying loans, paying dividends.

The bottom line is the **net change in cash** for the period, which links to the cash figure on the balance sheet.

## Worked example: Alpenrad AG, 2025

| Line | € m |
|---|---:|
| Net income | 30 |
| + Depreciation and amortisation | 30 |
| − Increase in inventory | −15 |
| − Increase in receivables | −5 |
| + Increase in payables | 10 |
| **Cash flow from operating activities** | **50** |
| Purchase of property, plant and equipment (capex) | −35 |
| **Cash flow from investing activities** | **−35** |
| Dividends paid | −12 |
| Repayment of loans | −5 |
| **Cash flow from financing activities** | **−17** |
| **Net change in cash** | **−2** |

Cash fell from €42 m to €40 m, which matches the balance sheet in lesson 2.

Notice why operating cash flow (€50 m) differs from net income (€30 m): depreciation reduced profit but cost no cash this year (+30), while building up inventory and receivables tied up cash (−20), partly offset by paying suppliers later (+10).

## Free cash flow

**Free cash flow (FCF)** estimates the cash left after the business has paid for the investments it needs to keep running and growing. The simplest common version:

**Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capital expenditure (capex)**

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's free cash flow
> FCF = 50 − 35 = **€15 m**, or €0.75 per share (20 million shares).
>
> Alpenrad paid €12 m in dividends, so the dividend was covered by free cash flow, with €3 m to spare.

> [!WARNING] There is no single definition
> Free cash flow is not defined by IFRS. It is an **alternative performance measure**, and companies calculate it differently: some deduct lease payments, interest or acquisitions, some do not. Analysts also use more precise versions, such as *free cash flow to the firm* (before payments to lenders) and *free cash flow to equity* (after debt flows). Always check how a company defines the figure it reports.

## Quick health checks

**Cash conversion**: compare operating cash flow with net income over several years.

> [!EXAMPLE] Cash conversion
> Alpenrad: 50 ÷ 30 ≈ **1.7**. Operating cash flow was higher than profit, mainly because of depreciation.
>
> If, year after year, a company reports rising profits but operating cash flow stays flat or negative, ask why. Often receivables are growing: customers are not paying, or sales are being booked very aggressively.

**Typical patterns** (signs, not verdicts):

| Operating | Investing | Financing | Often means |
|---|---|---|---|
| + | − | − | Mature business funding investment, dividends and debt repayment from its own cash |
| + | − | + | Growing business that also borrows or raises equity to invest |
| − | − | + | Young or struggling business living on new money from investors or lenders |
| − | + | ± | Selling assets to stay afloat |

> [!MISTAKE] Treating any positive cash flow as good news
> A company can improve operating cash flow for one year by delaying payments to suppliers or by selling its receivables. Look at several years and at the working capital lines, not just the total.

> [!TIP] Capex compared with depreciation
> If capex is consistently well below depreciation, the company may be under-investing and its assets ageing. If capex is far above depreciation, it is expanding, which costs cash today in the hope of more profit later.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - The cash flow statement shows real cash moving in three groups: operating, investing, financing.
> - Operating cash flow adjusts net income for non-cash items and changes in working capital.
> - Free cash flow ≈ operating cash flow − capex; definitions vary, so check them.
> - Compare operating cash flow with net income over several years.
> - Dividends that are not covered by free cash flow have to be paid from cash reserves or borrowing.
