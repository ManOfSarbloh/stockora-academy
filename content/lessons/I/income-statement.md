The **income statement** (also called the profit and loss statement or P&L; German: *Gewinn- und Verlustrechnung*, GuV) shows how much money a company **earned and spent over a period**, usually a year or half a year. Unlike the balance sheet, it is a film rather than a photo.

## The staircase

The SEC's beginners' guide suggests picturing the income statement as a **set of stairs**. You start at the top with sales and walk down, subtracting one kind of cost at each step, until you reach the profit or loss at the bottom.

1. **Revenue** (sales, turnover): money from selling products or services. Returns and discounts are deducted to get *net* revenue.
2. **Cost of sales**: the direct cost of making what was sold (materials, factory wages).
3. = **Gross profit**.
4. **Operating expenses**: costs not linked directly to production, such as administration, marketing and research.
5. **Depreciation and amortisation**: the cost of long-lived assets spread over the years they are used.
6. = **Operating profit** (income from operations, often close to **EBIT**).
7. **Interest** income and expense.
8. = **Profit before tax**.
9. **Income tax**.
10. = **Net income** (net profit, net earnings): the "bottom line".

The order of lines can differ between companies and accounting standards, but the logic is always the same.

## Worked example: Alpenrad AG, financial year 2025

| Line | € m |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | 500 |
| Cost of sales | −300 |
| **Gross profit** | **200** |
| Selling, administration and research | −120 |
| Depreciation and amortisation | −30 |
| **Operating profit (EBIT)** | **50** |
| Net interest expense | −10 |
| **Profit before tax** | **40** |
| Income tax | −10 |
| **Net income** | **30** |

## Margins: profit per euro of sales

A **margin** expresses a profit line as a percentage of revenue. It lets you compare companies of different sizes, and the same company across years.

| Margin | Formula | Alpenrad |
|---|---|---:|
| Gross margin | gross profit ÷ revenue | 200 ÷ 500 = **40%** |
| Operating margin | operating profit ÷ revenue | 50 ÷ 500 = **10%** |
| Net margin | net income ÷ revenue | 30 ÷ 500 = **6%** |

The SEC guide defines the **operating margin** as income from operations divided by net revenues: for each euro of sales, the percentage that was operating profit.

> [!NOTE] Margins differ a lot by industry
> A supermarket chain and a software company have very different cost structures, so their "normal" margins are far apart. The SEC guide puts it simply: desirable ratios vary by industry. Compare a company with its own history and with similar businesses, never with a fixed "good" number.

## EBIT and EBITDA

- **EBIT** = earnings before interest and taxes. It roughly equals operating profit and shows what the business earns before the effects of how it is financed (interest) and taxed.
- **EBITDA** = earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. It adds depreciation and amortisation back to EBIT.

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's EBITDA
> EBITDA = EBIT 50 + depreciation and amortisation 30 = **€80 m**, an EBITDA margin of 80 ÷ 500 = **16%**.

EBITDA is popular because it is close to the cash the operating business generates before investment, and it is useful for comparing companies with different depreciation policies. But it has a big blind spot: **machines, buildings and software wear out and must be replaced**. Depreciation is the accounting estimate of that real cost. A company with heavy investment needs can show a high EBITDA and still produce little cash.

> [!WARNING] EBITDA is not defined by IFRS
> EBITDA is an **alternative performance measure (APM)**: a figure calculated by the company, not one defined by the accounting standards. ESMA names EBITDA as an example of an APM and expects companies to define such figures and reconcile them to the financial statements. Two companies' "EBITDA" may be calculated differently, so check the definition in the report.

## Earnings per share (EPS)

**EPS** is net income divided by the number of shares. Investor.gov defines it as a public company's net profit divided by the number of its common shares. The SEC guide explains it as the amount each share would receive if the company paid out all its profit, which companies almost never do.

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's EPS
> Net income €30 m ÷ 20 million shares = **€1.50 per share**.

Under IFRS (IAS 33), companies show two EPS figures with equal prominence:

- **Basic EPS** uses the weighted average number of shares outstanding during the period.
- **Diluted EPS** assumes that convertible bonds, options and similar instruments are turned into shares, which can reduce EPS. A large gap between basic and diluted EPS tells you that existing shareholders may be diluted in the future.

## Things to watch

- **Growth source.** Did revenue grow because more was sold, because prices rose, or because the company bought another company? The management report usually explains this.
- **One-off items.** A sale of a building can inflate profit once. A big restructuring charge can depress it once. Look at what is likely to repeat.
- **Profit is not cash.** Revenue is often recorded when goods are delivered, not when the customer pays. The next lesson shows how to check whether profits turn into cash.

> [!MISTAKE] Comparing EPS across companies
> EPS depends on the number of shares, which is arbitrary: a company with 10 million shares and one with 100 million shares can have identical profits. EPS is useful for following **one** company over time, or as an input to the P/E ratio, not for comparing two companies directly.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - The income statement covers a period: revenue at the top, net income at the bottom.
> - Gross, operating and net margins show profit per euro of sales; compare within an industry.
> - EBIT ≈ operating profit; EBITDA adds back depreciation and amortisation and is not an IFRS figure.
> - EPS = net income ÷ shares; watch the difference between basic and diluted EPS.
> - Profit is an accounting result; cash is checked in the cash flow statement.
