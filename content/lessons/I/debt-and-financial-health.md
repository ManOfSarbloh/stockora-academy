Debt is not bad in itself. Borrowing lets a company build a factory today and pay for it from future profits, and lesson 5 showed that debt can raise the return on equity. The danger is that **interest and repayments are due whether business is good or bad**. Shareholders are paid last: if a company cannot pay its lenders, its shares can lose most or all of their value.

The examples use the made-up Alpenrad AG: total liabilities €300 m, equity €200 m, interest-bearing debt €160 m, cash €40 m, EBITDA €80 m, EBIT €50 m, net interest expense €10 m, current assets €200 m (including €100 m of inventory), current liabilities €120 m.

## Leverage ratios

**Debt-to-equity.** The SEC's beginners' guide defines it as **total liabilities ÷ shareholders' equity**. Many analysts instead use only interest-bearing (financial) debt. Both versions exist, so check which one a source uses.

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's debt-to-equity
> Total liabilities version: 300 ÷ 200 = **1.5**
> Financial debt version: 160 ÷ 200 = **0.8**

**Net debt to EBITDA** compares debt after cash with a rough measure of yearly operating cash generation. It answers: *how many years of EBITDA would it take to repay the net debt?*

> [!EXAMPLE] Net debt to EBITDA
> Net debt = 160 − 40 = €120 m. Net debt ÷ EBITDA = 120 ÷ 80 = **1.5 years**.

Net debt and EBITDA are both alternative performance measures, not IFRS figures (ESMA lists both as examples), so companies may calculate them differently. Lenders often write a maximum ratio into loan agreements (see covenants below).

**Equity ratio** = equity ÷ total assets. In German-speaking countries this *Eigenkapitalquote* is a common measure. Alpenrad: 200 ÷ 500 = **40%**.

## Can it pay the interest?

**Interest coverage** = EBIT ÷ interest expense. It shows how many times operating profit covers the interest bill.

> [!EXAMPLE] Alpenrad's interest coverage
> 50 ÷ 10 = **5 times**. Operating profit could fall by 80% before it no longer covers interest.

Some sources use after-tax operating profit in the numerator (Damodaran's data does), so the same company can show different coverage numbers in different databases.

## Liquidity: the next twelve months

Liquidity ratios look at the short term:

- **Current ratio** = current assets ÷ current liabilities. Alpenrad: 200 ÷ 120 ≈ **1.67**.
- **Quick ratio** = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities. It leaves out inventory, which may be hard to sell quickly. Alpenrad: (200 − 100) ÷ 120 ≈ **0.83**.
- **Inventory turnover** (SEC guide) = cost of sales ÷ average inventory. Alpenrad's inventory rose from €85 m to €100 m, so the average is €92.5 m: 300 ÷ 92.5 ≈ **3.2 times a year**. A falling turnover can mean products are not selling.

> [!NOTE] There are no universal "safe" levels
> Utilities with stable, regulated income often carry much more debt than technology companies with uncertain income. The SEC guide notes that desirable ratios vary by industry. Compare with similar companies and with the company's own history.

## Look in the notes

The headline ratios are a start. The notes add the details that matter in a crisis:

- **Maturity profile.** When is the debt due? A large bond due next year must be refinanced, perhaps at a much higher interest rate. This is **refinancing risk**.
- **Fixed or floating rate.** Floating-rate debt becomes more expensive when interest rates rise.
- **Covenants.** Conditions attached to loans and bonds, such as a maximum net debt to EBITDA. Breaking them can make the debt payable early.
- **Leases.** Under IFRS 16, companies show most leases (longer than 12 months and not of low value) on the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a **lease liability**. Check whether a company's "net debt" includes lease liabilities; definitions differ.
- **Pensions.** An under-funded pension plan is a debt-like obligation.
- **Off-balance-sheet commitments** and guarantees.

> [!WARNING] Debt hurts most when everything goes wrong at once
> A recession can cut profits just as banks become less willing to lend. A company with high debt, short maturities and floating rates can get into trouble quickly, even if it was profitable the year before. Shareholders bear the first losses.

> [!MISTAKE] Looking only at the debt total
> €500 m of debt can be harmless for a company with stable cash flows and long fixed-rate maturities, and dangerous for a smaller company with volatile profits and a large bond due next year. Look at the ability to pay (coverage, cash flow) and the timing (maturities), not just the amount.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Debt raises returns in good times and risk in bad times; shareholders are paid last.
> - Debt-to-equity has two common versions: total liabilities or financial debt only.
> - Net debt ÷ EBITDA and interest coverage show the ability to carry the debt.
> - Current and quick ratios look at the next twelve months.
> - Check the notes for maturities, floating rates, covenants, leases and pensions.
