**Fundamental analysis** asks a simple question: *what is this business actually worth, and how healthy is it?* Instead of studying the price chart (that is technical analysis, Category J), it studies the company itself: its sales, profits, cash, debts, competitors and management.

This category teaches you to read the numbers and to understand the common ratios and valuation methods. It does **not** tell you which shares to buy. Even a careful analysis can be wrong, because the future is uncertain.

> [!NOTE] One example company for the whole category
> To keep things concrete, every lesson in this category uses the same **made-up** company: **Alpenrad AG**, an imaginary Austrian bicycle maker. Its numbers are invented for teaching and are kept simple. Real reports are longer and messier, but they follow the same logic.

## The four financial statements

The SEC's beginners' guide describes **four main financial statements**:

1. The **balance sheet** shows what a company owns and what it owes at a fixed point in time.
2. The **income statement** shows how much money it made and spent over a period.
3. The **cash flow statement** shows the money flowing between the company and the outside world over a period.
4. The **statement of shareholders' equity** shows how the owners' stake changed over time.

The next three lessons cover the first three in detail, because those are the ones investors use most.

## What is inside an annual report

A listed company's annual report is much more than the four statements. The typical parts:

| Part | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Letter from the CEO or management board | Management's message. Often upbeat; read it last, not first. |
| **Management report** (EU) or **MD&A** (US) | Management's explanation of the results, trends and main risks. |
| Financial statements | The audited numbers. |
| **Notes** to the financial statements | Accounting methods, details behind each line, debts, pensions, share-based pay, and much more. |
| Auditor's report | The independent auditor's opinion on whether the statements give a true and fair view. |
| Responsibility statement | Management confirms that the report is, to the best of its knowledge, a fair picture. |

The SEC guide makes a point worth remembering: **read the footnotes**. The notes explain the accounting policies that need management's most difficult judgements, and they often contain the information that matters most.

In the US, the equivalent of an annual report filed with the regulator is the **Form 10-K**. Investor.gov describes its main sections: *Business* (what the company does), *Risk Factors* (significant risks, generally in order of importance), *MD&A* (management's view of the past year) and *Financial Statements and Supplementary Data* (the audited statements).

> [!AUSTRIA] Annual financial reports under the Stock Exchange Act
> Companies whose shares are listed on a regulated market in Austria must publish an **annual financial report** (*Jahresfinanzbericht*) **within four months** after the end of each financial year and keep it publicly available for **at least ten years** (§ 124 BörseG 2018). It contains the audited financial statements, the management report (*Lagebericht*) and the responsibility statements of the legal representatives.
>
> A **half-yearly financial report** must follow within **three months** after the end of the first six months (§ 125 BörseG 2018). Since 26 November 2015 there has been no general legal obligation to publish **quarterly reports** (FMA). Under the Vienna Stock Exchange's current prime market rules, companies may choose whether, and in what form, to publish reports for the first and third quarters.

## Which accounting rules apply

The numbers in a report depend on the accounting rules used:

- **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards): since financial years starting on or after 1 January 2005, EU companies whose securities trade on a regulated market must prepare their **consolidated** (group) accounts using IFRS as adopted by the EU (Regulation (EC) No 1606/2002).
- **US GAAP**: used by US companies in their SEC filings.
- **National rules**, such as the Austrian Commercial Code (UGB), apply to many individual company accounts.

The rules are similar in spirit but differ in details, so the same business can show slightly different numbers under different standards. When you compare two companies, check which standards they use.

> [!NOTE] A change coming in 2027
> The IASB's new standard **IFRS 18** replaces IAS 1 for annual reporting periods beginning on or after **1 January 2027**. It adds two defined subtotals to the income statement, **operating profit** and **profit before financing and income taxes**, and requires companies to explain "management-defined performance measures" they use in public communication. In the EU, a standard applies once it has been adopted by the EU, so check the company's notes for which version it applies.

## Consolidated or individual accounts?

A large company is usually a **group**: a parent company that owns subsidiaries. **Consolidated** accounts combine the whole group as if it were one company. These are the accounts most investors use, because the share price reflects the whole group. If the parent prepares consolidated accounts, the Austrian annual financial report must contain both the consolidated and the parent's own accounts (§ 124 (2) BörseG 2018).

## Where to find reports

- **Investor relations (IR) page** of the company's website: annual and half-year reports, presentations, and the financial calendar with upcoming report dates.
- **SEC EDGAR** for companies that file with the US SEC: a free full-text search across filings such as the 10-K.
- **Electronic format:** since financial years starting on or after 1 January 2020, annual financial reports of issuers under § 124 BörseG must be prepared in the **European Single Electronic Format (ESEF)**, using Inline XBRL, so the numbers are machine-readable (FMA).

> [!TIP] Read in this order
> 1. Business description: do you understand how the company makes money?
> 2. Risk factors or the risk section of the management report.
> 3. The three main statements, comparing at least two years.
> 4. The notes behind any line that surprised you.
> 5. The auditor's report: is it a clean ("unqualified") opinion?
> 6. Only then the CEO letter and the investor presentation.

> [!MISTAKE] Reading only the press release
> Results press releases and investor presentations are written by the company to present results in a good light. They often highlight adjusted figures. The audited statements and their notes are the reference. Lesson 10 covers adjusted figures in detail.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Fundamental analysis studies the business: sales, profits, cash, debts, competition and management.
> - The four statements: balance sheet, income statement, cash flow statement, statement of shareholders' equity.
> - In Austria, listed companies publish an annual financial report within four months and a half-yearly report within three months.
> - EU-listed groups use IFRS for their consolidated accounts; US companies use US GAAP.
> - Read the notes and the auditor's report, not just the press release.
