The **balance sheet** (German: *Bilanz*) is a **snapshot** of a company on one date, usually the last day of the financial year. It lists what the company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and what is left for the owners (shareholders' equity).

## The accounting equation

Every balance sheet follows one rule:

**Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity**

The two sides must always be equal, or "balance". This is not a coincidence: everything a company owns was paid for either with borrowed money (liabilities) or with the owners' money (equity, including profits kept in the business).

- **Assets** are things of value the company owns or controls: cash, money customers still owe it (receivables), inventory, factories, machines, and intangible things such as patents, brands and software.
- **Liabilities** are amounts the company owes: bank loans, bonds, money owed to suppliers (payables), wages, taxes, and obligations to deliver goods or services that customers have already paid for.
- **Shareholders' equity** is what would be left if the company sold all its assets at their balance sheet values and paid off all its liabilities. The SEC guide notes that it is sometimes called capital or net worth.

## Current and non-current

Both sides are split by time:

- **Current assets** are expected to turn into cash within one year (cash, receivables, inventory). **Non-current assets** take longer or are used to run the business (buildings, machines, intangibles).
- **Current liabilities** are due within one year. **Long-term (non-current) liabilities** are due later.

## Worked example: Alpenrad AG

Alpenrad AG is our made-up Austrian bicycle maker. Its balance sheet at 31 December 2025, in € millions:

| Assets | € m | Liabilities and equity | € m |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cash | 40 | Payables to suppliers | 70 |
| Receivables | 60 | Short-term bank debt | 30 |
| Inventory | 100 | Other current liabilities | 20 |
| **Current assets** | **200** | **Current liabilities** | **120** |
| Property, plant and equipment | 220 | Long-term loans and bonds | 130 |
| Intangible assets | 80 | Provisions and other | 50 |
| **Non-current assets** | **300** | **Total liabilities** | **300** |
| | | **Shareholders' equity** | **200** |
| **Total assets** | **500** | **Total liabilities and equity** | **500** |

Check: 300 + 200 = 500. It balances.

## Numbers you can calculate

**Working capital** = current assets − current liabilities. The SEC guide describes it as the money left over if a company paid its current liabilities from its current assets.

> [!EXAMPLE] Working capital and the current ratio
> Alpenrad's working capital = 200 − 120 = **€80 m**.
>
> The **current ratio** = current assets ÷ current liabilities = 200 ÷ 120 ≈ **1.67**. Alpenrad has about €1.67 of short-term assets for each €1 of short-term obligations.

**Book value** is another name for shareholders' equity. Divided by the number of shares, it gives **book value per share**.

> [!EXAMPLE] Book value per share
> Alpenrad has 20 million shares. Book value per share = €200 m ÷ 20 m = **€10**.
>
> If the share trades at €24, the market values the company at 2.4 times its book value. Lesson 7 explains this price-to-book ratio.

**Net debt** = interest-bearing debt − cash. It shows how much debt would remain if the company used all its cash to repay it.

> [!EXAMPLE] Net debt
> Interest-bearing debt = short-term bank debt 30 + long-term loans and bonds 130 = €160 m.
> Net debt = 160 − 40 cash = **€120 m**.

## What the balance sheet does not show

- **It is one moment in time.** The SEC guide stresses that it shows the position at the end of the period, not the flows during it. A company can tidy up its cash position just before the reporting date.
- **Book values are not market values.** A factory bought 30 years ago may be on the books at a low value. A brand built over decades by advertising may not appear at all: under IFRS, internally generated brands, customer lists and similar items are not recognised as intangible assets (IAS 38).
- **Goodwill.** When a company buys another company for more than the value of its identifiable net assets, the rest of the price is recorded as **goodwill**, an intangible asset (IFRS 3). Under IAS 36, goodwill is tested for **impairment**: if the acquired business is now worth less than its carrying amount, goodwill is written down, which reduces profit and equity.

> [!MISTAKE] "Negative equity means bankruptcy"
> Not necessarily. Some profitable companies have negative book equity because they paid out more in dividends and share buybacks than they earned in profit over the years. Negative equity is a reason to look closer, not an automatic verdict. Equally, a large positive equity figure does not guarantee safety if the assets are hard to sell.

> [!TIP] Compare at least two dates
> A single balance sheet tells you little. Compare this year with last year: did debt rise faster than assets? Did inventory pile up while sales stayed flat? Changes are often more revealing than levels.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Assets = liabilities + shareholders' equity, always.
> - Current means within one year; non-current means longer.
> - Working capital = current assets − current liabilities; book value = shareholders' equity.
> - Net debt = interest-bearing debt − cash.
> - Book values are historical accounting values, not what the business would sell for.
