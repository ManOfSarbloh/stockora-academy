Most companies report honestly. But accounting involves many judgements, and management has reasons to present results in the best light. This lesson shows where to look more carefully.

## Adjusted figures: alternative performance measures

Results announcements are full of figures such as "adjusted EBITDA", "underlying profit", "core earnings" or "free cash flow". These are not defined by the accounting standards. ESMA, the EU's securities markets authority, calls them **alternative performance measures (APMs)**: financial measures of performance, financial position or cash flows **other than** those defined in the applicable financial reporting framework. Its examples include EBITDA, operating earnings, earnings before one-time charges and net debt. Revenue, profit and earnings per share are *not* APMs, because the standards define them.

APMs can be genuinely useful, for example to show the trend without a one-off gain. But they can also be used to make results look better. ESMA's guidelines (2015) expect companies that use APMs to:

- **define** each APM and how it is calculated, with a meaningful label;
- **reconcile** it to the closest figure in the financial statements, explaining the adjustments;
- **not display it more prominently** than the figures from the financial statements;
- **not mislabel items as non-recurring**: ESMA notes that items such as restructuring costs or impairment losses that affected past periods and will affect future periods will rarely be non-recurring;
- show **comparatives** for previous periods and keep the definition **consistent over time**, explaining any change.

> [!EXAMPLE] "One-off" costs every year
> Alpenrad AG (our made-up company) reports EBIT of €50 m. Its press release headlines "adjusted EBIT" of €62 m, excluding €12 m of "one-off restructuring costs".
>
> Look at the last five reports. If there were "one-off" restructuring costs in four of the five years, they are part of running this business. The honest comparison is the unadjusted €50 m.

> [!TIP] Always read the reconciliation
> Find the table that bridges the adjusted figure to the IFRS figure. Ask of each adjustment: is it really unusual? Does it consistently make results look better? Is share-based pay (a real cost to shareholders, because it dilutes them) being excluded?

## Accounting red flags

None of these proves wrongdoing. Each is a reason to ask questions:

**Profits and cash diverge**
- Net income rises year after year, but operating cash flow does not (lesson 4).
- Receivables grow much faster than revenue: are customers really paying?
- Inventory piles up faster than sales.

**Accounting choices**
- Changes in accounting policies or estimates that happen to raise profit, such as longer useful lives for assets (less depreciation).
- Large amounts of costs **capitalised** as assets instead of being expensed. Under IAS 38, research costs are expenses, and development costs can be capitalised only if specific criteria are met, so compare how similar companies treat them.
- Growing goodwill from many acquisitions, which makes underlying growth hard to see.

**Governance and process**
- A change of auditor, especially after disagreements, or an audit opinion that is not clean (qualified, adverse or a disclaimer), or a "going concern" warning.
- Late publication: in Austria the annual financial report is due within four months of the year end (§ 124 BörseG 2018). Delays are a serious warning sign.
- Large transactions with **related parties**, such as companies owned by managers.
- Complex structures, with many subsidiaries in places where information is hard to check.
- Management that attacks critics instead of answering their questions.

**Signals from the company itself**
- A **profit warning** (*Gewinnwarnung*): an ad hoc announcement that profit will be lower, or the loss larger, than previously forecast.
- Repeatedly missing its own forecasts.

## Case study: Wirecard (2020)

Wirecard was a German payments company that rose into the **DAX 30** index. ESMA's peer review (November 2020) summarises what happened:

- Over the years, allegations of accounting irregularities were raised several times, including by the media and short sellers, as the company grew rapidly. The company **systematically rejected** them.
- On **22 June 2020**, Wirecard announced that **€1.9 billion** it had claimed to hold in escrow accounts in the Philippines **did not actually exist**. Its auditors had not finalised their audit report before the deadline for the annual financial report.
- Wirecard **filed for insolvency** the following week. Shareholders suffered huge losses.
- ESMA's review found **deficiencies** in how the German enforcers, BaFin and the Financial Reporting Enforcement Panel (FREP), had examined Wirecard's financial reporting.

Lessons for investors:

1. **Cash can be faked too.** The missing money was supposed to be cash, the "safest" asset on a balance sheet. Ask where the cash is held and whether it is ever used.
2. **Index membership and a big market value are no guarantee.** Wirecard was in Germany's leading index.
3. **Persistent, specific allegations deserve attention**, especially when the company answers with attacks rather than evidence.
4. **A delayed audit is a major warning.**
5. **Diversification** limits the damage any single fraud can do to your portfolio (Category M).

> [!AUSTRIA] Who checks financial reports in Austria
> Since 1 July 2013, the financial reports of companies listed on a regulated market with Austria as home member state are reviewed under the Accounting Control Act (*Rechnungslegungs-Kontrollgesetz*, RL-KG). Reviews are generally carried out by the **Austrian Financial Reporting Enforcement Panel (OePR)**, an independent, non-profit association recognised by the Ministry of Finance, while the **FMA** acts as the authority. These reviews take place after the reports are published and cannot check everything, so they reduce but cannot eliminate the risk of errors or fraud.

> [!WARNING] Fraud is rare, but it can wipe out an investment
> If a company's numbers are false, every ratio and every DCF in the previous lessons is worthless. You cannot audit a company yourself. You can notice warning signs, avoid putting too much of your money in any single company, and be wary of stories that seem too good to be true.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - APMs such as adjusted EBITDA are not defined by IFRS; ESMA expects definitions, reconciliations and consistency.
> - "One-off" costs that appear every year are normal costs.
> - Red flags: profits without cash, rising receivables, accounting changes, auditor problems, late reports, related-party deals.
> - Wirecard showed that even reported cash and DAX membership are no guarantee.
> - In Austria, the OePR and the FMA review listed companies' financial reports.
