In Category A you learned that "the stock market" is a network, and that the stock exchange is its best-known part. This topic looks inside: how trading actually works, what happens after a trade, and which rules keep the market orderly.

## What an exchange does

A stock exchange has a few core jobs:

1. **Listing:** it decides which companies' shares may be traded, based on admission rules, and supervises their ongoing duties.
2. **Matching:** it collects buy and sell orders in an **order book** and matches them by fixed, non-discretionary rules.
3. **Price discovery:** by bringing many orders together, it produces prices that reflect the current balance of supply and demand.
4. **Transparency:** it publishes prices and trading volumes, so everyone can see what happened.
5. **Orderly trading:** it has safeguards, such as auctions and volatility interruptions, to stop prices from jumping chaotically.

In EU terms, a classic stock exchange is a **regulated market**: a multilateral system that brings together many buyers and sellers under non-discretionary rules, and that must be authorised.

## Primary and secondary market, again

- The **primary market** is where new shares are issued and the company receives money (for example, in an IPO).
- The **secondary market** is where existing shares change hands between investors. This is where almost all everyday trading happens.

An exchange plays a role in both: it hosts IPOs and then provides the secondary market.

## From trading floor to computer

> [!AUSTRIA]
> The Vienna Stock Exchange (Wiener Börse) was founded in **1771** by Empress Maria Theresa, originally to help raise capital for the state; at first, bonds, bills of exchange and currencies were traded. The first share on its list was that of the Austrian central bank in 1818, and one of its first shareholders was Ludwig van Beethoven, who bought eight shares in 1819. Today, trading in Vienna is fully electronic.

For centuries, exchanges were physical places where traders met and shouted orders. Today, almost all trading is electronic: orders arrive over networks, a computer (the **matching engine**) applies the rules, and trades happen in fractions of a second. In Vienna, trading in liquid shares combines **continuous trading**, where orders are matched in a central order book by price-time priority, with **auctions** at set times, such as the opening and closing auctions.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> When you place an order in your broker's app, you are not talking to the exchange directly. Your broker, which is a member of the exchange (or works with one), sends your order into the exchange's system. There it waits in the order book until it can be matched, or is executed immediately if there is a matching order.

> [!MISTAKE] "The exchange decides what a share is worth"
> The exchange runs the rules and the computers. Prices come from the orders of buyers and sellers. The exchange's job is to make sure that process is fair, orderly and transparent.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Exchanges list companies, match orders, produce prices, publish data and keep trading orderly.
> - In the EU, a classic stock exchange is a "regulated market" with non-discretionary rules.
> - Everyday trading happens on the secondary market, between investors.
> - Trading today is electronic; the Vienna Stock Exchange, founded in 1771, is fully electronic.
