**Liquidity** describes how quickly and easily you can buy or sell a security **without substantially moving its price**. A liquid share has many buyers and sellers, tight spreads and large quantities in the order book. An illiquid share has few, so trading it is slower and more expensive.

## Signs of liquidity

| Liquid share | Illiquid share |
|---|---|
| Narrow spread (e.g. €50.00 / €50.02) | Wide spread (e.g. €8.10 / €8.60) |
| Many shares offered at each price | Few shares at each price |
| Large daily trading volume | Days with hardly any trades |
| A normal order hardly moves the price | Even a small order can move the price a lot |

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You want to sell **2,000 shares** quickly.
>
> - In a liquid share, buyers for 2,000 shares are waiting at or very close to the best bid, so you get close to the quoted price.
> - In an illiquid share, only 300 shares are bid at €8.10, the next 500 at €7.90, the rest at €7.50. Selling all 2,000 at once pushes the price down and you get a much lower average price, or you have to wait.

## Market makers

A **market maker** is a firm that stands ready to buy or sell a particular security at publicly quoted prices. It continuously posts both a bid and an ask, so there is (almost) always someone to trade with. It earns money from the difference between the prices at which it buys and sells, and must manage the risk of holding shares that may move in price.

Exchanges often support market making, especially for less-traded shares, because it improves liquidity for everyone. In Vienna, for example, the presence of liquidity providers such as market makers is one of the factors the exchange considers when setting the price corridors for volatility interruptions.

## Why liquidity matters to you

- **Cost:** the spread is a hidden cost every time you trade. Wide spreads eat into returns, especially if you trade often.
- **Exit:** in a crisis, illiquid shares can be very hard to sell at a reasonable price.
- **Fraud risk:** low liquidity makes it easier for manipulators to move prices, a reason why the SEC warns that microcap stocks can be difficult to sell.

> [!MISTAKE] "I can always sell at the price on the screen"
> Only if there are enough buyers at that price. In illiquid shares, the displayed price may be based on a small trade hours ago, and selling a meaningful amount can push the price down.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Liquidity is how easily you can trade without moving the price.
> - Signs of liquidity: narrow spreads, deep order books, high trading volume.
> - Market makers always quote a bid and an ask and earn the spread.
> - Illiquid shares cost more to trade and can be hard to sell when you need to.
