Most orders on today's exchanges are not typed in by people. They are generated by computer programs: **algorithmic trading**. A special and much-discussed form of it is **high-frequency trading (HFT)**: trading at extremely high speed, with very large numbers of orders.

## What makes trading "high-frequency"

EU law (MiFID II) defines a high-frequency algorithmic trading technique as algorithmic trading characterised by:

- **infrastructure built to minimise delays (latency)**, including at least one of: **co-location** (placing the firm's computers in the exchange's data centre), **proximity hosting**, or high-speed direct electronic access;
- systems that decide on orders **without human intervention** for individual trades; and
- **very high message rates** during the day: orders, quotes and cancellations.

According to SEC testimony from 2009, HFT strategies typically involve a large number of orders and also a large number of cancellations, often within fractions of a second.

## What HFT firms do

Typical strategies include:

- **Market making:** constantly quoting buy and sell prices and earning tiny spreads, many thousands of times a day.
- **Arbitrage:** exploiting tiny price differences for the same share on different venues, or between related instruments.
- **Reacting to information:** trading on news or order flow faster than others.

## The debate

| Arguments for | Arguments against |
|---|---|
| Narrower spreads and more quotes, which can lower trading costs for everyone | A speed "arms race" that benefits those who can pay for the fastest technology |
| Prices across venues stay aligned | Liquidity can vanish in stress: orders are cancelled in an instant |
| Competition in market making | Risk that fast traders profit from detecting and trading ahead of large orders |

The SEC's 2009 testimony noted both sides: HFT can play a constructive role, for example by providing liquidity and helping to reduce spreads, but a high-frequency trader may also be able to profit from trading ahead of a large order. Rapid, automated trading has also been linked to sudden, extreme price swings such as the "flash crash" of 6 May 2010 in the US (Category K).

> [!NOTE]
> Regulators have responded with rules. The EU's MiFID II, for example, contains a specific definition of HFT (above) so that firms using it can be identified and supervised, and exchanges use safeguards such as volatility interruptions (see the lesson on circuit breakers).

> [!MISTAKE] "I can beat high-frequency traders by being quick on my app"
> A retail investor cannot compete on speed with machines placed next to the exchange's servers, and should not try. Long-term investors are mostly unaffected by microsecond differences; what matters for them are costs, diversification and time.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Algorithmic trading uses computer programs to place orders; HFT is its fastest form.
> - EU law defines HFT by low-latency infrastructure, automated decisions and very high message rates.
> - HFT can narrow spreads but raises concerns about fairness and stability under stress.
> - Retail investors should not try to compete on speed.
