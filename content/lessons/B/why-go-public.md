"Going public" means that a company's shares become available to the general public and are traded on a stock exchange. The classic way is an **initial public offering (IPO)**: the first time a company offers its shares to the general public.

## Why companies go public

- **To raise money:** an IPO can bring in a large amount of new capital to fund growth.
- **To let early investors sell:** founders, employees and venture capital funds get a market where they can eventually sell their shares.
- **To use shares as a currency:** a listed company can pay for acquisitions partly with its own shares.
- **Visibility and trust:** being listed raises a company's profile with customers, partners and future employees.

## The downsides

- **Cost:** banks, lawyers, auditors and the exchange all charge fees, and being listed costs money every year.
- **Disclosure:** listed companies must publish regular reports and important news promptly, also to their competitors.
- **Pressure:** the share price is visible every day, and investors watch every quarter.
- **Less control:** new shareholders vote, and the company can become a takeover target.

## Routes onto the stock market

| Route | How it works | New money for the company? |
|---|---|---|
| **IPO** | Banks (underwriters) help the company sell new and/or existing shares to investors at a set price, then the shares start trading | Yes, if new shares are sold |
| **Direct listing** | Existing shares are simply admitted to trading; the opening price is found by the market. Some exchanges also allow a version with new shares | Traditionally no |
| **SPAC** | A shell company with no business of its own first goes public, raises money and then merges with a private company (the "de-SPAC") | Yes, via the SPAC |
| **Reverse merger** | A private company merges into an existing listed shell company and takes it over | Usually no |
| **Spin-off** | A listed company separates a division and gives its shares to its own shareholders | No |
| **Privatisation** | A government sells shares of a state-owned company to the public | Goes to the government |

> [!WARNING]
> The SEC warns that in a SPAC, investors rely heavily on the sponsors who set it up, whose financial interests can differ from those of ordinary shareholders. With reverse mergers, the SEC notes that information about the company may be limited. In both cases, there is no traditional IPO process with its checks.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> The Vienna Stock Exchange offers different segments for different companies: the **prime market** and **standard market** for shares admitted to the Official Market, and the **direct market plus** and **direct market** on the Vienna MTF, which are aimed at smaller and younger companies.

> [!MISTAKE] "If a company goes public, it must be doing great"
> Going public is a business decision that also benefits the sellers: founders and early investors often sell part of their shares in the IPO. An IPO tells you the company wants public money and believes investors will pay its price, not that it is a good investment.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Going public brings money, liquidity for early owners and visibility, at the cost of fees, disclosure and pressure.
> - The classic route is an IPO with underwriting banks.
> - Direct listings, SPACs, reverse mergers, spin-offs and privatisations are other routes, each with its own risks.
> - An IPO is not a quality seal; read the prospectus and remember that sellers know more than buyers.
