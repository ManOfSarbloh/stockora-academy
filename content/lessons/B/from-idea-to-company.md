Every listed share started as somebody's idea: a recipe, an app, a machine, a shop. But an idea cannot issue shares. First, the people behind it have to choose a **legal form** for their business, and that choice decides whether shares can exist at all.

## Sole trader or company?

The simplest way to run a business is on your own account, as a **sole trader** (in Austria: Einzelunternehmen). It is quick to start, but there is no separation between you and the business: you are personally liable for all its debts, and there are no shares to sell to anyone.

A **corporation** (in German: Kapitalgesellschaft) is different. It is a separate **legal person**: it can own things, sign contracts, borrow money and be sued in its own name. Its owners put in money, called **share capital**, and in return they own the company. In general, the company itself is liable for its debts, not its owners.

That separation is what makes shares possible: ownership of the company can be divided into pieces and passed on to other people.

## Common company forms

| Country | Smaller company | Company that can be listed on an exchange |
|---|---|---|
| Austria | GmbH (Gesellschaft mit beschränkter Haftung) | AG (Aktiengesellschaft) |
| Germany | GmbH | AG |
| Switzerland | GmbH | AG |
| United Kingdom | Ltd (private limited company) | plc (public limited company) |
| France | SARL | SA |
| USA | LLC | Corporation ("Inc."), often a so-called C-corporation |

The details differ by country, but the pattern is the same: there is usually a lighter form for smaller businesses and a stricter form whose shares can be offered to the public and traded on a stock exchange.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> - A **GmbH** needs a share capital (Stammkapital) of at least **€10,000**, and in principle half of it must be paid in cash when the company is founded. Its ownership stakes are not traded on a stock exchange.
> - An **AG** needs a share capital (Grundkapital) of at least **€70,000**, divided into shares (Aktien).
> - Both only come into existence when they are entered in the **Firmenbuch** (commercial register).
>
> The Vienna Stock Exchange lists the transformation of a company into an AG as one of the first steps for a company that wants to go public.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> In the United Kingdom, a public limited company (plc) must have allotted share capital with a nominal value of at least **£50,000** (or a prescribed euro equivalent of **€57,100**) before it can start trading.

> [!VERIFY]
> Minimum capital amounts and founding rules change from time to time (Austria lowered the GmbH minimum to €10,000 from 2024). Always check the official source of the country concerned before founding a company.

## Why the legal form matters to investors

When you buy a listed share, you are buying a piece of a corporation such as an AG or a plc. Because the company is a separate legal person, your risk as a shareholder is limited to what you paid for your shares (see "Your rights as a shareholder" in Category A).

> [!MISTAKE] "Any business can sell shares on the stock exchange"
> Only corporations whose capital is divided into shares, and which meet the exchange's admission rules, can be listed. A sole trader or a partnership has no shares to sell, and in Austria a GmbH would first have to become an AG.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A sole trader is personally liable and cannot issue shares.
> - A corporation is a separate legal person; its owners' liability is generally limited.
> - Listed shares come from public company forms such as the Austrian AG, the UK plc or the US corporation.
> - In Austria, a GmbH needs at least €10,000 and an AG at least €70,000 of share capital, and both are created by entry in the Firmenbuch.
