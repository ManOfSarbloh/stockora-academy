An IPO is a project that takes many months and involves a whole team: the company's management, investment banks, lawyers, auditors, the regulator and the stock exchange. The Vienna Stock Exchange describes it in three phases: **preparation**, **structuring** and **placement**.

![Timeline of an IPO: preparation, choosing banks, due diligence and prospectus, approval, investor education and bookbuilding, pricing and allocation, first trading day, stabilisation and lock-up](./ipo-timeline.svg)

## 1. Preparation

The company gets ready to be a public company. In Austria this includes turning the company into an **AG** if it is not one already, introducing international accounting standards, strengthening its governance and planning, and checking whether it is attractive enough for investors.

## 2. Choosing the banks

The company selects one or more **investment banks** to manage the IPO, often after a "beauty contest" in which banks pitch for the job. These banks are called **underwriters**; the lead bank is often called the **bookrunner**. They advise on the structure, find investors and sell the shares.

## 3. Due diligence and the prospectus

The banks and lawyers examine the company's economic, legal, tax and financial situation in detail (**due diligence**). From this, they write the **prospectus**: the official offering document describing the company, its business, finances, risks, management and the terms of the offer.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> In Austria, a securities prospectus must be approved by the **FMA** before it may be published and the public offer may start. The FMA checks the prospectus for **completeness, coherence and comprehensibility**. It does **not** check whether the information is correct: the issuer is responsible for that. In the US, companies register the offering with the SEC, typically using a registration statement on **Form S-1**.

## 4. Valuation and price range

The banks estimate what the company is worth. In meetings with institutional investors (investor education), they collect feedback on valuation and possible order sizes, and from this derive a **price range** within which the shares can realistically be sold.

## 5. Roadshow and bookbuilding

The offer is announced and the **subscription period** begins. Management presents the company to investors (the **roadshow**), and investors place orders: how many shares they want and up to what price. Collecting these orders is called **bookbuilding**.

## 6. Pricing and allocation

After the subscription period, the order book is evaluated and the **final offer price** is set: the banks recommend a price, and the company decides. If there are more orders than shares (the offer is **oversubscribed**), the shares are **allocated**: investors may get only part of what they ordered.

## 7. Greenshoe and stabilisation

Banks often sell slightly more shares than originally planned (an **over-allotment**), backed by an option to buy extra shares from the company or its owners at the offer price: the **greenshoe option**. If the share price falls after listing, the banks can buy shares in the market to support the price instead of using the option.

> [!NOTE]
> Under EU rules, the greenshoe may not exceed **15% of the original offer**, stabilisation for a share IPO must end no later than **30 calendar days** after trading starts, and shares may never be stabilised **above** the offer price. All of this must be disclosed.

## 8. Lock-up and quiet period

- **Lock-up:** company insiders and large shareholders agree not to sell their shares for a period after the IPO. According to the SEC's investor education site, most lock-ups in the US last **180 days**, though terms vary. When a lock-up ends, many shares can come onto the market at once, which can push the price down.
- **Quiet period:** in the US, the time around the registration of the offering when the company must be careful about what it says publicly about the offer.

## Primary and secondary shares

An IPO can include two kinds of shares:

- **Primary shares:** newly issued shares. The money goes **to the company**.
- **Secondary shares:** existing shares sold by current owners (founders, VC funds). The money goes **to those sellers**, not to the company.

Reading the prospectus tells you how much of the money actually goes into the business.

> [!MISTAKE] "The prospectus was approved, so the company must be safe"
> Approval by the regulator means the document is complete and understandable, not that the information is true or that the investment is good. The company remains responsible for the content, and the risk is yours.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - An IPO runs through preparation, structuring and placement, usually over many months.
> - Underwriting banks manage the IPO; due diligence leads to the prospectus.
> - In the EU, the national regulator (in Austria the FMA) approves the prospectus for completeness, not correctness.
> - Bookbuilding collects orders; the final price and allocation follow.
> - Greenshoe, stabilisation and lock-ups shape the first weeks and months after listing.
> - Primary shares bring money to the company; secondary shares pay existing owners.
