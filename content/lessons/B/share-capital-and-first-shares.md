When a company is founded, its owners put money (or sometimes other assets) into it. That contribution is the **share capital**. In a stock corporation, the share capital is divided into **shares**, and the founders receive the first ones in return for their contribution.

## How the first shares come about

> [!EXAMPLE]
> Two friends found an AG with **€100,000** of share capital, each paying in €50,000.
>
> The company divides its share capital into **100,000 shares**. Each founder receives **50,000 shares**, so each owns **50%**.
>
> They could just as well have chosen 10,000 shares or 1,000,000 shares. The number of shares is a choice; what matters is each owner's fraction.

The founders usually also sign a **shareholders' agreement** (in Austria often called a Syndikatsvertrag). It sets out rules between the owners that go beyond the law, for example who may sell shares to whom, what happens if a founder leaves, and how big decisions are made.

## Authorised and issued shares

A company's founding documents can allow it to create more shares than it has handed out so far:

- **Authorised shares:** the maximum number of shares the company is allowed to issue under its founding documents.
- **Issued shares:** the shares the company has actually handed out to shareholders.

The gap gives the company room to bring in new investors later without changing its founding documents every time. (The exact legal mechanism differs between countries.)

## Par value or no par value?

In Austria, an AG can create its shares in one of two ways, but not both at the same time:

- **Par value shares (Nennbetragsaktien):** each share has a fixed nominal amount printed on it, at least €1 or a multiple of it. With €100,000 of share capital and a par value of €1, there are 100,000 shares.
- **No-par shares (Stückaktien):** there is no nominal amount on the share; each share simply represents an equal fraction of the share capital.

> [!MISTAKE] "The par value is what a share is worth"
> The par value is an accounting amount, not a price. A share with a par value of €1 can trade on the exchange at €0.20 or at €200. What investors pay depends on what they think the company is worth, not on the number printed on the share.

## Registered or bearer shares?

Shares can also differ in how ownership is recorded:

- **Registered shares (Namensaktien):** the owners are entered by name in the company's share register (Aktienbuch). Towards the company, only the person entered in the register counts as a shareholder.
- **Bearer shares (Inhaberaktien):** ownership is not recorded by name in a company register.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> Since 1 August 2011, Austrian AGs that are **not** listed on a stock exchange must issue registered shares and keep a share register. Listed AGs, and those whose shares are about to be admitted to trading, may choose between registered and bearer shares; bearer shares must be represented by a global certificate (Sammelurkunde).

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Share capital is what the owners pay into the company; in an AG it is divided into shares.
> - The founders receive the first shares in proportion to what they contribute.
> - Authorised shares are the maximum a company may issue; issued shares are those actually handed out.
> - Par value is an accounting amount, not the market price.
> - In Austria, unlisted AGs must use registered shares and keep a share register.
