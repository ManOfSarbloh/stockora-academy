Most companies do not raise all the money they will ever need at once. They raise it in **rounds**, each one bigger than the last, as the business proves itself. With each round, new investors buy a slice of the company, and the existing owners are diluted.

## The typical stages

1. **Bootstrapping:** the founders use their own savings and the company's first revenues.
2. **Friends and family:** small amounts from people who know the founders.
3. **Angel investors:** wealthy individuals who invest their own money directly in young companies, often bringing experience as former entrepreneurs.
4. **Seed round:** typically a company's first formal funding round, from friends and family, angels or early-stage funds, often used for product development and market research. Seed money is frequently raised with a **convertible note** or a **SAFE** (simple agreement for future equity): instruments that turn into shares later, which avoids having to agree on a valuation too early.
5. **Series A, B, C and so on:** later rounds, named in order, typically led by **venture capital (VC) funds**. VC funds are private funds that invest in fast-growing companies in exchange for equity, usually preferred shares, and often take a seat on the board.
6. Later, **private equity** investors, a sale of the company, or an **IPO** (going public) may follow.

> [!NOTE]
> Round names are a convention, not a legal category. A "seed round" at one company can be larger than a "Series A" at another.

## Pre-money and post-money valuation

Investors and founders agree on what the company is worth. Two numbers matter:

- **Pre-money valuation:** the company's value **before** the new money arrives.
- **Post-money valuation:** the value **after** the investment = pre-money + new money.

The investor's share is: **investment ÷ post-money valuation**.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> The founder owns **100%** of a company. An investor offers **€1 million** at a **pre-money valuation of €4 million**.
>
> - Post-money valuation: €4m + €1m = **€5 million**
> - Investor's share: €1m ÷ €5m = **20%**
> - Founder's share after the round: **80%**
>
> If the company had 1,000,000 shares before, the price per share is €4m ÷ 1,000,000 = **€4**, so the investor receives €1m ÷ €4 = **250,000 new shares**. Check: 250,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = 20%.

## Special terms in private rounds

Investors in private rounds often receive **preferred shares** with extra rights. The most important one for beginners to understand is the **liquidation preference**: the right to be paid back before ordinary shareholders when the company is sold or wound up. It is often expressed as a multiple of the investment, such as 1x or 2x.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> An investor put in €1 million with a **1x liquidation preference** and owns 20%. A few years later, the company is sold for only **€3 million**.
>
> - The investor first receives their **€1 million** back.
> - The remaining €2 million goes to the other shareholders.
>
> (Depending on the exact terms, the investor may or may not also share in what is left. The details are negotiated in each round.)

## Employee shares: options and vesting

Start-ups often pay part of their employees' rewards in **stock options**: the right to buy shares later at a fixed price. Options usually **vest** over time: the employee earns the right to them step by step, for example by staying with the company for a set period or reaching milestones. Many plans also have a **cliff**: if the employee leaves before a minimum period, they get nothing.

## Crowdfunding

With crowdfunding, many private individuals each invest small amounts, usually through an online platform. In the EU, crowdfunding platforms can be authorised under a common EU regulation; in Austria, the FMA supervises such platforms, but not the companies or projects that receive the money.

> [!WARNING]
> According to the FMA, crowdfunding investments are often **not secured** by any assets, deposit guarantee schemes do not apply, and money is frequently invested as a subordinated loan that may not have to be repaid in a crisis. Only invest money you will not need and whose risks you understand, and expect that young companies can fail completely.

> [!MISTAKE] "A high valuation means the company is a sure success"
> A valuation is a price agreed between investors and founders, based on hopes for the future. Many companies raised money at high valuations and later failed or had to raise money at a lower one.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Companies raise money in stages: own money, friends and family, angels, seed, then Series A, B, C.
> - Post-money valuation = pre-money valuation + new money; investor's share = investment ÷ post-money.
> - Private investors often get preferred shares with a liquidation preference.
> - Employee options usually vest over time.
> - Early-stage and crowdfunding investments carry a high risk of total loss.
