The IPO is not the end of the story. A listed company lives under a spotlight, changes its share count over time, and one day its shares may disappear from the exchange.

## Ongoing duties

A listed company must keep investors informed. In the EU, this includes annual and half-yearly financial reports and the prompt publication of price-relevant news (ad-hoc announcements). It also holds a general meeting every year. Category R covers these rules in detail.

## The share count changes

- **New shares (capital increase):** the company sells new shares to raise more money, for example in a **rights issue**, where existing shareholders get the first right to buy. Existing owners are diluted if they do not take part.
- **Fewer shares (buyback):** the company buys its own shares back from the market, which increases the percentage owned by each remaining share.
- **Share splits** change the number of shares without changing the company's value.

All of these are corporate actions, explained with numbers in Category V.

## How shares disappear

1. **Takeover or merger:** another company buys the company. Shareholders are usually paid in cash, in shares of the buyer, or a mix, and the old shares stop trading.
2. **Squeeze-out:** a majority shareholder forces the remaining small shareholders out against a cash payment.
3. **Delisting:** the company leaves the stock exchange. The shares still exist, but they become much harder to sell.
4. **Going private:** investors (often private equity) buy all the shares and take the company off the exchange.
5. **Bankruptcy:** the company fails. Its assets are sold, creditors are paid first, and ordinary shareholders often receive nothing. In a reorganisation, existing shareholders are frequently replaced by new owners.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> Under the Austrian Gesellschafter-Ausschlussgesetz, a shareholder who owns at least **nine tenths (90%)** of a company's capital can have the general meeting decide to transfer the remaining shares to them, against an **appropriate cash compensation** for the minority shareholders.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A large group owns **92%** of a listed Austrian AG after a takeover offer. It uses a squeeze-out to buy the remaining **8%**.
>
> You own 100 shares. You cannot refuse to sell, but you receive a cash compensation per share, which is checked by an independent expert. After the squeeze-out, the company has a single owner and its shares no longer trade.

> [!MISTAKE] "If the company delists, my shares are worthless"
> Not necessarily. Delisting does not make shares disappear; you still own part of the company. But selling becomes much harder without an exchange, and information may become scarcer. A bankruptcy is different: there, shareholders often do lose everything.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Listed companies report regularly and must publish price-relevant news promptly.
> - Capital increases add shares; buybacks remove them; both change your percentage.
> - Shares can disappear through takeovers, squeeze-outs, going private, delisting or bankruptcy.
> - In Austria, a shareholder with at least 90% can squeeze out the minority against appropriate cash compensation.
