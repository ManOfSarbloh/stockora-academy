As a company grows, it often brings in new owners: investors, employees, partners. To keep track of who owns what, companies keep a **capitalisation table**, or **cap table**: a list of everyone who holds the company's shares and related securities, with the number and type held and what they paid.

## A simple cap table

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A start-up AG is founded with **1,000,000 shares**:
>
> | Owner | Shares | Ownership |
> |---|---|---|
> | Founder Anna | 600,000 | 60% |
> | Founder Ben | 400,000 | 40% |
> | **Total** | **1,000,000** | **100%** |

## What dilution means

When a company issues **new** shares, the total number of shares grows. Existing shareholders keep the same number of shares, but their **percentage** goes down. This is called **dilution**.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> The company above issues **250,000 new shares** to an investor.
>
> | Owner | Shares | Before | After |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Anna | 600,000 | 60% | 600,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = **48%** |
> | Ben | 400,000 | 40% | 400,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = **32%** |
> | Investor | 250,000 | – | 250,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = **20%** |
> | **Total** | **1,250,000** | 100% | **100%** |
>
> Anna and Ben still own exactly as many shares as before, but each share is now a smaller slice of the company.

## Is dilution bad?

Not necessarily. Dilution means owning a smaller **percentage**. Whether you are better or worse off depends on what happens to the **value** of the company.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> Before the investment, the company is valued at €4 million. Anna's 60% is worth €2.4 million.
>
> The investor pays **€1 million** for the new shares, so the company now has €1 million more in the bank and is valued at **€5 million** after the investment. Anna's 48% of €5 million is worth **€2.4 million**: the same as before.
>
> If the money helps the company grow to €20 million, Anna's 48% is worth €9.6 million. A smaller slice of a much bigger cake.

Dilution hurts when new shares are sold **too cheaply**, that is, at a price below what the existing shares are really worth. Then value moves from the old owners to the new ones.

## The option pool

Start-ups often reserve shares for employees, so they can offer them **stock options**: the right to buy shares later at a fixed price (the strike price), usually after a vesting period. The reserved shares are called the **option pool**, and they are shown on the cap table too. When options are exercised, new shares are issued, which dilutes everyone else.

## Why cap tables matter for investors

Cap tables become long and complicated: several funding rounds, different share classes, options, convertible notes. Understanding who owns what, and on which terms, is essential before investing in a young company. For listed companies, you will see the same idea when a company issues new shares to raise money (Category V).

> [!MISTAKE] "My percentage went down, so I lost money"
> A lower percentage is not automatically a loss. Check what the company received for the new shares and what your stake is worth afterwards.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A cap table lists who holds a company's shares and related securities, and on which terms.
> - Issuing new shares reduces existing owners' percentages: dilution.
> - Dilution is not automatically a loss; it depends on the price of the new shares and what the money achieves.
> - Option pools reserve shares for employees and dilute everyone when used.
