The offer price has been set and the shares have been allocated. Now comes the moment the company has worked towards for months: the **first trading day**, when anyone can buy and sell the shares on the stock exchange.

## The first price

Until now, the price was agreed between the company, its banks and selected investors. On listing day, the market takes over. Buy and sell orders from all kinds of investors meet for the first time, often in an **opening auction** that collects orders and finds the price at which the most shares can be traded. (How auctions work is covered in Category D.)

That first market price can be very different from the offer price:

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company sells shares in its IPO at an offer price of **€20**.
>
> - **Pop:** the first trade happens at **€24**, so investors who got shares in the IPO are 20% up on paper within minutes. The company, however, only received €20 per share it sold.
> - **Break:** the first trade happens at **€18**, so IPO investors are 10% down on day one.
>
> Both happen. A first-day "pop" makes headlines; a "broken" IPO is quieter but just as real.

## Underpricing

When shares regularly jump on the first day, it suggests the offer price was set below what the market was willing to pay: this is called **underpricing**. Banks and companies may accept some underpricing to make sure the IPO is fully sold and starts well. For the company and the selling shareholders, it means leaving money on the table; for investors who received shares, it is a gift. But in popular IPOs, demand is often far higher than the number of shares, so many investors receive fewer shares than they ordered, or none.

## Free float and market capitalisation

- **Free float:** the part of a company's shares that is actually available for trading by the public, as opposed to shares held long-term by founders, families, governments or strategic owners.
- **Market capitalisation at IPO:** the offer price (or first market price) × the total number of shares, not just the ones sold in the IPO.

> [!AUSTRIA]
> For the Vienna Stock Exchange's top segment, the **prime market**, at least **25%** of the shares must be in free float, and the free float must be worth at least **€20 million**.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company has **50 million shares** and sells **15 million** of them in its IPO at **€20**.
>
> - Money raised in the IPO: 15m × €20 = **€300 million** (for the company, the sellers, or both)
> - Market capitalisation: 50m × €20 = **€1 billion**
> - Free float, if the other 35 million stay with long-term owners: 15m ÷ 50m = **30%**

## Flipping

Investors who receive IPO shares and sell them immediately are said to be **flipping**. According to the SEC, underwriters may discourage this, for example by giving fewer shares in future IPOs to customers who flip.

## After day one

The first day is just the start. As described in the previous lesson, stabilisation can last up to 30 days and lock-ups often end after several months, both of which can move the price.

> [!MISTAKE] "I'll buy at the open on IPO day; new shares always go up"
> Some IPOs jump, others fall, and the first day is often very volatile. Buying at a high opening price after a "pop" means paying the market's most optimistic price. Read the prospectus and be clear about why you would buy the shares at that price.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - On listing day, the market sets the first price, often through an opening auction.
> - The first price can be above (pop) or below (break) the offer price.
> - Underpricing benefits IPO buyers but costs the company and selling owners.
> - Free float is the part of the shares available to the public; market cap counts all shares.
> - Day-one excitement says little about long-term value.
