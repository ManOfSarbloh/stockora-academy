In old films, shareholders keep beautifully printed share certificates in a drawer. Today, shares almost always exist only **electronically**, as entries in accounts. That is safer, faster and makes trading in seconds possible. But it raises a fair question: where exactly is "my" share?

## Step 1: the share gets an identity

Before a share can be traded, it needs an **ISIN**, the 12-character international identification number (see Category A). In Austria, ISINs are assigned by the **Oesterreichische Kontrollbank (OeKB)**. The stock exchange also gives the share a **ticker**.

## Step 2: the share is deposited at a central securities depository

A **central securities depository (CSD)** is the institution that records securities in a **book-entry system** (electronic accounts) and keeps them safe. In Austria, this is **OeKB CSD**, a subsidiary of the OeKB. Its core services include the initial recording of securities in its book-entry system and their safekeeping.

Instead of printing thousands of individual certificates, a company's whole issue is often represented by one **global certificate** (Sammelurkunde) held at the CSD, or issued directly in electronic form. Ownership then changes by changing account entries, not by handing over paper.

Other well-known CSDs include Clearstream (Germany and Luxembourg), Euroclear (Belgium and other countries) and the Depository Trust Company (DTC) in the United States.

## Step 3: the chain of accounts

When you buy shares through a broker, you usually sit at the end of a chain:

**You** → your **broker** (your securities account, "Depot") → the broker's **custodian bank** (if the broker uses one) → the **CSD** → the **issuing company**.

Each link keeps records of what it holds for the next one. Your broker's records show your shares; the CSD's records show the total held by your broker's custodian for all its clients.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You buy 50 shares of an Austrian company. After the trade is settled:
>
> - your broker's account statement shows **50 shares** in your Depot;
> - at the CSD, the account of your broker's custodian increases by 50 shares (together with all its other clients' shares);
> - nothing is printed and nothing is posted.

## Where does the share register fit in?

For **registered shares**, the company also keeps a **share register** with the names of its shareholders. In Austria, only the person entered in the register counts as a shareholder towards the company. For listed companies with bearer shares, there is no register by name, and the chain of accounts is what proves ownership.

> [!NOTE]
> What happens to your shares if a broker or bank fails, and how investor protection works, is covered in Category F.

> [!MISTAKE] "My shares are kept at the stock exchange"
> The exchange is where shares are traded, not where they are held. Your shares are recorded in your broker's or bank's books and, further up the chain, at a central securities depository.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Modern shares exist as electronic book entries, not paper.
> - Each share gets an ISIN (in Austria from the OeKB) and is held at a CSD (in Austria: OeKB CSD).
> - Ownership runs through a chain: you, broker, custodian, CSD, company.
> - Registered shares are also recorded by name in the company's share register.
