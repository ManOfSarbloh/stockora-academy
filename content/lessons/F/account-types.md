## Cash account

In a **cash account**, you can only buy what you can pay for with your own money. If you have €1,000, you can buy shares worth up to €1,000 (minus fees). This is the standard account for most private investors, and the safest: you cannot lose more than you put in.

## Margin account

In a **margin account**, the broker **lends** you money to buy securities, using your account as collateral. That increases your buying power, but also your potential losses.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You have **€5,000** and borrow another **€5,000** on margin to buy shares worth **€10,000**.
>
> - If the shares rise 20% to €12,000, you repay €5,000 (plus interest) and keep about **€7,000**: a gain of about 40% on your own money.
> - If the shares fall 20% to €8,000, you still owe €5,000 and are left with about **€3,000**: a loss of about **40%**.
>
> Borrowing doubled both the gain and the loss, and you pay interest on the loan either way.

If prices fall far enough, the broker issues a **margin call**: you must add money or securities. The SEC warns that a broker can also **sell your securities without notifying you**, possibly at a substantial loss. Leverage is explained in depth in Category N.

> [!WARNING]
> Margin trading can lose you more than you expect, very fast. Beginners should not use margin accounts.

## Tax-advantaged accounts in other countries

Many countries encourage long-term saving with special accounts in which investment returns are tax-free or tax-deferred, within annual limits. A few examples:

| Country | Account | Key feature (as of 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | **ISA** (Individual Savings Account) | Up to **£20,000** per tax year across all ISAs; returns inside are tax-free |
| United States | **IRA** and **401(k)** | Retirement accounts with tax advantages; 2026 contribution limits of **$7,500** (IRA) and **$24,500** (401(k)) |
| Japan | **NISA** (Nippon Individual Savings Account) | Tax-exempt investment account; about 24 million accounts by mid-2024, according to Japan's Financial Services Agency |
| Canada | **TFSA** (Tax-Free Savings Account) | Income and gains are generally tax-free, even when withdrawn; contributions are not tax-deductible |

> [!AUSTRIA]
> In an ordinary Austrian securities account, gains and dividends are subject to capital gains tax (KESt), explained in Category Q.

> [!VERIFY]
> Limits and rules for tax-advantaged accounts change frequently. Always check the official government page of the country concerned.

> [!MISTAKE] "A margin account just gives me more money to invest"
> It gives you borrowed money, which must be paid back with interest whatever happens to your investments. Losses are magnified exactly like gains.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - A cash account lets you invest only your own money; you cannot lose more than you put in.
> - A margin account lends you money; it magnifies gains and losses, and margin calls can force sales.
> - Many countries offer tax-advantaged accounts (ISA, IRA/401(k), NISA, TFSA) with annual limits.
> - In an ordinary Austrian Depot, KESt applies to gains and dividends (Category Q).
