## Payment for order flow (PFOF)

With **payment for order flow**, a broker receives money from a trading firm (often a market maker) for sending its customers' orders to that firm. This allowed some brokers to offer trades with no commission.

**The controversy:** critics argue that it creates a **conflict of interest**: the broker may send orders where it gets paid most, not where customers get the best prices. Supporters argue that customers benefit from zero commissions and that execution quality can still be good.

> [!NOTE]
> In the EU, the revised MiFIR (Article 39a) **prohibits** investment firms from receiving payment for order flow from third parties for executing or forwarding retail and professional clients' orders to a particular venue. Member States could grant a temporary exemption for firms already established in their country, but only **until 30 June 2026**. Since then, the ban applies everywhere in the EU.

Whatever the payment model, investment firms in the EU must take sufficient steps to obtain the best possible result for their clients when executing orders ("best execution", MiFID II Article 27). For retail clients, the best result is measured by the total cost: the price plus all costs related to execution. Firms must have an order execution policy and inform clients about it. Category D explained where orders can be executed.

## If your bank or broker fails

> [!AUSTRIA]
> Austria has two separate protection schemes for clients of banks and investment firms:
>
> | | What is protected | Limit |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Deposit guarantee** | Cash: current, savings and securities clearing accounts | Up to **€100,000** per person per bank (in special cases more) |
> | **Investor compensation** | Claims if a bank or investment firm cannot return your **securities** or money owed from securities business | Up to **€20,000** per investor |
>
> The two claims are independent of each other. Investor compensation must be **claimed actively**, within a deadline (for investment firms, one year from the bankruptcy, according to the FMA).

Remember: **securities in your Depot belong to you.** In a bank insolvency, they should normally be returned to you. Investor compensation is meant for cases where they cannot be returned, for example because of fraud or errors.

> [!WARNING]
> Investor compensation does **not** protect you against **falling prices**, bad investment decisions or poor advice. According to the FMA, fictitious gains and front-end loads are not compensated either. It only covers the failure of the firm to return what belongs to you, up to the limit.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> You have €8,000 cash in your clearing account and shares worth €50,000 in your Depot at an Austrian bank that goes bankrupt.
>
> - The **€8,000 cash** is covered by the deposit guarantee.
> - The **shares** are yours and should be returned or transferred to another bank.
> - Only if shares cannot be returned does investor compensation step in, up to €20,000.

> [!MISTAKE] "My shares are insured against losses up to €20,000"
> No. Investor compensation does not cover market losses. If your shares fall from €20,000 to €5,000, nobody compensates you.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - Payment for order flow pays brokers for routing orders; it creates conflicts of interest and is now banned in the EU.
> - Brokers must aim for best execution of your orders.
> - In Austria, cash is protected up to €100,000 per bank; investor compensation covers up to €20,000 if securities cannot be returned.
> - Neither scheme protects you against falling prices.
