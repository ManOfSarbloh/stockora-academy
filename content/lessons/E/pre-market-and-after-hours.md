In the United States, trading does not only happen between 9:30 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. Many venues also offer **extended-hours trading**: a **pre-market** session in the early morning and an **after-hours** session in the evening. The exact times differ by venue and broker. The NYSE, for example, runs a late session until 8:00 p.m. Eastern Time on its equity markets.

## Why extended hours exist

Companies often publish important news, such as quarterly results, **outside regular hours**, before the open or after the close. Extended sessions let investors react before the next regular session.

## The risks, according to the SEC

The SEC's investor education site lists several risks of extended-hours trading:

- **Less liquidity:** fewer people trade, so it can be harder to get your order filled, and some stocks may not trade at all.
- **Wider spreads:** with fewer participants, the gap between bid and ask is usually wider, or there are no quotes at all.
- **Bigger price swings:** stocks with little trading activity can move more sharply than during regular hours.
- **Prices that don't hold:** a price in extended hours may not match the price at the next regular open.
- **Unlinked venues:** different extended-hours systems may show different prices for the same stock at the same time.
- **News shocks:** company announcements combined with thin trading can lead to large moves.
- **Professionals:** many extended-hours traders are professionals with more information than individual investors.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> A company reports results at 4:05 p.m. New York time, after the close. In the after-hours session, the price jumps from **$50** to **$56**, but only a few thousand shares trade, with a spread of $1.
>
> The next morning, as the full market opens, the price settles at **$53**. Someone who bought at $56 the evening before is already down about 5%.

## Order rules

Many brokers accept only **limit orders** in extended hours, to protect clients from very bad prices. A limit order executes only at your price or better; if the market moves away, it is not filled. Ask your broker whether unfilled extended-hours orders are cancelled at the end of the session or carried into the regular session.

> [!NOTE]
> European exchanges also have phases before the open and after the close, such as "trade at close" in Vienna, where orders can be executed at the official closing price for a few minutes after the closing auction.

> [!MISTAKE] "The after-hours price is the real price"
> After-hours prices are based on few trades and can reverse quickly at the next open. Treat them as an early, unreliable signal, not as the market's verdict.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - US markets offer pre-market and after-hours sessions around the 9:30–16:00 core session.
> - Extended hours mean less liquidity, wider spreads, bigger swings and prices that may not hold.
> - Many brokers allow only limit orders outside regular hours.
> - Company news is often released outside regular hours, which is why big moves happen there.
