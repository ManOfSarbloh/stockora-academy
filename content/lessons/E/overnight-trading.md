Stock markets have long had fixed hours, but in an always-on, global economy there is pressure to trade longer. In the United States, exchanges are preparing for trading **23 hours a day, five days a week** ("23/5").

## What is planned in the US

According to Nasdaq, on **6 December 2026** the Nasdaq Stock Market is scheduled to add a **night session from 9:00 p.m. to 4:00 a.m. Eastern Time**. Together with the existing early session (4:00–9:30 a.m.) and late session (4:00–8:00 p.m.), this would create a near-continuous trading day, with a short pause each evening. Other venues are preparing similar sessions, and the plans depend on market data and other systems being ready.

> [!VERIFY]
> This lesson was written in October 2026, before the planned launch. Check the exchanges' official pages for the current status and exact times.

## Why longer hours?

- **Global investors:** people in Asia and Europe want to trade US shares during their own daytime.
- **News:** major news does not wait for New York business hours.

## The catch: thin markets at night

The SEC's warnings about extended-hours trading apply even more to the middle of the night:

- **few participants**, so less liquidity and wider spreads;
- **bigger price swings** on small trades;
- prices that may not hold when the main session opens.

> [!EXAMPLE]
> At 3:00 a.m. New York time (9:00 Vienna time), a share might have a spread of 1% instead of, say, 0.05% during the day. For 100 shares at $50 ($5,000), buying and selling straight back would then cost about **$50** in spread, compared with about **$2.50** during regular hours.

> [!MISTAKE] "If I can trade 23 hours a day, I should"
> Being able to trade at any time does not mean it is a good idea. For long-term investors, there is rarely a reason to trade in a thin, volatile night session.

## Key takeaways

> [!TAKEAWAYS]
> - US exchanges plan near-continuous "23/5" trading; Nasdaq's night session is scheduled to start on 6 December 2026.
> - Night sessions are convenient for global investors but tend to be thin, with wider spreads and bigger swings.
> - Extended-hours risks from the SEC apply even more at night.
